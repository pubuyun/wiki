## 1. Overall Structure of the Respiratory System

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line, and you will encounter a broad, upward-curving structure. This is the diaphragm, located at the bottom of the thoracic cavity. Slowly trace this curved structure with your fingers, feeling how it extends across the bottom of the chest. Continue exploring upward from the diaphragm, and you will encounter two large, leaf-shaped structures on either side. These represent the left and right lungs. Between the lungs, you will find a tube extending upward. This is the trachea. Following the trachea upward will lead you to the larynx, pharynx, and the head and neck region containing the oral and nasal cavities. Please note that the head in the graphic faces toward the right, while the chest is represented as though viewed from the front of the human body. Therefore, the lung on the left side of the graphic corresponds to the person’s right lung, while the lung on the right side of the graphic corresponds to the person’s left lung. Next, we will begin with the head and gradually explore the respiratory system by following the pathway through which air enters the body.

## 2. Nasal Cavity

Now, move your finger to the head region near the upper part of the graphic. Slowly trace the outline along the right side of the head until you locate the outward-projecting nose. Once you find the nostril, slowly move your finger toward the left, into the interior of the head. You will encounter a horizontally extending cavity containing several curved raised structures. This is the nasal cavity. Slowly trace the outline of the nasal cavity, feeling its shape and the curved structures inside it. These raised structures primarily represent the nasal conchae and other internal features of the nasal cavity. The nasal cavity is the main entrance through which air enters the respiratory system. When we inhale, air usually enters through the nostrils and passes into the nasal cavity. Nasal hairs and mucus help trap some dust particles and microorganisms, reducing the amount of foreign material entering the lower respiratory tract. The nasal mucosa contains numerous blood vessels that help warm inhaled air, while its moist surface adds water vapor to the air. Together, these processes help filter, warm, and humidify the air we breathe, reducing irritation caused by cold air, dry air, and foreign particles.

## 3. Frontal Sinus and Sphenoidal Sinus

Now, return your finger to the nasal cavity and slowly move straight upward. Above the front part of the nasal cavity, near the forehead, you will encounter a small cavity. This structure represents the frontal sinus. Gently explore its outline with your fingertip, feeling its position relative to the nasal cavity. Next, return your finger to the nasal cavity and move toward the upper-left direction, deeper into the head. You will encounter another small cavity. This structure represents the sphenoidal sinus. Compared with the frontal sinus, the sphenoidal sinus is located deeper within the skull. Both the frontal sinus and the sphenoidal sinus belong to a group of structures called the paranasal sinuses. The paranasal sinuses are air-filled cavities within the skull that communicate with the nasal cavity. They help reduce the weight of the skull and may contribute to voice resonance. Their inner surfaces are lined with mucous membranes that produce mucus. When these membranes become inflamed and swollen due to infection or other causes, symptoms of sinusitis may occur, including nasal congestion, facial pain, or a sensation of pressure. Please note that although the paranasal sinuses communicate with the nasal cavity, they are not part of the main pathway through which air travels into the lungs. Instead of passing through the frontal or sphenoidal sinuses, inhaled air primarily travels backward through the nasal cavity toward the pharynx.

## 4. Oral Cavity and Tongue

Now, return your finger to the nose and slowly move straight downward. Below the nasal cavity, you will encounter a larger hollow region. This is the oral cavity, also known as the mouth. Slowly explore the structures within this region of the graphic. You will encounter a relatively broad, elongated structure extending toward the right. This represents the tongue. Slowly trace its surface with your finger, feeling its position and shape within the oral cavity. The tongue is an organ composed primarily of muscles and is involved in chewing, swallowing, taste perception, and speech. It helps move and mix food inside the mouth and pushes food toward the pharynx during swallowing. The oral cavity can also serve as a passageway for air entering the body. For example, during intense physical activity or when the nasal cavity is obstructed, we may breathe more frequently through the mouth. However, compared with the nasal cavity, the oral cavity is less effective at filtering, warming, and humidifying inhaled air.

## 5. Pharynx

Now, move your finger to the back of the nasal cavity and slowly move toward the lower-left direction. Alternatively, you can start from the back of the oral cavity and move toward the left. Behind the nasal and oral cavities, you will encounter a shared passageway extending vertically. This structure is the pharynx. Slowly trace this passageway with your finger, feeling how it connects the nasal and oral cavities above with the larynx below. The pharynx belongs to both the respiratory and digestive systems because it serves as a passageway for both air and food. Anatomically, the pharynx can be divided into three regions: the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx. After entering the pharynx through the nasal or oral cavity, air continues downward toward the larynx and then enters the trachea. During swallowing, food also passes through the pharynx before entering the esophagus. Therefore, the pharynx is an important region where the respiratory and digestive pathways intersect. The body relies on a series of coordinated movements to ensure that breathing and swallowing occur properly while minimizing the risk of food entering the airway.

## 6. Epiglottis

Now, return your finger to the back of the tongue and slowly move toward the lower-left direction. Behind the base of the tongue and above the larynx, you will encounter a small, flap-shaped raised structure. This represents the epiglottis. Gently trace its outline with your fingertip, feeling its position near the entrance to the larynx. The epiglottis is a flap of elastic cartilage covered by a mucous membrane. It plays an important protective role during swallowing. During normal breathing, the epiglottis generally remains in a relatively upright position, allowing air to pass through the laryngeal entrance and into the trachea. When we swallow food or liquids, the epiglottis bends backward and downward, helping cover the entrance to the larynx and reducing the likelihood of food or liquid entering the airway. Please note that the epiglottis does not act alone like a lid to close the airway. During swallowing, the larynx also moves upward and forward, while the vocal folds close to protect the airway. These coordinated movements help direct food into the esophagus rather than the trachea.

## 7. Larynx

Now, return your finger to the epiglottis and slowly move downward along the respiratory passageway. You will encounter a slightly enlarged structure at the upper end of the trachea. This is the larynx. Slowly trace its outer outline with your finger, feeling how its shape differs from the longer tube below. The larynx connects the pharynx to the trachea and serves as an important passageway through which air enters the lower respiratory tract. It consists of several cartilages, muscles, ligaments, and other structures that help keep the airway open and contribute to voice production and airway protection. Inside the larynx are the vocal folds. When we speak or sing, exhaled air passes through the vocal folds, causing them to vibrate and produce sound. Muscles within the larynx can also adjust the position and tension of the vocal folds, influencing the pitch and quality of the sounds we produce.

## 8. Esophagus

Now, keep one finger just below the larynx and move slightly toward the left side of the graphic. You will encounter a relatively narrow tube extending downward behind the trachea. This structure is the esophagus. Slowly trace the esophagus downward, feeling how it lies close to the trachea while remaining a separate passageway. The esophagus belongs to the digestive system rather than the respiratory system. It is a muscular tube connecting the pharynx to the stomach. Through coordinated contractions and relaxations of its muscles, the esophagus transports swallowed food and liquids toward the stomach. This process is called peristalsis. The graphic places the esophagus and trachea close together to help us understand how air and food follow different pathways after passing through the pharynx. Air passes through the larynx into the trachea, while food enters the esophagus. Therefore, although the respiratory and digestive systems share a passageway in the pharynx, they continue along separate routes below it.

## 9. Trachea

Now, return your finger to the region just below the larynx and slowly move straight downward along the central part of the graphic. You will encounter a relatively thick tube extending vertically. Slowly slide your finger along its surface, and you will feel several raised, ring-shaped ridges arranged horizontally at regular intervals. This tube is the trachea, and the raised ridges represent the cartilaginous rings that support it. The trachea connects the larynx to the left and right main bronchi, transporting air into the chest. In the human body, the tracheal cartilages are not completely closed rings. Instead, they are C-shaped structures made of hyaline cartilage, with an opening at the back. These cartilages support the trachea and help prevent it from collapsing during breathing while allowing a degree of flexibility. The inner surface of the trachea is also lined with a mucous membrane containing mucus-producing cells and ciliated cells. Mucus traps some dust particles, microorganisms, and other foreign materials entering the airway. Meanwhile, the cilia continuously beat in a coordinated direction, moving the mucus and trapped particles upward toward the pharynx, where they can be swallowed or expelled. This process is an important defense mechanism of the respiratory system.

## 10. Left and Right Main Bronchi

Now, continue moving your finger straight downward along the trachea. When you reach its lower end, you will notice that the single tube divides into two branches, forming a shape similar to an upside-down letter Y. These two branches represent the left and right main bronchi. Place one finger on each branch and slowly trace them outward, feeling how air can travel through two separate passageways into the lungs. The branch extending toward the left side of the graphic enters the person’s right lung and is therefore the right main bronchus. The branch extending toward the right side of the graphic enters the person’s left lung and is the left main bronchus. These two main bronchi transport air from the trachea into the corresponding lungs. In humans, the right main bronchus is usually shorter, wider, and more vertically oriented than the left main bronchus. As a result, when food particles or other foreign objects accidentally enter the trachea, they are more likely to pass into the right main bronchus and reach the right lung. This is why aspirated foreign objects more commonly enter the right bronchial tree.

## 11. Right Lung

Now, return your finger to the point where the trachea divides into two branches. Slowly follow the right main bronchus toward the left side of the graphic. As you move outward, you will enter a large, leaf-shaped region. This structure represents the person’s right lung. Slowly trace its outer outline, feeling its relatively narrow upper portion, broader lower portion, and smoothly curved edges. The right lung is located on the right side of the thoracic cavity and is one of the main organs responsible for gas exchange. Because most of the heart is positioned toward the left side of the chest, the right lung is generally wider and often has a greater volume than the left lung. In the human body, the right lung is divided into three lobes: the superior lobe, middle lobe, and inferior lobe. These lobes are separated by fissures. Please note that the graphic primarily illustrates the overall shape of the right lung and the distribution of its internal bronchi rather than showing every boundary between the lung lobes. Inside the right lung, numerous branching airways eventually lead to the alveoli. Once air reaches the alveoli, oxygen can diffuse into the surrounding capillaries, while carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli and is subsequently expelled during exhalation. Therefore, the right lung is not only an important region into which inhaled air travels but also a major site of gas exchange between the body and the external environment.

## 12. Left Lung

Now, return your finger to the point where the trachea divides into two branches. Slowly follow the left main bronchus toward the right side of the graphic. You will encounter another large, leaf-shaped region. This represents the person’s left lung. Slowly trace its outer edge, feeling how its overall shape resembles that of the right lung. Next, move your finger toward the inner edge of the left lung, closer to the center of the chest. You will notice a slight indentation along its outline. This indentation is associated with the position of the heart within the thoracic cavity. Because most of the heart is located toward the left side of the body, the left lung must leave some space for it and is therefore generally slightly smaller than the right lung. In the human body, the left lung is divided into two lobes, the superior lobe and inferior lobe, and has a cardiac notch that accommodates the position of the heart. Although the lungs are essential organs of the respiratory system, they do not actively contract like muscles to draw air into the body. Instead, air moves into and out of the lungs mainly because the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles move the chest wall, producing changes in thoracic volume and pressure inside the lungs. Next, we will explore the branching airways within the lungs.

## 13. Bronchi Within the Lungs

Now, place your finger on either main bronchus and slowly follow it into the lung. You will notice that the relatively thick tube gradually divides into several smaller branches, which then divide into even finer tubes. Slowly trace one of these branches with your finger, feeling how it becomes narrower and repeatedly divides into smaller pathways. These tree-like structures represent the bronchi and bronchioles within the lungs. After entering the lungs, the main bronchi divide into lobar bronchi, segmental bronchi, and progressively smaller bronchi and bronchioles, transporting air to different regions of the lungs. Because these branching tubes resemble the structure of a tree, the trachea and its successive bronchial branches are collectively known as the bronchial tree. In the human body, the bronchioles continue branching and eventually connect to numerous alveoli through structures including respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts. Alveoli are extremely small, sac-like structures and are the main sites of gas exchange in the respiratory system. Please note that the graphic primarily illustrates the branching structure of the bronchial tree and does not individually represent the alveoli. The human lungs contain hundreds of millions of alveoli, which together provide an enormous surface area for gas exchange. When we inhale, air travels through the bronchi and bronchioles before eventually reaching the alveoli. The walls of the alveoli are extremely thin and are surrounded by dense networks of capillaries. Because the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveoli is normally higher than that in the deoxygenated blood entering the lungs, oxygen diffuses across the alveolar and capillary walls into the bloodstream. It is then transported throughout the body, primarily by hemoglobin in red blood cells. At the same time, carbon dioxide in the blood diffuses into the alveoli down its partial pressure gradient and is expelled through the airways during exhalation. Through this process, the respiratory system continuously supplies the body with oxygen while helping remove carbon dioxide produced by cellular metabolism.

## 14. Diaphragm

Finally, move both hands below the left and right lungs and locate the broad, upward-curving structure. This is the diaphragm. Slowly trace it from the left side of the graphic toward the right, feeling how it extends across the bottom of the chest. The diaphragm is a dome-shaped skeletal muscle that separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity. It is also the most important muscle involved in breathing. When we inhale, the diaphragm contracts and moves downward, causing its normally dome-shaped surface to become flatter. This increases the volume of the thoracic cavity, allowing the lungs to expand and the pressure inside them to decrease. When the pressure inside the lungs becomes lower than the surrounding atmospheric pressure, air flows into the body through the nasal or oral cavity, passes through the pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi, and eventually reaches the lungs. When we exhale, the diaphragm gradually relaxes and returns to its upward-curving position. As the volume of the thoracic cavity decreases, the pressure inside the lungs rises, causing air to flow outward through the respiratory tract. During quiet exhalation, the natural elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall also plays an important role, so active contraction of additional expiratory muscles is usually unnecessary. Now, try taking a deep breath and then slowly breathing out. As you inhale, your diaphragm contracts and moves downward. As you exhale, it gradually relaxes and returns to its upward-curving shape. Through this simple exercise, you can experience how the respiratory system relies on the movements of the respiratory muscles to draw air into the lungs and expel it from the body.
