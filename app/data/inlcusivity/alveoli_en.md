Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. Next, we will begin with the respiratory bronchiole and follow the pathway through which air reaches the alveoli, gradually exploring the alveoli and their surrounding blood vessels to understand how gas exchange takes place in the human body.

## 1. Overall Structure of the Alveoli

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line, and you will feel a long, narrow tube near the lower-left part of the graphic. Slowly follow this tube toward the upper right, feeling how it extends and connects to several rounded, raised structures. Near the upper-left and upper-right parts of the graphic, you will encounter two groups of closely packed, rounded structures resembling clusters of grapes. Together, these structures represent some of the smallest airways and alveoli found at the ends of the respiratory passages. The alveoli are the main sites of gas exchange in the human body. Inhaled air travels through the trachea, bronchi, and bronchioles before eventually reaching the alveoli. Here, oxygen enters the bloodstream, while carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli and is expelled during exhalation. Please note that this graphic represents only a small portion of the structures found at the ends of the airways, rather than an entire lung.

## 2. Respiratory Bronchiole

Now, return your finger to the lower-left part of the graphic and locate the long, narrow tube you explored earlier. Starting from its wider end, slowly trace it toward the upper right, feeling how it connects to smaller branches and clusters of alveoli. This tube represents the respiratory bronchiole. After passing through the trachea, bronchi, and progressively smaller bronchioles, inhaled air enters the respiratory bronchioles before continuing into the alveolar ducts and alveoli. Unlike the larger airways, respiratory bronchioles already have alveoli opening from their walls, allowing some gas exchange to occur here. They therefore serve both to transport air and to mark the transition from the conducting airways to the gas-exchanging region of the lungs. The tube in the graphic has been enlarged for tactile exploration. In the human body, respiratory bronchioles are much smaller.

## 3. Alveolar Duct

Now, continue moving your finger upward along the respiratory bronchiole until you locate the narrow passageway connecting to the rounded structures in the upper-left part of the graphic. Slowly trace this passageway, feeling how it gradually leads into clusters of alveoli. This passageway represents the alveolar duct. Alveolar ducts connect respiratory bronchioles to alveolar sacs. After passing through the respiratory bronchioles, air continues along the alveolar ducts and enters the connected alveoli. Unlike ordinary bronchioles, the walls of alveolar ducts consist almost entirely of alveolar openings. As a result, alveolar ducts not only transport air but also participate in gas exchange. Through these passages, air can be distributed to multiple surrounding alveoli.

## 4. Alveolar Sac

Now, continue moving upward along the alveolar duct. You will encounter a group of closely packed, rounded bumps in the upper-left part of the graphic. Gently place both hands over these bumps and slowly trace their outer contours, feeling how several similarly sized, smooth structures cluster together to form a shape resembling a bunch of grapes. This group represents an alveolar sac. An alveolar sac consists of several alveoli that open into a shared air space. Air arriving through the alveolar duct can enter different alveoli through this common space. By arranging large numbers of alveoli close together, the lungs provide an enormous surface area for gas exchange within a limited volume, improving the efficiency of oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange. The alveolar sac in the graphic has been enlarged to make these microscopic structures easier to explore through touch.

## 5. Individual Alveolus

Now, place your finger on the alveolar sac you just explored and select one of its more prominent rounded bumps. Slowly trace around its edge, feeling its smooth, rounded outline. This individual structure represents an alveolus. An alveolus is a tiny, sac-like structure at the end of the respiratory passages. It contains an air space and serves as a major site of oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange. In the human body, the alveolar wall is extremely thin and is formed mainly by a single layer of alveolar epithelial cells. Numerous capillaries lie closely against its outer surface. Oxygen can diffuse across the alveolar and capillary walls into the blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction into the alveolus. The inner surface of each alveolus is also covered by a thin layer of fluid containing pulmonary surfactant. This substance reduces surface tension, helping maintain alveolar stability and reducing the likelihood of collapse. Please note that the rounded bump in the graphic is intended to represent the external shape of an alveolus. Real alveoli are not solid spheres; they are tiny air-filled sacs with openings connecting them to the airways.

## 6. Right Alveolar Cluster

Now, slowly move your finger from the alveolar sac in the upper-left part of the graphic toward the right. After crossing the gap between the two groups, you will encounter another collection of relatively large, rounded bumps. Slowly explore their surfaces, feeling how they are closely arranged to form another grape-like cluster. This represents the alveolar cluster on the right side of the graphic. Next, move your finger toward the lower right. Unlike the complete, rounded bumps you explored earlier, you will encounter structures with openings and curved edges. These features illustrate the internal air spaces of the alveoli and how they are connected. Although alveoli are extremely numerous, they are not completely isolated structures. Multiple alveoli can communicate through shared air passages, allowing air to enter different alveoli. By showing both the rounded external surfaces and some of the internal structures, the graphic helps us understand the shape and arrangement of alveoli from different perspectives.

## 7. Alveolar Atrium

Now, move your finger to the lower-right part of the graphic and locate the region with curved edges and openings that you explored earlier. Slowly trace these curved structures, feeling the internal space enclosed by them. Between the openings of several alveoli, you will find a shared space connecting them. This region represents the alveolar atrium. The alveolar atrium is a common air space within an alveolar sac, located at the end of an alveolar duct and connected to the openings of multiple alveoli. After air travels through the alveolar duct into the alveolar sac, it can pass through this shared space to reach the surrounding alveoli. The alveolar atrium is not an individual alveolus but a region connecting several alveoli. This structure helps illustrate how air can travel from a single narrow passageway into multiple alveoli. The graphic enlarges this shared space and its surrounding openings so that they can be distinguished through touch.

## 8. Capillaries

Now, slowly move your finger from the alveolar openings in the lower-right part of the graphic toward the middle of the right alveolar cluster. Here, you will encounter several thin, interconnected, raised lines that are much smaller than the rounded alveolar bumps. Slowly follow one of these narrow structures with your fingertip, feeling how it connects with others to form a network. These structures represent the capillaries surrounding the alveoli. Capillaries are among the smallest blood vessels in the human body, and their walls consist mainly of a single layer of endothelial cells. The dense capillary network surrounding the alveoli brings blood into close contact with the air inside them. When blood with a relatively low oxygen content flows through these capillaries, oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood down its partial pressure gradient and binds to hemoglobin in red blood cells. At the same time, carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli and is subsequently expelled during exhalation. The capillaries in the graphic have been enlarged and simplified. In the human body, they are much smaller and form a denser network closely surrounding the alveolar surfaces.

## 9. Pulmonary Artery and Pulmonary Vein

Now, slowly move your finger from the capillary network on the right toward the lower-left part of the graphic, following the direction in which the blood vessels connect. Near the respiratory bronchiole, you will find several narrow, tube-like structures extending toward the alveolar region alongside the airway. These structures represent blood vessels connected to the alveolar capillary network. The pulmonary arteries carry blood with a relatively low oxygen content from the heart to the lungs. As this blood flows through the capillaries surrounding the alveoli, it gains oxygen and releases carbon dioxide through gas exchange. The oxygen-rich blood then enters the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart, where it can be pumped to the rest of the body. Therefore, the main distinction between the pulmonary arteries and pulmonary veins is the direction of blood flow: pulmonary arteries carry blood away from the heart toward the lungs, while pulmonary veins carry blood from the lungs back to the heart.

## 10. Alveolar Wall and Gas Exchange

Finally, place one hand on a rounded alveolar bump on the right side of the graphic and the other hand on the capillary network lying close to its surface. Feel the smooth, rounded outline of the alveolus and the thin, interconnected structures representing the capillaries. The close relationship between these structures is essential for efficient gas exchange. In the human body, both the alveolar wall and the capillary wall are extremely thin. Together, they form the air-blood barrier, allowing oxygen and carbon dioxide to diffuse across a very short distance. During inhalation, the partial pressure of oxygen inside the alveoli is normally higher than that in the deoxygenated blood entering the lungs. Oxygen therefore diffuses across the air-blood barrier into the bloodstream. Meanwhile, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide is higher in the incoming blood than in the alveolar air, so carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction into the alveoli and is expelled during exhalation. In addition, the large number of alveoli provides an enormous surface area for gas exchange. The extensive capillary network and continuous blood flow help maintain the partial pressure gradients needed for diffusion, while breathing constantly renews the air inside the alveoli. Together, these features allow the alveoli to exchange gases efficiently and continuously. The graphic enlarges the alveolar walls, capillaries, and other microscopic structures for tactile exploration. The actual air-blood barrier is far thinner than represented here.
