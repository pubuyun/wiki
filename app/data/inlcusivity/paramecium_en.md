## 1. Overall Structure of the Paramecium

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line until you reach the lower-left edge of the Paramecium. Slowly trace around its outer outline with your fingers, feeling its elongated, slightly curved shape. Both ends are rounded, while the middle is wider, giving it an overall appearance similar to the sole of a shoe. The region enclosed by this outline represents an entire Paramecium. Paramecium is a unicellular eukaryotic organism commonly found in freshwater environments such as ponds and ditches. Although it consists of only one cell, it can independently carry out essential life processes, including movement, feeding, digestion, excretion, and reproduction. In the current orientation of the graphic, the lower-left end of the Paramecium is its anterior end, while the upper-right end is its posterior end.

## 2. Cilia

Now, keep your finger near the lower-left outer edge of the Paramecium and move slightly outward. You will encounter numerous short, closely arranged, rod-like projections extending away from the cell surface. These structures are called cilia. Slowly move your finger around the outer edge of the Paramecium, and you will notice that cilia cover almost its entire surface. Paramecium moves by beating large numbers of cilia rhythmically, pushing against the surrounding water to move forward, rotate, or change direction. In addition, cilia near the oral groove generate water currents that carry bacteria and other tiny food particles toward the oral groove, helping the Paramecium feed. The cilia in this graphic have been enlarged and simplified. In reality, Paramecium has far more cilia, and each one is much smaller than those represented in the graphic.

## 3. Pellicle

Now, move your finger slightly inward from the cilia until you reach the continuous boundary running along the body, just inside the bases of the cilia. Slowly trace this boundary, feeling how it surrounds the entire cell. This boundary represents the pellicle. The pellicle is a specialized surface covering of the Paramecium, consisting of the cell membrane and associated surface structures. It protects the internal components of the cell and helps the Paramecium maintain a relatively stable body shape while retaining some flexibility. Gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide can also diffuse across the cell surface. Together, the pellicle and cilia form important structures on the surface of the Paramecium, allowing it to maintain its shape while moving efficiently through water.

## 4. Anterior Collecting Canals and Contractile Vacuole

Now, return your finger to the lower-left anterior end of the Paramecium and slowly move inward. You will encounter several long, narrow structures extending from different directions toward a central point. These structures represent the collecting canals. Slowly trace one of the collecting canals toward the center until you reach a small, smooth, rounded bump. This is the contractile vacuole. The collecting canals gather excess water from the cell and transport it into the contractile vacuole. As the contractile vacuole gradually fills with water, it eventually contracts and expels the water from the cell. Because Paramecium lives in freshwater, water continuously enters its cell through osmosis. If this excess water were not removed, the cell could swell and potentially burst. Therefore, the collecting canals and contractile vacuole work together as an important osmoregulatory system, helping maintain the cell’s internal water balance. Another similar group of structures is located near the upper-right posterior end of the Paramecium, which we will explore later.

## 5. Food Vacuole

Now, starting from the anterior contractile vacuole, slowly move toward the upper right, following the direction in which the Paramecium’s body extends. After passing several small, rounded bumps, you will encounter a larger, circular structure. This represents a food vacuole. After Paramecium takes in food, the food becomes enclosed within a membrane-bound compartment, forming a food vacuole. As the food vacuole moves through the cell with the flow of cytoplasm, it fuses with structures containing digestive enzymes, allowing the food inside to be gradually broken down. The nutrients released during digestion then enter the cytoplasm, providing energy and materials for the Paramecium’s life processes. There are several food vacuoles of slightly different sizes distributed throughout the graphic. They perform the same basic function and may represent food vacuoles at different stages of digestion. You may explore the remaining food vacuoles after completing the guide.

## 6. Oral Groove

Now, move your finger to the middle-upper section of the left edge of the Paramecium, looking for a region where the outer outline curves inward. Here, you will encounter a relatively large, elongated structure extending inward from the left edge of the body and gradually becoming narrower. This is the oral groove. Starting from its wider end, slowly trace the oral groove toward its narrower inner end. The oral groove is an important structure involved in feeding. Cilia near the oral groove beat rhythmically to generate water currents, carrying bacteria and tiny organic particles deeper into the groove. The food then passes through the cytostome, or cell mouth, and the cytopharynx, or cell gullet, before entering the cell and forming food vacuoles. Unlike the human mouth, the oral groove does not contain organs such as teeth or a tongue. Instead, it is a specialized depression on the surface of the Paramecium that helps collect and ingest food.

## 7. Cytoproct

Now, slowly move your finger toward the lower right from the oral groove, following the body of the Paramecium and looking for a small opening near its surface. This location represents the cytoproct, also known as the anal pore. The cytoproct is the site where Paramecium expels undigested food residues. After nutrients have been absorbed from a food vacuole, the remaining undigested materials are carried toward the cytoproct and released from the cell through exocytosis. The cytoproct and contractile vacuole have different functions. The cytoproct primarily removes undigested food residues, while the contractile vacuole removes excess water from the cell. Together, these structures help the Paramecium eliminate unwanted materials and maintain normal cellular activities. The position and opening of the cytoproct have been simplified in the graphic to make them easier to identify through touch.

## 8. Macronucleus and Micronucleus

Now, move your finger to the upper-middle region of the Paramecium, slightly toward the right side of its interior. You will encounter a relatively large, oval-shaped bump with a somewhat irregular outline. This structure represents the macronucleus. The macronucleus regulates most of the Paramecium’s everyday cellular activities, including metabolism, growth, and the regulation of internal cellular functions. Slowly trace around the edge of the macronucleus. On one side, you will encounter a much smaller, nearly circular bump. This is the micronucleus. The micronucleus is mainly responsible for preserving and transmitting genetic information and plays an important role in sexual reproduction. Paramecium contains both a macronucleus and a micronucleus, each serving a different function. In simple terms, the macronucleus primarily controls everyday cellular activities, while the micronucleus is mainly involved in genetic inheritance and reproduction.

## 9. Posterior Collecting Canals and Contractile Vacuole

Now, slowly move toward the upper right from the nuclei, approaching the posterior end of the Paramecium. You will once again encounter several long, narrow structures extending toward a central point. These represent the posterior collecting canals. Trace one of these canals toward the center until you reach a small, rounded bump. This is the posterior contractile vacuole. This group of structures performs the same function as the anterior collecting canals and contractile vacuole. The collecting canals gather excess water from the cell, while the contractile vacuole periodically contracts to expel it. Paramecium typically has two contractile vacuole systems, one near the anterior end and the other near the posterior end. Through their regular cycles of water collection and expulsion, these systems help the Paramecium maintain a stable internal environment in freshwater.

## 10. Cytoplasm

Finally, place your finger on one of the relatively flat regions between the raised structures inside the Paramecium. This area, located within the pellicle and surrounding structures such as the nuclei, food vacuoles, and contractile vacuoles, represents the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm includes the cytosol and the various cellular structures distributed within it, providing an essential internal environment for the Paramecium’s life processes. The cytoplasm is capable of flowing, allowing structures such as food vacuoles to move within the cell. In addition, many chemical reactions necessary for maintaining life take place in the cytoplasm. Although the graphic represents the cytoplasm as a relatively flat region, real cytoplasm is not an empty space. Instead, it is a complex environment containing numerous molecules and microscopic structures.
