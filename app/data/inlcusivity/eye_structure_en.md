## 1. Overall Structure of the Eyeball

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line until you reach a large, approximately circular structure. Slowly trace the outer outline of this structure with both hands. You will notice that its overall shape resembles a sphere, but part of it protrudes outward on the left side of the graphic, while a tube-like structure extends toward the right from the opposite side. This entire structure represents the human eyeball. The eyeball is an important part of the visual system. It is located inside the eye socket and is mainly responsible for receiving light from the surrounding environment and converting light signals into nerve signals. The eyeball contains several different structures, including the cornea, iris, lens, vitreous humor, and retina. These structures work together to allow light to enter the eye and eventually form an image on the retina. In the current graphic, the protruding part on the left represents the front of the eyeball, while the part connected to the optic nerve on the right represents the back of the eyeball. The graphic shows a longitudinal cross-section of the eyeball, allowing us to explore structures that are normally located inside the eye and cannot be directly observed from the outside.

## 2. Cornea

Now, move your finger to the far left side of the eyeball and locate the curved structure protruding outward. Starting from the upper end of this structure, slowly trace downward along its outer edge. You will notice that this part of the outline curves outward toward the left side of the graphic, forming a relatively rounded arc. This protruding structure represents the cornea. The cornea is located at the very front of the eyeball. It is a transparent tissue covering the iris and pupil. The cornea helps protect the internal structures of the eye by preventing some dust, microorganisms, and other external substances from entering. In addition, the cornea plays a very important role in refracting light. When light enters the eye from the surrounding environment, it first passes through the cornea. Because air and the cornea have different refractive indices, light changes direction as it passes through the cornea. The cornea provides most of the eye’s refractive power, helping direct light toward the retina. In reality, the cornea is transparent, while the graphic represents its position and shape through a raised, curved outline.

## 3. Iris and Pupil

Move your finger slightly to the right from the curved edge of the cornea, entering the region behind it. Here, you will approach a vertically oriented, oval-shaped structure. Above and below this structure, you can also feel several smaller curved structures. These areas help us understand the relative positions of the iris and lens. The iris is located behind the cornea and in front of the lens. It is a ring-shaped structure inside the eye that gives the eye its color. The eye colors we commonly observe, such as brown, blue, and green, are mainly determined by the pigments and tissue structure of the iris. At the center of the iris is an opening called the pupil. Please note that the pupil is not a separate solid organ. Instead, it is an opening in the center of the iris that allows light to pass through. Therefore, you may not be able to feel the pupil as an independent raised structure in the graphic. The main function of the iris is to regulate the amount of light entering the eye by changing the size of the pupil. When the surrounding light is bright, the circular muscles of the iris contract, making the pupil smaller and reducing the amount of light entering the eye. When the surrounding light is dim, the radial muscles of the iris contract, making the pupil larger and allowing more light to enter. Therefore, by adjusting pupil size, the iris controls the amount of incoming light and helps the eye adapt to different lighting conditions.

## 4. Lens

Now, move your finger to the left interior region of the eyeball and locate a relatively large, oval-shaped structure behind the cornea. You will feel a raised structure extending vertically. Its upper and lower ends gradually narrow, while the middle is wider, giving it a shape similar to a thick optical lens. Slowly trace its edge to explore the curved outline on both sides. This structure represents the lens. The lens is located behind the iris and pupil. It is a transparent, elastic, biconvex structure inside the eyeball. Its main function is to further refract incoming light so that it can be accurately focused on the retina. When we look at objects at different distances, the shape of the lens changes. When viewing nearby objects, the lens becomes more convex, increasing its refractive power. When viewing distant objects, the lens becomes relatively flatter, reducing its refractive power. This process of adjusting the focal length by changing the shape of the lens is called accommodation. Together, the lens and cornea form an important part of the eye’s optical system, allowing us to see objects at different distances clearly. The graphic represents the lens as a distinct oval-shaped raised structure, making it easier to recognize its shape and position through touch.

## 5. Ciliary Body and Suspensory Ligaments

Place your finger on the lens you just located and slowly move upward along its edge. Near the upper end of the lens, you will feel several smaller curved structures. Now, move downward along the edge of the lens. Near its lower end, you can also locate small structures adjacent to it. These areas represent the connections between the lens and the surrounding tissues. In the actual eyeball, the lens is connected to the ciliary body through suspensory ligaments. The ciliary body contains the ciliary muscle, which can contract and relax to change the tension exerted on the lens by the suspensory ligaments. When we look at nearby objects, the ciliary muscle contracts, reducing tension in the suspensory ligaments. As a result, the lens becomes more convex due to its natural elasticity. When we look at distant objects, the ciliary muscle relaxes, increasing tension in the suspensory ligaments and making the lens relatively flatter. Therefore, the ciliary body, suspensory ligaments, and lens must work together to carry out accommodation. The graphic simplifies these small structures and does not represent every suspensory ligament individually. We will therefore understand their functions mainly through the connections near the upper and lower ends of the lens.

## 6. Aqueous Humor

Now, place your finger back on the cornea and slowly move toward the region between the cornea and the lens. You will notice that there is some space between these two structures. This region helps us understand where the aqueous humor is located in the front part of the eyeball. The aqueous humor is a transparent fluid found mainly in the anterior and posterior chambers of the eye. The anterior chamber is located between the cornea and iris, while the posterior chamber is located behind the iris and in front of the lens. The aqueous humor is produced by the ciliary body and continuously circulates and is renewed in the front part of the eye. One of its main functions is to supply nutrients to tissues that lack blood vessels, such as the cornea and lens, while helping remove metabolic waste. In addition, the aqueous humor helps maintain normal pressure inside the eyeball, known as intraocular pressure. Appropriate intraocular pressure helps maintain the normal shape and structure of the eyeball. Please note that the aqueous humor is a transparent liquid rather than a solid organ that can be directly felt. The graphic mainly uses the relative positions of the cornea, iris, and lens to help us understand where it is located.

## 7. Vitreous Humor

Now, starting from the lens, slowly move your finger straight toward the right. After passing the lens, you will enter a large region inside the eyeball. Gently feel this area with your palm. Unlike the lens and surrounding curved structures you explored earlier, most of this region has a relatively flat surface. This area represents the main space occupied by the vitreous humor inside the eyeball. The vitreous humor is located behind the lens and in front of the retina, occupying most of the interior of the eyeball. It is a transparent, gel-like substance composed mainly of water. One of its important functions is to help maintain the shape of the eyeball and provide structural support inside the eye. Because the vitreous humor is transparent, light can pass through it after leaving the lens and continue toward the retina at the back of the eyeball. In addition, the vitreous humor has certain connections with internal eye tissues, including the retina. In reality, the vitreous humor is not a hard, solid structure but a transparent gel. The graphic represents the space it occupies as a large, relatively flat region, allowing us to understand its position inside the eyeball through touch.

## 8. Sclera

Slowly move your finger upward from the vitreous humor region until you approach the outer circular edge of the eyeball. You will encounter several curved structures extending along the outer region of the eyeball, running approximately parallel to one another. These continuous curves represent different tissue layers of the eyeball wall. First, slowly trace the outermost curved outline and explore how it extends around the back of the eyeball. This region represents the sclera, the outermost layer of the eyeball. The sclera is a tough tissue composed mainly of connective tissue. It forms the white part of the eye that we normally see. It covers most of the outer surface of the eyeball and connects to the cornea at the front. The main function of the sclera is to protect the delicate internal structures of the eye while helping the eyeball maintain a stable shape. In addition, some of the extraocular muscles that control eye movement attach to the sclera, allowing the eyeball to rotate in different directions. In the graphic, the sclera is represented by a continuous curved structure around the outer region of the eyeball. In reality, the sclera is a protective tissue layer with a certain thickness.

## 9. Choroid

Now, move your finger slightly inward from the curved edge of the sclera. Between the outer boundary and the interior of the eyeball, you will feel another curved structure following the outline of the eye. This region represents the location of the choroid. The choroid is a tissue layer located between the sclera and retina. It contains numerous blood vessels and pigments. Blood vessels in the choroid supply oxygen and nutrients to the outer layers of the retina, helping the photoreceptor cells maintain their normal functions. In addition, the choroid contains a large amount of pigment that absorbs light not directly used in vision, reducing unnecessary reflection and scattering inside the eyeball. This helps improve the clarity of visual images. Therefore, the choroid not only supplies nutrients but also helps maintain a suitable optical environment inside the eye. The graphic represents the position of the choroid through adjacent curved layers in the eyeball wall.

## 10. Retina

Continue moving your finger inward along the outer region of the eyeball until you locate the inner curved boundary near the vitreous humor. Slowly trace this boundary and explore how it forms a curved outline around the back of the eyeball. This inner layer of the eyeball wall represents the retina. The retina is an important light-sensitive tissue lining the inner surface of the eyeball. After light passes through the cornea, pupil, lens, and vitreous humor, it eventually reaches the retina. The retina contains two main types of photoreceptor cells: rods and cones. Rods are highly sensitive to low levels of light and mainly help us see in dim environments, but they do not distinguish colors. Cones function mainly in brighter conditions, allowing us to distinguish colors and perceive clearer, more detailed visual information. When light stimulates these photoreceptor cells, they convert light stimuli into electrical signals, which are then transmitted and processed by nerve cells within the retina. These signals eventually travel through the optic nerve to the brain. The brain further processes them, allowing us to perceive the shapes, colors, sizes, and positions of objects. The retina in the graphic has been simplified and does not individually represent microscopic structures such as rods and cones. Therefore, we will understand this part mainly through its position along the inner wall of the eyeball.

## 11. Retinal Blood Vessels

Now, slowly move your finger from the inner wall of the eyeball toward the right, entering the region slightly to the right of the center of the graphic. Here, you will encounter several long, narrow, curved raised structures. Slowly trace one of the more distinct narrow structures. You will notice that it gradually divides into branches extending in different directions, forming a tree-like pattern. These structures represent the blood vessels on the surface of the retina. The nerve cells in the retina require a continuous supply of oxygen and nutrients to maintain normal visual function. The central retinal artery and its branches supply blood to the inner layers of the retina, while the central retinal vein and its branches carry blood away from these regions. Meanwhile, the outer layers of the retina receive most of their nutritional support from blood vessels in the choroid. These blood vessels are closely associated with retinal tissue and work together to maintain normal retinal function. The blood vessels in the graphic have been enlarged and simplified, allowing their branching patterns to be clearly felt through touch. In reality, retinal blood vessels are much smaller and have a more complex distribution than those shown in the graphic.

## 12. Optic Disc and Blind Spot

Slowly move your finger along the retinal blood vessels toward the right side of the graphic. You will notice that several narrow branches gradually converge toward the same region. Continue moving right until you approach the connection between the back of the eyeball and the external tube-like structure. This area represents the location of the optic disc. The optic disc is the region where the axons of retinal ganglion cells gather and leave the eyeball. It is also an important location through which the central retinal artery and vein pass. The optic disc has another important characteristic: it contains neither rods nor cones, so it cannot directly detect light. This region corresponds to the physiological blind spot in our visual field. Although each eye has a physiological blind spot, we usually do not notice it under normal conditions. This is because the visual fields of our two eyes complement each other, and the brain can also use surrounding visual information to fill in some of the missing details. The graphic mainly represents the position of the optic disc through the connection between the back of the eyeball and the optic nerve. The blind spot is not shown as a separate raised structure.

## 13. Optic Nerve

Finally, continue moving your finger toward the right from the optic disc region you just located. You will encounter a relatively thick, tube-like structure extending outward from the back of the eyeball. Slowly trace this tube-like structure until you reach its far-right end. This structure represents the optic nerve. The optic nerve consists of a large number of axons from retinal ganglion cells and is responsible for transmitting nerve signals generated by the retina to the brain. After light enters the eyeball and stimulates the photoreceptor cells in the retina, visual information is processed by nerve cells within the retina and eventually carried out of the eyeball through the optic nerve. These signals then travel along the visual pathways to the visual processing regions of the brain. By processing these nerve signals, the brain allows us to form visual perceptions of objects in the surrounding environment. Therefore, although the eyeball receives light and converts it into nerve signals, the complete process of vision also requires the brain. Please note that the optic nerve is not a tube that transports light. Instead, it is a structure responsible for transmitting nerve signals. The graphic represents the optic nerve as a relatively thick, tube-like structure extending from the back of the eyeball, making it easier to recognize its position and direction through touch.
