Opening: Welcome to the guided exploration of the ear tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. Next, we will begin with the overall structure of the ear and gradually explore the outer, middle, and inner ear to understand how the human body detects sounds and maintains balance.

## 1. Overall Structure of the Ear

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. You will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to reach the ear model. On the left side of the graphic, you will feel a large, irregularly curved structure. Moving toward the right, you will encounter a long, tube-like passageway. Continue moving right to reach a central region containing several smaller structures. To the right of this region, you will find a prominent spiral-shaped structure. Together, these features represent the major components of the ear, from the outside to the inside. The human ear is divided into three main regions: the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear. The outer ear collects and directs sound waves, the middle ear transmits vibrations through the auditory ossicles, and the inner ear converts mechanical vibrations into nerve signals while also contributing to balance.

## 2. Pinna

Now, move your finger to the large, curved structure on the far left of the graphic. Slowly trace its outer edge, feeling its irregular outline and several curved ridges within it. This structure is the pinna, also known as the auricle, which is the visible external part of the ear. The pinna consists mainly of elastic cartilage covered by skin. Its primary function is to collect sound waves from the surrounding environment and direct them into the external auditory canal. Its distinctive curved shape also helps us determine where sounds are coming from, particularly their position in the vertical direction.

## 3. External Auditory Canal

Now, return your finger to the central part of the pinna and slowly move toward the right. You will encounter a tube-like structure extending inward. This is the external auditory canal, also known as the ear canal. Slowly trace this passageway, feeling how it extends from the pinna toward the deeper structures of the ear. The external auditory canal connects the pinna to the eardrum and carries sound waves toward it. It also produces resonance at certain frequencies, enhancing the transmission of some sounds. In addition, earwax and fine hairs within the canal help trap dust and other foreign particles, protecting the internal structures of the ear.

## 4. Eardrum

Now, continue moving right along the external auditory canal until you reach its end. Here, you will encounter a relatively thin, sheet-like or curved structure. This represents the eardrum, also called the tympanic membrane. The eardrum is a thin membrane separating the external auditory canal from the middle ear. When sound waves travel through the ear canal and reach the eardrum, they cause it to vibrate. These vibrations are then transmitted to the auditory ossicles in the middle ear, allowing sound to continue toward the inner ear. The eardrum therefore plays an important role in sound transmission while also separating the outer ear from the middle ear cavity.

## 5. Auditory Ossicles

Now, continue moving slowly toward the right from the eardrum into the central region of the middle ear. You will encounter several small, interconnected bumps and curved structures representing the auditory ossicles. The human middle ear contains three auditory ossicles: the malleus, incus, and stapes. Starting near the eardrum, slowly trace these structures toward the right to explore how they connect. The malleus is attached to the eardrum and transfers its vibrations to the incus. The incus then passes the vibrations to the stapes, which transmits them into the inner ear through the oval window. Through their lever action and the difference in surface area between the eardrum and oval window, the ossicles increase the pressure transmitted to the inner ear. This allows sound vibrations to pass more efficiently from the air into the fluid-filled inner ear.

## 6. Eustachian Tube

Now, return your finger to the middle ear region and slowly move straight downward. You will encounter a long, narrow tube extending downward from the middle ear. This is the Eustachian tube, also known as the auditory tube. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear cavity to the nasopharynx, the upper part of the throat behind the nose. Its main functions are to equalize air pressure on both sides of the eardrum and help drain secretions from the middle ear. Normally, the Eustachian tube remains closed most of the time but briefly opens during activities such as swallowing or yawning. For example, when an airplane takes off or lands, changes in external air pressure may cause a feeling of fullness in the ears. Swallowing or yawning can help open the Eustachian tube and gradually restore the pressure balance across the eardrum.

## 7. Semicircular Canals

Now, return your finger to the central region of the middle ear and slowly move straight upward. You will encounter several curved, loop-like structures representing the semicircular canals. Slowly trace each loop with your finger, feeling its curved outline. Each inner ear contains three semicircular canals: the anterior, posterior, and lateral semicircular canals. They are arranged in approximately three mutually perpendicular planes. To make them easier to explore through touch, the graphic simplifies these canals into relatively regular loops. In reality, the semicircular canals are not perfectly closed circular rings. They contain fluid and sensory structures that detect head movements. When the head rotates, the relative movement of fluid stimulates sensory hair cells, helping the brain detect changes in the direction and speed of head rotation. The semicircular canals therefore play an important role in maintaining balance.

## 8. Vestibule

Now, move your finger downward and toward the right from the semicircular canals. You will reach a relatively broad region near the center of the inner ear. This is the vestibule. The vestibule is located between the semicircular canals and the cochlea and forms an important part of the inner ear. It contains two structures called the utricle and saccule, which have sensory organs capable of detecting gravity and linear acceleration. For example, when we travel upward or downward in an elevator or experience sudden acceleration in a vehicle, sensory cells within these structures help detect changes in movement. Together with the semicircular canals, the vestibule forms part of the vestibular system, helping the body sense head position and movement while maintaining posture and balance.

## 9. Cochlea

Now, slowly move your finger toward the right from the vestibule. You will encounter a prominent spiral-shaped structure resembling a snail shell. This is the cochlea. Starting from the outer edge, slowly trace the spiral toward its center, feeling how it gradually coils inward. The cochlea is the main structure responsible for hearing within the inner ear. After sound vibrations pass through the eardrum and auditory ossicles, movements of the stapes at the oval window cause fluid and other structures inside the cochlea to move. These movements stimulate sensory hair cells, which convert mechanical stimulation into electrical signals and trigger nerve impulses in the auditory nerve. Different regions of the cochlea respond most strongly to different sound frequencies, allowing us to distinguish between high-pitched and low-pitched sounds.

## 10. Auditory Nerve

Finally, move your finger to the area near the connection between the cochlea and the central region of the inner ear. Look for a long, narrow structure extending outward from this area. This represents the auditory nerve. Slowly trace it with your finger, feeling the direction in which it extends away from the inner ear. The auditory nerve, also called the cochlear nerve, is the part of the vestibulocochlear nerve responsible for transmitting auditory information. It carries nerve signals generated in the cochlea to the brainstem. These signals then travel through several neural pathways to the auditory cortex of the brain. After processing these signals, the brain allows us to perceive sounds and distinguish differences in pitch, loudness, and sound location. Please note that the vestibulocochlear nerve also contains the vestibular nerve, which transmits information related to balance. However, this structure in the graphic primarily represents the transmission of auditory signals.

You have now learned about the pinna, external auditory canal, eardrum, auditory ossicles, Eustachian tube, semicircular canals, vestibule, cochlea, and auditory nerve, as well as their roles in hearing and balance. Sound waves are collected by the outer ear, transmitted through the middle ear, and eventually converted into nerve signals in the inner ear for processing by the brain. Meanwhile, the vestibule and semicircular canals continuously help us detect the position and movement of our heads.
