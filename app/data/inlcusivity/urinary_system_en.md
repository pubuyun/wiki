### 1. Overall Structure of the Urinary System

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line, and you will encounter a relatively large, rounded raised structure in the lower region of the graphic. Continue exploring upward. You will feel two long, narrow, tube-like structures extending upward from the lower region, eventually connecting to two larger structures in the upper-left and upper-right regions of the graphic. Place one hand on each of these structures. Both have shapes resembling beans, with outwardly curved outer edges and slightly indented inner edges facing the center. These two structures represent the kidneys. Now, move your fingers to the region between the kidneys. Here, you will encounter two relatively thick, vertically extending tube-like structures, along with several smaller branches extending outward from the center toward both sides. These structures represent the major blood vessels connected to the kidneys. Try gently feeling the surfaces of the two thicker tubes. You will notice that one has a relatively smooth surface, while the other has a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges. These different tactile features help us distinguish arteries from veins. Together, these structures represent the urinary system and its associated blood vessels as shown in the graphic. The human urinary system mainly consists of two kidneys, two ureters, one urinary bladder, and one urethra. The kidneys filter blood, regulate the amounts of water and electrolytes in the body, and produce urine containing metabolic waste products, excess water, and other substances. Urine then travels through the ureters into the bladder for temporary storage before leaving the body through the urethra. In addition to eliminating metabolic waste, the urinary system helps regulate water, electrolyte, and acid-base balance, maintaining a stable internal environment.

### 2. Left Kidney

Move both hands to the upper-left region of the graphic. Here, you will encounter a relatively large raised structure with a smooth, rounded surface. Place your finger at the uppermost part of this structure and slowly trace its outer edge downward. You will notice that the outer edge curves outward, forming a rounded outline. Continue tracing along the lower edge, then move upward along the side closest to the center of the graphic. You will notice that this inner edge does not curve outward like the outer edge. Instead, it contains an inward indentation. The overall shape resembles a bean and represents the kidney on the left side of the graphic. The kidneys are important organs of the urinary system responsible for producing urine. Blood continuously flows through the kidneys. Inside each kidney are numerous microscopic structures called nephrons, which are the basic functional units responsible for urine formation. After blood enters the kidneys, it undergoes processes including filtration, selective reabsorption, and secretion. Most of the water and useful substances needed by the body are returned to the bloodstream, while certain metabolic waste products, excess water, and electrolytes are eliminated through urine. The kidneys also help regulate blood pressure and acid-base balance and contribute to the regulation of red blood cell production by secreting erythropoietin. The graphic mainly shows the external outline of the kidney and does not fully represent microscopic internal structures such as nephrons. The internal structure of a real kidney is much more complex than what is shown in the graphic.

### 3. Right Kidney

Now, slowly move your fingers toward the right from the upper region of the left kidney, passing over the two tube-like structures in the center of the graphic until you reach another large raised structure in the upper-right region. Place both hands on this structure and slowly trace its entire outer edge. You will notice that its shape is very similar to that of the left kidney you explored earlier. It also has an outwardly curved outer edge and an indented region facing the center of the graphic. This structure represents the other kidney, located on the right side of the graphic. Under normal circumstances, humans have two kidneys, positioned on either side of the vertebral column, behind the peritoneum. Both kidneys perform essentially the same functions, filtering blood and producing urine. Although the human body has two kidneys, they are not responsible for filtering different types of waste. Both kidneys participate in regulating water, electrolytes, and metabolic waste products, working together to maintain a stable internal environment. In the human body, the right kidney is usually positioned slightly lower than the left because of the location of the liver. Please note that this graphic presents the urinary system from a frontal view of the human body. Therefore, the left and right sides of the graphic do not correspond directly to the anatomical left and right sides of the person represented. The two kidneys have been simplified in the graphic to emphasize their positions, external shapes, and connections with blood vessels and ureters.

### 4. Renal Hilum

Place your finger back on the kidney in the upper-left region of the graphic. Slowly move along its inner edge, which faces the center of the graphic. You will feel an inward indentation along this side of the kidney. Near this region, you will also encounter several smaller tube-like structures extending toward the center or lower part of the graphic. This indented region represents the renal hilum. The renal hilum is the area on the inner side of the kidney through which structures such as blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatic vessels enter or leave the kidney. It is also the region where the renal pelvis connects to the ureter. The renal artery carries blood into the kidney through this region, while the renal vein carries blood away from the kidney. Try feeling the surfaces of the blood vessels connected near the renal hilum. You will notice a difference in their textures: the renal artery has a smooth surface, while the renal vein has raised, ring-shaped ridges. Urine produced by the kidney gradually collects in the renal calyces and renal pelvis before leaving the kidney through the ureter connected to the renal pelvis. Now, move your finger to the corresponding region on the inner side of the kidney on the right side of the graphic. You will find another indentation facing the center. This represents the renal hilum of the other kidney, which also serves as an important connection point for blood vessels and the urinary drainage pathway.

### 5. Abdominal Aorta

Now, slowly move your fingers from the kidney on the right side of the graphic toward the center. Between the two kidneys, you will encounter two relatively thick, vertically extending tube-like structures. Locate the thicker tube positioned slightly to the right of the center of the graphic, with a relatively smooth surface. Place your finger at its upper end and slowly trace it downward. You will notice that this tube extends downward and gradually divides into different branches in the lower region of the graphic. Slowly trace this tube again, paying attention to its smooth surface. Unlike the nearby inferior vena cava, which has a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges, this tube does not have prominent ring-shaped textures. This structure represents the abdominal aorta. The abdominal aorta is the portion of the aorta located in the abdomen. It carries oxygen-rich blood from the heart to various organs and tissues in the abdomen and lower limbs. Near the kidneys, the abdominal aorta gives rise to the left and right renal arteries, which supply blood to the two kidneys. The kidneys require a continuous and sufficient blood supply to filter blood and produce urine. The abdominal aorta itself does not transport urine but belongs to the circulatory system. In the graphic, the abdominal aorta has been enlarged and given a smooth surface, allowing us to identify its direction, major branches, and differences from nearby veins through touch.

### 6. Renal Arteries

Move your finger back to the abdominal aorta at approximately the same height as the two kidneys. From the side of the abdominal aorta, slowly trace one of the short, tube-like branches extending toward a kidney. You will notice that these branches extend outward from the thicker central blood vessel toward both sides, connecting to the regions near the renal hila. Slowly move your fingers along these branches, feeling their relatively smooth surfaces. These structures represent the renal arteries. Like the abdominal aorta, the renal arteries have smooth surfaces in the graphic, distinguishing them from the renal veins, which have raised, ring-shaped ridges. The renal arteries carry blood from the abdominal aorta into the kidneys. After entering the kidneys, they divide into progressively smaller blood vessels, eventually supplying blood to numerous nephrons. Within the nephrons, the glomeruli filter some of the water and small molecules from the blood, producing glomerular filtrate, also known as primary urine. Through subsequent processes of reabsorption and secretion, the composition of this filtrate changes continuously until urine is formed. Therefore, although the renal arteries belong to the circulatory system, they are essential for urine formation in the kidneys. Please note that the blood vessel branches shown in the graphic have been simplified. In reality, the renal arteries continue branching after entering the kidneys, forming a much more complex vascular network.

### 7. Renal Veins

Now, place your finger back near the renal hilum on the inner side of a kidney. In addition to the renal artery you located earlier, you will find another tube-like structure connecting the kidney to the other thick blood vessel in the center of the graphic. Slowly trace this connecting structure from the kidney toward the center. You will notice a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges along its surface, making it noticeably different from the smooth renal artery you explored earlier. This structure represents the renal vein. The graphic uses these raised, ring-shaped ridges to help us distinguish the renal vein from the renal artery. The renal veins carry blood away from the kidneys and drain it into the inferior vena cava. Inside the kidneys, blood undergoes filtration in the glomeruli and exchanges substances with the surrounding tissues through the capillaries around the renal tubules. It eventually leaves the kidneys through the venous system. The renal veins transport blood, not urine. This is an important distinction. Although the kidneys are connected to both blood vessels and ureters, these structures perform different functions. The renal arteries and renal veins transport blood, while the ureters carry urine produced by the kidneys to the urinary bladder.

### 8. Inferior Vena Cava

Continue moving your finger along the renal vein toward the center of the graphic. You will encounter a relatively thick tube-like structure. It runs approximately parallel to the abdominal aorta you explored earlier and is located slightly to the left of the center of the graphic. Place your finger on this tube and slowly trace it both upward and downward. You will notice a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges along its surface, creating a segmented tactile texture. This structure represents the inferior vena cava. Try placing your other hand on the nearby abdominal aorta and comparing the surfaces of the two blood vessels. The abdominal aorta has a relatively smooth surface, while the inferior vena cava has prominent raised, ring-shaped ridges. This design helps us distinguish the two adjacent major blood vessels through touch. The inferior vena cava is one of the major veins in the human body. It collects venous blood from various regions of the lower body and returns it to the right atrium of the heart. The renal veins from both kidneys eventually drain into the inferior vena cava. Although the inferior vena cava and abdominal aorta are located close to each other in the graphic, blood flows through them in different directions. The abdominal aorta carries blood from the heart toward the abdomen and lower limbs, while the inferior vena cava returns venous blood from the lower body to the heart. Together, these two major blood vessels participate in blood circulation and support the normal functioning of the kidneys.

### 9. Ureters

Now, place both hands near the lower inner edges of the two kidneys. Starting from these positions, slowly trace the long, narrow tube-like structures extending downward. You will notice that two narrow tubes extend downward from the regions near the left and right kidneys, pass through the middle and lower parts of the graphic, and eventually connect to a larger rounded structure below. These two narrow structures represent the ureters. Under normal circumstances, humans have two ureters, one connected to each kidney. The main function of the ureters is to transport urine produced by the kidneys to the urinary bladder. After urine is formed in the kidneys, it first collects in the renal pelvis before entering the ureters. The walls of the ureters contain smooth muscle. This muscle produces rhythmic contractions known as peristalsis, which propel urine along the ureters toward the bladder. Therefore, urine does not flow into the bladder solely because of gravity. It can also be actively propelled by the peristaltic movements of the ureters. Both ureters eventually enter the bladder, allowing urine from the two kidneys to collect in one place. In the graphic, the ureters have been enlarged into two clearly distinguishable narrow structures. In reality, the ureters contain an internal passage and muscular walls that allow them to transport urine continuously.

### 10. Urinary Bladder

Continue moving your finger downward along either of the ureters you just located. As you approach the lower region of the graphic, you will encounter a relatively large raised structure with a smooth surface. Place both hands on the upper part of this structure and slowly trace its entire outer edge. You will notice that its upper region is relatively wide, with sides that curve outward, forming a rounded, pouch-like outline that gradually narrows toward the bottom. This structure represents the urinary bladder. The urinary bladder is the organ responsible for temporarily storing urine in the human urinary system. Urine produced by the two kidneys travels through their respective ureters before entering the bladder. The bladder wall contains a substantial layer of smooth muscle and is capable of stretching. As urine gradually enters the bladder, the bladder expands to accommodate and temporarily store it. As the bladder fills, stretch receptors in its wall detect changes in tension and contribute to the urination reflex through nerve signals. When conditions are appropriate for urination, the detrusor muscle in the bladder wall contracts while the urethral sphincters relax in a coordinated manner, allowing urine to leave the body through the urethra. The bladder does not produce urine. Its main functions are to store urine and facilitate its elimination. In the graphic, the bladder is represented by a relatively full, pouch-like raised structure. In reality, the shape and size of the bladder change depending on the amount of urine stored inside.

### 11. Urethra

Finally, place your finger at the lowest part of the urinary bladder. From the region where the bladder gradually narrows, continue moving straight downward. You will encounter a short, narrow tube-like structure extending from the bottom of the bladder. This structure represents the urethra. The urethra is the passage through which urine travels after leaving the bladder before being eliminated from the body. During urination, the detrusor muscle in the bladder wall contracts while the urethral sphincters relax in a coordinated manner, allowing the urine stored in the bladder to flow out through the urethra. Although the terms urethra and ureter sound similar, these structures differ in their positions and functions. The ureters connect the kidneys to the bladder and transport urine from the kidneys into the bladder. The urethra, on the other hand, connects the bladder to the outside of the body and allows urine to leave the body. In the human body, the male and female urethras differ in length and structure. The graphic simplifies the urethra, mainly illustrating its connection to the urinary bladder.
