### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the human digestive system tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall structure of the digestive system, oral cavity, salivary glands, pharynx and esophagus, stomach, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, duodenum, small intestine, large intestine, appendix, and rectum and anus.

### 1. Overall Structure of the Digestive System

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line until you reach the outline of the lower part of the human body. Slowly move both hands upward along the body’s outline. You will feel the shoulders extending outward on both sides and the rounded outline of the head at the very top. Now, move both hands back toward the center of the body and slowly explore the raised structures inside. You will encounter organs of different shapes: some are long and narrow tubes, others are relatively large with smooth surfaces, and some consist of tubes that curve and coil repeatedly. Together, these structures form the human digestive system represented in the graphic. The digestive system is mainly responsible for breaking down food into smaller nutrient molecules that the body can absorb and use, while eliminating materials that cannot be digested or absorbed. After food enters the oral cavity, it passes through the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine before eventually leaving the body through the rectum and anus. In addition, organs such as the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas also participate in digestion. Next, we will explore these structures step by step, following the general path that food takes through the human body.

### 2. Oral Cavity

Move both hands to the very top of the graphic and locate the rounded outline of the head. Slowly trace downward along the left edge of the head until you encounter a part that protrudes toward the left. At this position, you can feel a small opening that represents the oral cavity. Slowly trace the edge of the oral cavity with your finger to explore how it connects to the passage behind it. The oral cavity is the first part of the digestive system that food enters. When food enters the mouth, the teeth cut and grind it through chewing, breaking it into smaller pieces. Meanwhile, the tongue helps mix food with saliva and pushes it toward the pharynx, assisting with swallowing. Food undergoes not only physical digestion in the oral cavity but also some chemical digestion. For example, salivary amylase begins breaking down starch in food. The graphic mainly shows the overall position and shape of the oral cavity without fully representing smaller structures such as the teeth and tongue.

### 3. Salivary Glands

Now, keep your finger near the oral cavity and explore its position relative to the surrounding structures of the head. In the actual human body, several groups of salivary glands are located around the oral cavity, including the parotid glands, submandibular glands, and sublingual glands. These glands produce saliva and release it into the oral cavity through ducts. Saliva moistens and softens food, making it easier to chew and swallow. In addition, saliva contains salivary amylase, an enzyme that begins breaking down some starch into smaller sugar molecules. Therefore, although the salivary glands are not part of the digestive tract through which food directly passes, they play an important role in digestion by secreting saliva. Please note that the graphic mainly shows the overall structures of the oral cavity and its surrounding region. The salivary glands are not clearly represented as individually identifiable raised structures, so we will understand their location mainly in relation to the oral cavity.

### 4. Pharynx and Esophagus

Place your finger back on the oral cavity and slowly move downward along the passage behind it. You will notice that the region behind the oral cavity gradually connects to a passage extending downward. This region represents the pharynx. The pharynx is an important connection between the oral cavity and the esophagus. It is also a shared passage for both air and food. When we swallow, the body performs a series of coordinated movements that direct food into the esophagus while reducing the possibility of food entering the trachea. Now, continue moving your finger downward along this passage. You will encounter a relatively long, continuous tube-like structure extending downward. It passes through the neck and chest before connecting to a larger, sac-like structure in the upper abdomen. This long, narrow tube represents the esophagus. The main function of the esophagus is to transport swallowed food from the pharynx to the stomach. The wall of the esophagus contains layers of muscle that contract and relax in a coordinated manner, producing a type of movement called peristalsis. Peristalsis pushes food downward through the esophagus until it reaches the stomach. The esophagus itself is not a major site of digestion or nutrient absorption. Its primary function is to transport food. The graphic simplifies the esophagus into a distinct tube-like structure, making it easier to understand the path food takes from the mouth to the stomach through touch.

### 5. Stomach

Continue moving your finger downward along the esophagus. When you reach its lower end, you will encounter a raised structure that becomes noticeably wider. This structure is located in the upper-right region of the abdomen in the graphic. It has an irregular outline, with one side relatively rounded and the other gradually narrowing. Slowly trace its outer edge to explore its curved, enlarged shape. This structure represents the stomach. The stomach is an important digestive organ in the human digestive system. After food enters the stomach through the esophagus, muscles in the stomach wall repeatedly contract, mixing and churning the food with gastric juice. Gastric juice contains hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes. Hydrochloric acid provides an acidic environment, while pepsin begins breaking down proteins into shorter peptides. Through continuous mixing and initial digestion in the stomach, food gradually forms a semi-fluid mixture called chyme. The chyme then leaves the lower end of the stomach in small amounts and enters the small intestine for further digestion and absorption. The stomach wall also contains a protective mucus layer that helps reduce damage caused by stomach acid and digestive enzymes. The stomach in the graphic has been simplified to emphasize its enlarged shape and its connections to the esophagus and small intestine.

### 6. Liver

Now, slowly move your finger from the upper part of the stomach toward the left. You will encounter a raised structure that is wider than the stomach and has a relatively smooth surface. It is located in the upper-left region of the abdomen in the graphic. Its overall outline is broad and gradually narrows toward the bottom. Slowly trace the outer edge of this structure to explore its position relative to the nearby stomach. This structure represents the liver. The liver is an important metabolic organ and the largest internal gland in the human body. One of its major roles in digestion is producing bile. Bile helps emulsify fats, breaking large fat globules into many smaller droplets. This increases the surface area available for lipase to act on, helping the digestion of fats. Please note that bile itself does not contain digestive enzymes, so it does not directly break down fats through enzyme-catalyzed reactions. In addition to producing bile, the liver is involved in nutrient metabolism, glycogen storage, and the processing of harmful substances. Although the liver participates in digestion, food does not pass directly through it. The graphic mainly shows the outer outline of the liver without fully representing its complex internal blood vessels and bile ducts.

### 7. Gallbladder

Place your finger along the lower edge of the liver and slowly move toward the center of the graphic. Beneath the liver, you can locate a smaller, sac-like raised structure extending downward. This structure is much smaller than the liver and is positioned closer to its lower surface. It represents the gallbladder. The main function of the gallbladder is to store and concentrate bile produced by the liver. When food containing fat enters the small intestine, the gallbladder can contract and release stored bile through the bile ducts into the duodenum. Once bile enters the small intestine, it helps emulsify fats, making them easier for lipase to digest. Therefore, the liver and gallbladder perform different functions during digestion. The liver produces bile, while the gallbladder mainly stores, concentrates, and releases it. The gallbladder in the graphic is relatively small, so you will need to distinguish it carefully from the much larger liver above it.

### 8. Pancreas

Now, slowly move your finger from the region near the gallbladder toward the right, returning to the area below the stomach. Beneath the stomach, you will encounter a relatively long, narrow structure extending horizontally. It is located close to the stomach and near the uppermost curved section of the small intestine. This structure represents the pancreas. The pancreas is one of the body’s important digestive glands. During digestion, it produces pancreatic juice and releases it into the duodenum through the pancreatic duct. Pancreatic juice contains several digestive enzymes. For example, pancreatic amylase participates in starch digestion, proteases such as trypsin help break down proteins further, and pancreatic lipase participates in fat digestion. In addition, pancreatic juice contains bicarbonate ions, which help neutralize the acidic chyme arriving from the stomach and create suitable conditions for digestive enzymes in the small intestine. The pancreas also helps regulate blood glucose levels by secreting hormones such as insulin and glucagon. Please note that food does not pass directly through the pancreas. Instead, the pancreas participates in digestion mainly by releasing digestive fluids into the small intestine.

### 9. Duodenum

Place your finger back below the stomach and locate the point where the stomach gradually narrows and connects to another tube. Continue tracing this tube until you encounter a curved section. It is located below the stomach, close to the pancreas, and gradually extends toward the center of the abdomen. This section represents the duodenum. The duodenum is the first part of the small intestine and the first region that chyme enters after leaving the stomach. When chyme enters the duodenum, it mixes with pancreatic juice from the pancreas and bile produced by the liver and delivered through the bile ducts. These digestive fluids help further break down carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, with bile mainly assisting fat digestion through emulsification. In addition, the duodenum receives bicarbonate ions from the pancreas and other sources, helping neutralize acidic chyme and create an environment more suitable for digestive enzymes. Therefore, the duodenum is a particularly important region in digestion, where the functions of the stomach, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas are closely connected.

### 10. Small Intestine

Now, continue moving your finger downward from the duodenum toward the central region of the abdomen in the graphic. Here, you will encounter many closely arranged, long, narrow tube-like structures that curve repeatedly. Slowly trace one of these tubes to explore its continuous bends and coils. You will notice that the tubes lie close together, forming a large region of irregular loops. This region represents the small intestine. The small intestine consists mainly of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. It is the major site where most chemical digestion and nutrient absorption take place in the human body. In the small intestine, digestive enzymes continue breaking food down into smaller molecules. For example, carbohydrates can eventually be broken down into monosaccharides, proteins into amino acids, and fats into fatty acids and monoglycerides, among other products. These products of digestion are then absorbed through the wall of the small intestine. In reality, the inner surface of the small intestine contains numerous circular folds, villi, and microvilli, which greatly increase the surface area available for absorption. Inside the intestinal villi are blood capillaries and central lacteals, which participate in transporting different nutrients. The small intestine in the graphic has been enlarged and simplified to emphasize its long, coiled external shape, without fully representing microscopic structures such as the villi.

### 11. Large Intestine

Slowly move your finger outward from the small intestine in the central abdomen. You will notice that a relatively thicker tube-like structure surrounds the smaller, coiled tubes. Slowly trace this thicker tube. You will feel that it extends around the small intestine, forming an approximately inverted U-shaped outline. This structure represents the large intestine. The large intestine mainly consists of the cecum, colon, and rectum, among other parts. In the graphic, the large intestine is noticeably thicker than the coiled small intestine inside it, allowing you to distinguish the two structures by their shapes and positions. Material that has not been digested or absorbed in the small intestine passes into the large intestine. One of the large intestine’s important functions is to absorb remaining water and some electrolytes, gradually making the intestinal contents more concentrated. In addition, the large intestine contains numerous intestinal microorganisms that can break down some substances the human body cannot digest and participate in the production of certain vitamins. As water continues to be absorbed, the remaining material gradually forms feces. Through movements such as peristalsis, the large intestine transports feces toward the rectum.

### 12. Appendix

Now, move your finger to the lower-left region of the abdomen in the graphic and locate the area beneath the large intestine. Slowly explore the edge of the large intestine. On one side, you will encounter a small, short protruding structure extending downward. This structure represents the appendix. The appendix is a narrow, tube-like structure connected to the cecum, near the beginning of the large intestine. Unlike the main sections of the small and large intestines, the appendix does not play a major role in food digestion or nutrient absorption. The appendix contains relatively abundant lymphoid tissue and is therefore thought to participate in intestinal immune functions. In addition, the appendix may provide a relatively stable environment for certain beneficial intestinal microorganisms. The graphic simplifies the appendix into a small protrusion beside the large intestine to help us recognize its shape and position.

### 13. Rectum and Anus

Finally, move your finger from the lower part of the large intestine along the thicker tube toward the center of the graphic. You will notice that the large intestine gradually extends downward, forming a relatively straight section at the bottom of the abdomen. This section represents the rectum. The rectum is located at the end of the large intestine and is mainly responsible for temporarily storing feces before they are eliminated from the body. When feces enter the rectum, the rectal wall stretches, stimulating sensory receptors that generate nerve signals and create the sensation of needing to defecate. Now, continue moving your finger downward along the rectum until you reach the lowest end of this tube in the graphic. This end corresponds to the direction of the anus. The anus is the final opening of the digestive tract and is responsible for eliminating feces from the body. Sphincter muscles surrounding the anus help control the process of defecation. During defecation, the muscles around the rectum and anus work together to allow feces to pass through the anus. Therefore, although the rectum and anus are located close to each other, they perform different functions. The rectum mainly stores feces temporarily, while the anus controls their final elimination from the body. The graphic mainly shows the downward extension of the rectum, while the anus is not represented as a distinct, separate raised structure.
