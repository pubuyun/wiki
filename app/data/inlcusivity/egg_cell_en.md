Opening: Welcome to the guided exploration of the egg cell tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. Next, we will explore the overall structure of the egg cell, the corona radiata cells, the zona pellucida, and the main structures inside the egg cell, learning about their positions and functions.

## 1. Overall Structure

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the egg cell region of the graphic. Slowly move both hands outward to explore the overall shape of the graphic. You will notice that the entire structure is circular. The outermost layer consists of a ring of individually raised, rounded structures. Moving inward, you will encounter a circular region, followed by a larger circular structure at the center representing the egg cell itself. Together, these structures represent the egg cell and its surrounding protective structures. The egg cell is the female reproductive cell. It is a single cell and is much larger than a sperm cell. It carries genetic material from the mother and combines with a sperm cell during fertilization to form a zygote, which can develop further under suitable conditions. Next, we will begin with the outermost region of the graphic and gradually move inward to explore each structure.

## 2. Corona Radiata Cells

Now, move your fingers to the outermost ring of raised structures. You will encounter many individually raised, rounded structures arranged in a circle around the central egg cell. Place your fingertip on one of these structures and carefully explore its surface. You will notice a smaller circular bump on each larger raised structure, representing its nucleus. These cells surrounding the egg cell are known as corona radiata cells. Slowly move your fingertip clockwise or counterclockwise along the outermost ring, feeling how these cells are closely arranged around the egg cell. The corona radiata cells are a layer of follicular granulosa cells surrounding the zona pellucida of the oocyte. They provide support and protection to the oocyte and contribute to nutrient supply and communication between cells. During fertilization, sperm typically need to pass through the region containing the corona radiata cells before they can reach the zona pellucida. The graphic uses a ring of individually raised, rounded structures to help us understand the positions of the corona radiata cells and how they are arranged around the oocyte.

## 3. Zona Pellucida

Now, slowly move your fingertip inward from the outermost corona radiata cells toward the center. After moving past the corona radiata cells, you will encounter a circular recessed region. This ring-shaped region represents the zona pellucida. Slowly trace this circular structure with your fingertip, feeling how it forms a continuous ring around the central egg cell. The zona pellucida is an extracellular layer composed mainly of glycoproteins. It surrounds the oocyte outside the cell membrane and is itself surrounded by the corona radiata cells. The zona pellucida provides protection to the oocyte and plays an important role during fertilization. When a sperm approaches the oocyte, it must interact with and pass through the zona pellucida before it can reach the oocyte’s cell membrane. After fertilization occurs, the oocyte undergoes a series of changes that alter the properties of the zona pellucida, helping prevent additional sperm from entering and reducing the likelihood of polyspermy, which occurs when more than one sperm fertilizes an egg. Please note that the zona pellucida is not actually a groove. In reality, it is an extracellular layer with a certain thickness. The graphic uses a circular recessed region to help us distinguish the zona pellucida from the surrounding corona radiata cells and the central oocyte through touch.

## 4. Egg Cell and Internal Structures

Now, continue moving your finger inward from the zona pellucida toward the center. You will enter a larger circular region representing the egg cell itself. The outermost boundary of the egg cell is formed by the cell membrane, which separates the cell’s internal environment from its surroundings and regulates the movement of substances into and out of the cell. Slowly move your finger across the central region, feeling the different raised structures within it. Most of the interior of the egg cell consists of cytoplasm, which contains various organelles. You will encounter several small, rounded bumps of different sizes. These structures represent organelles such as mitochondria. Mitochondria produce ATP through cellular respiration, providing energy for the cell’s activities. Next, continue exploring the cytoplasm until you locate a more prominent circular raised structure. This structure represents the nucleus, which contains genetic material in the form of DNA. Move your finger toward the center of the nucleus, where you will encounter an even smaller raised bump representing the nucleolus. The nucleolus is mainly involved in the synthesis of ribosomal RNA and the assembly of ribosomal subunits. Now, move your finger toward the lower part of the cytoplasmic region. Here, you will encounter a small, oval-shaped raised structure representing the first polar body. The first polar body is a small cell formed after a primary oocyte completes the first meiotic division. It typically contains only a small amount of cytoplasm. The formation of the first polar body indicates that the oocyte has completed meiosis I. Please note that in humans, the cell released during ovulation is usually a secondary oocyte arrested at metaphase II of meiosis, rather than a mature egg cell that has completed the entire process of meiosis. Therefore, the graphic is primarily intended to help us understand the basic components of the oocyte and its surrounding structures, rather than accurately represent every detail of a specific developmental stage at ovulation. By exploring the central circular region and the raised structures of different sizes within it, we can develop a better understanding of the egg cell’s basic structure and functions.
