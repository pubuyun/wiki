### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the bacterial cell tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall outline of the bacterial cell, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, flagella, pili, nucleoid, plasmid, and ribosomes.

### Overall Outline of the Bacterial Cell

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the bacterial cell region of the graphic. Slowly move both hands outward until you reach the outermost edge of the bacterial cell. Then, gently trace this edge with your fingers. You will feel an elongated, oval-shaped outline. The entire region enclosed by this outline represents the bacterial cell shown in the graphic. Bacteria are unicellular microorganisms that are usually extremely small, and most can only be observed with the help of a microscope. Bacteria come in various shapes, including spherical, rod-shaped, and spiral forms. This graphic represents a rod-shaped bacterium, with an elongated, oval-shaped cell body. Unlike animal and plant cells, bacteria do not have a nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane and are therefore classified as prokaryotes. Although bacterial cells have relatively simple structures, they can still carry out essential life processes such as growth, metabolism, and reproduction. Next, we will begin exploring the different structures of the bacterial cell, starting from the outside.

### Cell Wall

Now, keep your finger on the outer edge of the bacterial cell body. Slowly trace this continuous raised boundary, feeling how it surrounds the entire bacterial cell. This boundary represents the cell wall. The cell wall is a structure located outside the cell membrane. Its main functions are to maintain the shape of the bacterium and provide mechanical support and protection. The cell walls of most bacteria contain a substance called peptidoglycan, which helps the cell withstand pressure caused by internal osmotic conditions and reduces the risk of the cell bursting due to excessive water intake. Different types of bacteria have different cell wall structures, and some bacteria do not have cell walls at all. The graphic uses a continuous raised outer boundary to help us understand the position and protective function of the cell wall, but it does not fully represent its actual microscopic structure.

### Cell Membrane

Move your finger slightly inward from the cell wall toward the interior of the bacterial cell. Immediately inside the cell wall, you will feel another relatively smooth boundary. This boundary represents the cell membrane. The cell membrane is a thin membrane surrounding the cytoplasm, composed mainly of a phospholipid bilayer and proteins. It is selectively permeable, allowing it to regulate the movement of different substances into and out of the cell. For example, the cell membrane helps bacteria absorb nutrients needed for growth and release certain metabolic waste products, thereby maintaining a relatively stable internal environment. In addition, the bacterial cell membrane participates in important life processes such as energy conversion. Because bacteria do not have mitochondria, many bacteria use proteins embedded in their cell membranes to carry out certain key processes involved in cellular respiration. The graphic simplifies the cell membrane into a boundary immediately inside the cell wall, allowing us to distinguish these two adjacent layers through touch.

### Cytoplasm

Now, slowly move your finger further inward from the cell membrane toward the interior of the bacterial cell. You will feel a relatively flat, empty base region surrounded by raised structures of different shapes. This base region represents the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm is the region inside the cell membrane that contains the various components of the cell. Its fluid component, known as the cytosol, consists mainly of water, ions, proteins, and other dissolved substances. Many important life processes take place in the cytoplasm of bacterial cells, including certain metabolic reactions and protein synthesis. Unlike animal and plant cells, bacteria generally do not have membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, or the Golgi apparatus. However, they still contain important structures such as ribosomes. Next, we will continue exploring other structures located on the surface of the bacterial cell and within its cytoplasm.

### Flagella

Now, move your finger to the lower-left end of the bacterial cell body. Here, you will encounter three long, thin, thread-like raised structures extending outward from the bacterial cell. Slowly trace one of these structures with your finger, feeling its elongated shape as it extends away from the cell body. These structures represent flagella. Flagella are structures used for movement by certain bacteria and are usually composed of proteins. In many bacteria, flagella rotate using molecular motors located at their bases, propelling the bacteria through liquid environments in a manner similar to a propeller. Flagella can help bacteria move toward environments that are favorable for survival, such as areas with higher concentrations of nutrients, or away from certain harmful substances. Please note that not all bacteria have flagella, and the number and distribution of flagella can vary between bacterial species. This graphic shows three flagella, mainly to help us understand their external shape and role in bacterial movement.

### Pili

Starting from the flagella, slowly move your finger along the outer edge of the bacterial cell body toward the upper right. You will encounter many short, densely distributed, spike-like raised structures. Continue exploring around the outer edge of the bacterial cell, feeling how these small projections are distributed across its surface. These structures represent pili. Pili are small, thread-like structures found on the surfaces of certain bacteria and are usually composed of proteins. Many pili help bacteria attach to surfaces or other cells. For example, some bacteria use pili to attach to human tissues or form biofilms together with other bacteria. In addition, certain specialized types of pili participate in the transfer of genetic material between bacterial cells. Pili are generally shorter and thinner than flagella, and their main functions are also different. Flagella primarily help bacteria move, while many pili mainly help bacteria attach to surfaces. By representing flagella as longer structures and pili as shorter projections, the graphic allows us to distinguish these two types of structures through touch.

### Nucleoid

Now, move your finger to the central region of the bacterial cell body. Here, you will encounter a cluster of intertwined, curved, rope-like raised structures. Slowly trace these structures with your finger, feeling how they cross over and coil around one another. This region represents the nucleoid. The nucleoid is the region within a bacterial cell where most of its genetic material is concentrated. It usually contains a bacterial chromosome consisting of a single circular, double-stranded DNA molecule. Unlike the nuclei found in animal and plant cells, the nucleoid is not surrounded by a nuclear membrane. Therefore, bacteria do not have a true membrane-bound nucleus. The bacterial chromosome carries important genetic information that controls the cell’s life processes. Through gene expression, this genetic information influences bacterial growth, metabolism, reproduction, and other activities. Please note that the nucleoid is not a separate membrane-bound organelle but rather a region where DNA is relatively concentrated. The graphic uses intertwined, rope-like raised structures to help us understand the distribution of genetic material, although the actual structure and arrangement of bacterial DNA are more complex than those shown in the graphic.

### Plasmid

Now, slowly move your finger from the nucleoid toward the lower-right region of the bacterial cell, approaching the inner side of the cell wall. Here, you will encounter a small, closed, ring-shaped raised structure. Slowly trace this ring with your finger, feeling its curved shape and how its ends connect to form a complete loop. This structure represents a plasmid. Plasmids are usually small DNA molecules that exist independently of the bacterial chromosome, many of which have a circular, double-stranded structure. Plasmids can carry additional genetic information, such as certain antibiotic resistance genes, which may help bacteria survive in particular environments. Some plasmids can be transferred between bacterial cells, allowing different bacteria to acquire new genetic characteristics. In addition, plasmids are important tools commonly used in genetic engineering and synthetic biology. Scientists can use specially designed plasmids to introduce specific genes into bacteria, enabling them to produce target proteins or perform other functions. Please note that not all bacteria contain plasmids, and the number and shapes of plasmids may vary. The graphic uses a single small, closed ring to help us distinguish the plasmid from the main genetic material located in the nucleoid.

### Ribosomes

Finally, move your finger into the interior of the bacterial cell body and slowly explore the cytoplasm surrounding the nucleoid and plasmid. You will feel many small, round, grain-like raised structures scattered throughout this region. These structures represent ribosomes. Ribosomes are important cellular structures responsible for protein synthesis. They are composed of ribosomal RNA and proteins. Ribosomes read the genetic information carried by messenger RNA and link amino acids together in the corresponding sequence to form proteins. Bacteria continuously synthesize different proteins through their ribosomes to support essential life processes such as growth, metabolism, and reproduction. Unlike many other cellular structures, ribosomes are not surrounded by membranes. Bacterial ribosomes are typically classified as 70S ribosomes, which differ from the 80S ribosomes found in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. The graphic represents ribosomes as small particles scattered throughout the cytoplasm, helping us understand their distribution and function. In reality, ribosomes are extremely small and cannot be directly observed with the naked eye.

You have now learned about the major structures of a bacterial cell and how they work together to support essential life processes such as growth, metabolism, movement, and reproduction.
