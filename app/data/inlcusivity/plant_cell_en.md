Introduction
Welcome to the exploration of a plant cell. Before we begin, let us first establish the orientation of the model. Move both hands along the surface of the model and locate the large arrow sign in the corner. Adjust the model so that the tip of the arrow points directly upward. Once adjusted, the arrow will be located at the lower-left corner of the model, with its tip pointing upward. Keep the model in this position throughout the exploration. Following this orientation, we will begin with the external structures of the plant cell and gradually explore its internal structures.
1.Overall Cell Outline
Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands directly upward. During this movement, you will first feel a horizontal dividing line. After crossing this line, you will enter the plant cell model area. Spread both hands apart until you reach the outermost edge of the plant cell. Then, slowly move both hands along this edge. You will feel an approximately rectangular outline. The entire area enclosed by this outline is a plant cell. Plant cells usually have relatively regular shapes because external supporting structures help maintain their stable form.
2.Cell Wall
Move your finger along the outermost edge of the outline. Starting from the lower-left corner, move clockwise around the cell once. The outermost continuous boundary you feel is the plant cell wall. The cell wall is the outermost layer of a plant cell and is mainly composed of substances such as cellulose. It provides support, maintains the cell’s regular shape, and prevents the cell from bursting when it takes in too much water. Note: Animal cells do not have a cell wall.
3.Cell Membrane
Now, keep your finger at the lower-left corner of the cell wall you just explored. Move inward slightly from the cell wall. You will feel a layer of cell membrane closely attached to the inner side of the cell wall. It acts as a selective barrier that controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell, allowing the cell to maintain a stable internal environment. Both plant and animal cells have a cell membrane.
4.Cytoplasm
From the cell membrane, move slightly toward the inside of the cell. You will feel a relatively flat area inside the cell—the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm is a fluid-like substance in which various organelles are distributed. Organelles are small structures inside cells with specific functions. Next, we will explore various organelles as well as structures such as the cytoskeleton. The cytoplasm supports these organelles and helps these structures transport substances within the cell. Many important chemical reactions, such as anaerobic respiration, also occur in the cytoplasm.
5.Granule
Keep your finger at the lower-left area of the model, then slowly move it toward the upper right until you feel a small spherical structure. You will find a thin, curved, tube-like filament passing through it. This is a granule. Granules can store specific active substances and, under signal regulation, release their contents through exocytosis, thereby participating in cellular physiological responses.
6.Amyloplast
Now, slowly move your finger directly upward from the granule until you feel a nearly ellipsoidal structure. Its outer surface has a smooth double-membrane outline, while its interior has a relatively full matrix region. This is an amyloplast. It contains starch granules and synthesizes and stores starch as an energy reserve. It also participates in gravity sensing and helps regulate the gravitropic growth of roots.
7.Mitochondrion
Next, move your finger directly downward from the amyloplast back to the previous granule. From the granule, slowly move your finger toward the upper right until you feel a relatively flat, oval structure. Inside it, you can feel several short, strip-like protrusions extending inward from the edge of the structure. This structure is a mitochondrion. Mitochondria are the main sites of aerobic respiration in cells. During aerobic respiration, cells use oxygen to break down glucose and other organic substances, releasing the energy stored in them. Some of this energy is used to produce ATP molecules. ATP acts as the cell’s energy “currency,” providing energy for various life processes. The short, strip-like protrusions inside the mitochondrion represent cristae formed by the inner membrane. Cristae increase the surface area of the inner membrane, thereby improving the efficiency of ATP production during aerobic respiration. The model contains three mitochondria in total. The other two printed models have the same structure and can be explored freely after the guided tour.
8.Microtubule
Next, move your finger directly below the mitochondrion until you feel a cylindrical structure that is attached to the cell membrane and positioned at an angle. This structure represents a microtubule. It helps maintain cell shape, mediates intracellular transport, and participates in cell division and cell movement. Note that microtubules are not organelles; they are protein fibers of the cytoskeleton.
9.Intermediate Filament
Next, move your finger directly to the right of the microtubule until you feel a continuous, thin, filament-like structure. This is an intermediate filament. Like microtubules, it is not an organelle but another type of cytoskeletal fiber. It can strengthen the cell against stretching, anchor the nucleus and organelles, and help maintain the mechanical stability of the cell. There are four additional intermediate filaments in this cell model. Although their lengths and curves are not exactly the same, their thicknesses are consistent. They can be explored freely after the guided tour.
10.Chloroplast
From the intermediate filament, slowly move your finger directly upward. You will feel a relatively large, flat, oval structure with multiple groups of parallel, short, strip-like protrusions inside. This oval structure is a chloroplast. Chloroplasts are organelles capable of photosynthesis and are commonly found in plant cells and some photosynthetic protists. The short, strip-like structures inside represent grana formed by stacks of thylakoids in the chloroplast. The membranes of these thylakoids contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy. This light energy drives photosynthesis, converting carbon dioxide and water into glucose and releasing oxygen. The glucose produced can serve both as an energy source and as a raw material for synthesizing other organic substances. The model contains three chloroplasts in total. They have identical printed structures and can be explored freely after the guided tour.
11.Peroxisome
Now, move toward the upper left of the chloroplast until you feel a small, separate, circular structure. Its edge is curved, with the center slightly lower than the surrounding area. This is a peroxisome. It is mainly responsible for breaking down harmful substances in the cell and participates in the metabolism of fatty acids and other substances. The model contains two peroxisomes in total. They have identical printed structures and can be explored freely after the guided tour.
12.Large Vacuole
Now, move toward the upper left of the peroxisome. You will feel a large flat area enclosed by a continuous curved boundary. You can move your finger clockwise along its curved boundary to explore its irregular but smooth shape. This structure is the large vacuole of the plant cell. The vacuole stores large amounts of water, minerals, and other dissolved substances. When the vacuole takes in water and expands, it exerts outward pressure on the surrounding cytoplasm and cell wall. This pressure is called turgor pressure. Turgor pressure helps plant cells remain firm and helps the stems and leaves of plants stay upright. When a plant lacks water, the vacuole decreases in volume and turgor pressure decreases. The cell loses support, causing the plant to wilt.
13.Endoplasmic Reticulum
Next, locate the middle position of the large vacuole in the vertical direction. From here, move directly to the right until you feel a continuous, curved, network-like structure. You will feel many curved, membrane-like structures connected to one another, forming a continuous membrane network. This is the endoplasmic reticulum. It is divided into rough endoplasmic reticulum and smooth endoplasmic reticulum. Due to current limitations in model production and printing technology, their tactile differences cannot yet be represented. However, the main difference between rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum is that ribosomes are attached to the surface of rough endoplasmic reticulum, while smooth endoplasmic reticulum does not have them. Ribosomes are responsible for producing proteins, while the rough endoplasmic reticulum helps process these newly synthesized proteins and transports them to different locations within the cell. The smooth endoplasmic reticulum participates in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium ion storage.
14.Nucleus
Move toward the upper right, passing through a large area of the endoplasmic reticulum. You will feel a large circular structure. Move along its edge and you will feel a circular boundary with an outward protrusion. The area enclosed by this boundary represents the nucleus. The nucleus stores genetic information in DNA and is an important control center of the cell. It regulates gene expression and controls the growth, development, and various life processes of the cell. The outer boundary of the nucleus is called the nuclear membrane. It separates the genetic material from the cytoplasm while allowing specific substances to enter and leave the nucleus. Keep your finger inside the nucleus and continue exploring toward the center. You will feel a smaller, relatively smooth, circular protrusion. This is the nucleolus, which is mainly responsible for producing the components of ribosomes.
15.Golgi Apparatus
Finally, from the nucleus, move directly downward, passing through the endoplasmic reticulum on the other side, until you feel another group of curved, network-like structures. This is the Golgi apparatus. To distinguish it from the endoplasmic reticulum through touch, the middle of its structures has no indentation, while the structures of the endoplasmic reticulum have slight indentations in the middle. The Golgi apparatus acts like a logistics center inside the cell. It processes, sorts, and packages substances produced by the cell, which are then transported by vesicles to appropriate locations inside or outside the cell. As you explore around the Golgi apparatus, you will feel four smaller circular structures. These represent Golgi vesicles.
