Opening: Welcome to the guided exploration of the human male karyotype tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. This graphic represents a typical human male karyotype, consisting of 46 chromosomes arranged into 23 pairs. Chromosomes 1 through 22 form 22 pairs of autosomes, while the final pair consists of the XY sex chromosomes. The two chromosomes above each numbered label form a chromosome pair. For chromosomes 1 through 22, each pair consists of homologous chromosomes. Next, we will follow this orientation to explore the human male karyotype step by step.

## 1. First Row: Chromosomes 1–5

Move both hands to the first row at the very top of the graphic. Starting from the far left, you will encounter the first pair of chromosomes. The Braille label beneath this pair represents the number 1, identifying chromosome 1. Chromosome 1 is the largest chromosome in humans. Slowly move your fingers toward the right along this row, feeling the Braille labels numbered 2, 3, 4, and 5, along with their corresponding chromosomes above. These represent chromosomes 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. These five pairs are generally among the larger autosomes. Try comparing the lengths and shapes of the different chromosomes, noticing the differences between them.

## 2. Second Row: Chromosomes 6–12

Now, move both hands straight downward to the second row of chromosomes. Starting from the far left of this row, you will find the Braille label numbered 6, along with the corresponding chromosome 6 above it. Continue moving toward the right along this row, exploring chromosomes 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 in order. Compared with the first row, the chromosomes in this group are generally slightly smaller. You can try using both hands to feel chromosomes in the first and second rows simultaneously, comparing their lengths and shapes.

## 3. Third Row: Chromosomes 13–18

Continue moving straight downward to reach the third row of chromosomes. Starting from the far left, you will encounter the Braille label numbered 13, along with the corresponding chromosome 13 above it. Slowly move your fingers toward the right along this row, exploring chromosomes 14, 15, 16, 17, and 18 in order. The chromosomes in this group are generally smaller than those in the first two rows. However, please note that chromosome numbers are not assigned strictly according to their lengths from largest to smallest. In addition to differences in length, chromosomes may also vary in the position of their centromeres and their overall shapes.

## 4. Fourth Row: Chromosomes 19–22 and the XY Sex Chromosomes

Now, move both hands to the bottom row of chromosomes. Starting from the far left, you will encounter the Braille label numbered 19, along with the corresponding chromosome 19 above it. Slowly move your fingers toward the right along this row, exploring chromosomes 20, 21, and 22 in order. Among these, chromosome 21 is one of the smallest autosomes in humans. Due to slight variations that may occur during the production of the graphic, you may not be able to clearly distinguish its size from those of neighboring chromosomes through touch. Chromosomes 1 through 22 are collectively known as autosomes. Next, continue moving your finger toward the right from chromosome 22. Here, you will encounter the 23rd pair of chromosomes, with the Braille label XY beneath them. These are the XY sex chromosomes. Feel the X chromosome and Y chromosome separately, noticing the clear difference in their lengths. In a typical human karyotype, males usually have XY sex chromosomes, while females usually have XX sex chromosomes. If you have access to a female karyotype tactile graphic, you can try comparing the two through touch. The 23rd chromosome pair in females typically consists of two X chromosomes of similar length, while the 23rd pair in males consists of a longer X chromosome and a shorter Y chromosome. Sex chromosomes not only play a role in determining biological sex but also carry many genes associated with other human characteristics and essential life processes.

## 5. Chromosome Structure

Finally, choose any pair of X-shaped chromosomes and slowly trace the overall outline of one chromosome with your fingertip. You will notice a region near the middle where the chromosome becomes narrower. This region represents the centromere. The centromere divides the chromosome into two arms, known as the short arm and the long arm. Continue slowly tracing both sides of the chromosome, feeling their lengths and shapes. Next, explore the other chromosome with the same numbered label. For autosomes numbered 1 through 22, you will notice that the two chromosomes within each pair generally have similar overall shapes. They are known as homologous chromosomes and together form a chromosome pair. Homologous chromosomes usually have the same genes at corresponding locations, although the specific versions of these genes may differ. In contrast, the X and Y sex chromosomes in the 23rd pair differ significantly in size and shape and are not considered a fully homologous pair in the usual sense. Chromosomes are mainly composed of DNA and proteins, with DNA carrying the genetic information of the human body. Please note that the X-shaped chromosomes shown in the graphic represent chromosomes after DNA replication, when each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids. This does not mean that chromosomes always maintain an X shape inside cells. Through the raised outlines of the chromosomes, the graphic helps us understand their basic shapes, paired arrangement, and role in storing genetic information.

You have now learned how the 46 human chromosomes are arranged into 23 pairs, as well as the characteristics of autosomes, sex chromosomes, and basic chromosome structures.
