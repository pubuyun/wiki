### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the human heart tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. To help you distinguish arteries from veins through touch, we have designed a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges along the surfaces of the veins, while the surfaces of the arteries remain smooth. Please note that these textures are intended only to help identify different blood vessels in the graphic and do not represent the actual surface characteristics of blood vessels. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall structure of the heart, superior and inferior vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries, pulmonary veins, left atrium, mitral valve, left ventricle, aorta, and interventricular septum.

### 1. Overall Structure of the Heart

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line until you reach a large structure in the lower part of the graphic that gradually narrows toward the bottom. Slowly trace the outer outline of this structure with your fingers. You will notice that the lower part of the heart is relatively rounded and gradually tapers downward to form a pointed tip, while the upper part is wider and connects to several tube-like structures extending in different directions. This entire structure represents the human heart. The heart is a hollow organ composed mainly of cardiac muscle. It is located inside the chest cavity, between the lungs, and slightly toward the left side of the body. The heart consists of four main chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, and left ventricle. From the current viewing direction of the graphic, the two main chambers on the left represent the right atrium and right ventricle, while the two chambers on the right represent the left atrium and left ventricle. This is because the graphic shows the heart as viewed from the front of the human body. The heart’s main function is to continuously circulate blood throughout the body through regular contraction and relaxation. It pumps blood with a relatively low oxygen content to the lungs for gas exchange, while also delivering oxygen-rich blood to tissues throughout the body, supplying cells with oxygen and nutrients. Next, we will explore these structures step by step, following the direction of blood flow.

### 2. Superior and Inferior Vena Cava

Now, move both hands to the upper half of the graphic and locate a relatively thick, tube-like structure extending upward, slightly to the left of the center. Place your finger at the top of this tube and slowly trace downward along its surface. You will notice that it extends downward and connects to a relatively large chamber in the upper-left region of the heart. This tube represents the superior vena cava. Gently feel the surface of this vessel with your finger. You will notice a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges. These are the special tactile markings we have designed for veins, allowing you to distinguish them from arteries, which have smooth surfaces in the graphic. The superior vena cava collects venous blood from the head, neck, upper limbs, and parts of the chest, carrying it into the right atrium. This blood has already delivered some of its oxygen to body tissues, so it contains less oxygen than blood returning from the lungs. Now, move your finger to the lower-left region of the graphic, near the outer edge of the heart. You will encounter another relatively thick, tube-like structure extending upward from the bottom of the graphic and connecting near the same atrium. This tube represents the inferior vena cava. Like the superior vena cava, the inferior vena cava also has a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges along its surface. The inferior vena cava collects venous blood from regions including the abdomen, pelvis, and lower limbs, carrying it into the right atrium. Although the superior and inferior vena cava collect blood from different parts of the body, they serve the same purpose: returning blood from the systemic circulation to the heart. In the graphic, both venae cavae have been simplified into relatively thick tubes. In reality, the venae cavae are elastic blood vessels with hollow spaces through which blood flows.

### 3. Right Atrium

Starting from the lower end of the superior vena cava, continue moving your finger downward along the vessel. You will encounter a chamber region that is wider than the tube above it. This region is located slightly to the left of the center of the graphic. It has a relatively rounded outline and is surrounded by a distinct boundary. Slowly trace its edge to explore how this chamber connects to the blood vessel above it. This structure represents the right atrium. The right atrium is one of the heart’s four chambers. Its main function is to receive venous blood from the superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, and coronary sinus. After blood returns to the heart through the veins from different parts of the body, it first collects in the right atrium. Then, during the heart’s cycles of relaxation and contraction, the blood passes through an opening below the right atrium and enters the right ventricle. Therefore, the right atrium primarily receives blood and transfers it to the right ventricle. The right atrium does not pump blood directly to the lungs. Instead, it first sends blood into the right ventricle, which then pumps it toward the lungs.

### 4. Tricuspid Valve

Now, place your finger along the lower edge of the right atrium and move downward to locate the connection between the right atrium and right ventricle. This region represents the passage between the two chambers. In the actual heart, an important structure called the tricuspid valve is located here. The tricuspid valve lies between the right atrium and right ventricle and consists of three leaflets. When the right ventricle relaxes and blood flows from the right atrium into the right ventricle, the tricuspid valve opens, allowing blood to pass through. When the right ventricle contracts, the tricuspid valve closes, preventing blood from flowing backward into the right atrium. Therefore, the tricuspid valve helps ensure that blood flows in the correct direction. Heart valves do not actively pump blood. Instead, they open and close passively in response to changes in pressure on either side of the valve. The graphic mainly shows the connection between the right atrium and right ventricle without fully reproducing the three individual leaflets of the tricuspid valve. We will therefore understand its function mainly through its position between the two chambers.

### 5. Right Ventricle

Continue moving your finger downward from the right atrium, crossing the boundary between the two chambers. You will enter a larger chamber region. This area is located in the lower-left part of the graphic, extending downward toward the bottom of the heart. Slowly explore the outer boundary of this chamber to understand its position relative to the right atrium above it. This structure represents the right ventricle. The right ventricle is one of the heart’s four chambers. Its main function is to pump blood with a relatively low oxygen content to the lungs. After blood enters the right ventricle from the right atrium, the cardiac muscle of the right ventricle contracts, increasing the pressure inside the chamber and pushing blood into the pulmonary trunk. The blood then travels through the pulmonary arteries toward the left and right lungs. In the lungs, blood flows through capillaries surrounding the alveoli, where it releases carbon dioxide and takes up oxygen. The muscular wall of the right ventricle is thinner than that of the left ventricle because it only needs to pump blood to the lungs, rather than generating the high pressure required to circulate blood throughout the entire body. Although the right and left ventricles perform similar pumping functions, they are responsible for different circulatory pathways.

### 6. Pulmonary Trunk and Pulmonary Arteries

Now, slowly move your finger from the upper part of the right ventricle toward the center of the graphic. You will find a relatively thick, tube-like structure extending upward from the heart. Trace this tube upward with your finger. You will notice that it gradually curves toward the upper-right region of the graphic and connects to branches extending in different directions. This main vessel leaving the right ventricle represents the pulmonary trunk, which extends upward before dividing into the left and right pulmonary arteries. The main function of the pulmonary arteries is to transport blood with a relatively low oxygen content from the right ventricle to the lungs. This differs from the common assumption that arteries always carry oxygen-rich blood. In fact, arteries and veins are classified according to the direction of blood flow, rather than the oxygen content of the blood. Blood vessels that carry blood away from the heart are called arteries, while those that return blood to the heart are called veins. Therefore, although the pulmonary arteries carry blood with a relatively low oxygen content, they are still classified as arteries. You can feel the surface of this vessel with your finger. Unlike the veins, it does not have raised, ring-shaped ridges and instead has a relatively smooth surface. Between the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk is another structure called the pulmonary valve. It opens when the right ventricle contracts, allowing blood to enter the pulmonary trunk, and closes when the right ventricle relaxes, preventing blood from flowing backward. The graphic mainly uses tube-like structures to represent the pulmonary trunk and its branches without fully showing the internal leaflets of the pulmonary valve.

### 7. Pulmonary Veins

Now, move your finger to the upper-right region of the graphic. Here, you will encounter several relatively short, tube-like structures extending from the side of the graphic toward the interior of the heart. Starting from the outer end of one of these tubes, slowly trace it toward the heart. You will notice that these branches connect to an atrial region in the upper-right part of the graphic. These structures represent the pulmonary veins. Slowly move your finger along the surface of one pulmonary vein. Once again, you will feel a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges. These are the same tactile features you encountered on the superior and inferior vena cava. The pulmonary veins return blood to the heart after gas exchange has taken place in the lungs. When blood flows through the capillaries surrounding the alveoli, oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli. After gas exchange, the oxygen content of the blood increases, and the blood returns to the left atrium through the pulmonary veins. A typical human heart has four pulmonary veins, which carry blood from the left and right lungs into the left atrium. Unlike the pulmonary arteries, the pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood. Therefore, in pulmonary circulation, the pulmonary arteries transport blood with a relatively low oxygen content to the lungs, while the pulmonary veins return oxygen-rich blood to the heart.

### 8. Left Atrium

Continue moving your finger inward along the pulmonary veins you just located. In the upper-middle region, slightly to the right of the center of the graphic, you will encounter a relatively small chamber with a rounded outline. This chamber is located above the left ventricle and connects to the pulmonary veins. Slowly trace its edge to explore its position relative to the surrounding blood vessels and the chamber below it. This structure represents the left atrium. The left atrium is one of the heart’s four chambers. Its main function is to receive oxygen-rich blood from the pulmonary veins. After blood enters the left atrium through the pulmonary veins, it moves into the left ventricle during the heart’s cycles of relaxation and contraction. Therefore, the left atrium primarily receives blood returning from the lungs and transfers it to the left ventricle. Compared with the right atrium, the left atrium receives blood that has undergone gas exchange in the lungs and therefore usually contains a higher concentration of oxygen.

### 9. Mitral Valve

Now, place your finger below the left atrium and locate the connection between it and the larger chamber underneath. This region represents the passage between the left atrium and left ventricle. In the actual human heart, a structure called the mitral valve, also known as the bicuspid valve, is located here. The mitral valve consists of two leaflets and mainly controls blood flow from the left atrium into the left ventricle. When the left ventricle relaxes, the mitral valve opens, allowing blood to flow from the left atrium into the left ventricle. When the left ventricle contracts, the mitral valve closes, preventing blood from flowing backward into the left atrium. Therefore, the mitral valve serves a function similar to that of the tricuspid valve introduced earlier. Both are important structures that ensure blood flows in one direction. Their main differences are their locations and the number of leaflets they contain. The tricuspid valve is located between the right atrium and right ventricle, while the mitral valve is located between the left atrium and left ventricle. The graphic does not fully reproduce the two individual leaflets of the mitral valve. Therefore, we will understand this structure mainly through the connection between the left atrium and left ventricle.

### 10. Left Ventricle

Continue moving your finger downward from the left atrium, crossing the boundary between the two chambers. You will encounter a relatively large chamber in the lower-right region of the graphic. It extends downward and forms an important part of the lower portion of the heart. Slowly trace the outer boundary of this chamber and feel the division between it and the adjacent right ventricle. This structure represents the left ventricle. The left ventricle is one of the heart’s four chambers and is responsible for pumping blood throughout the body. After oxygen-rich blood enters the left ventricle from the left atrium, the left ventricle contracts forcefully, pumping the blood into the aorta. The blood then travels through the aorta and its branches to reach different organs and tissues throughout the body. Because the left ventricle must generate enough pressure to push blood to all parts of the body, its muscular wall is significantly thicker than that of the right ventricle. This characteristic demonstrates the relationship between the structure and function of the heart. The right ventricle primarily pumps blood to the lungs, while the left ventricle pumps blood throughout the body. The graphic helps us understand the differences between the two ventricles through their shapes and positions, although the muscular structures of the actual ventricles are more complex than those represented in the graphic.

### 11. Aorta

Now, slowly move your finger from the upper part of the left ventricle toward the center of the graphic. In the upper half of the graphic, you will encounter a relatively thick, tube-like structure that extends upward before forming a distinct curve. Slowly trace this curved vessel with your finger. You will notice that it forms an arch and gives rise to three tube-like branches extending upward from its uppermost region. This thick blood vessel represents the aorta. Like the pulmonary arteries explored earlier, the aorta has a relatively smooth surface, without the raised, ring-shaped ridges used to identify veins. The aorta is the largest artery in the human body and is responsible for transporting oxygen-rich blood pumped from the left ventricle to the rest of the body. When the left ventricle contracts, blood passes through the aortic valve and enters the aorta. The aortic valve is located between the left ventricle and the aorta and prevents blood from flowing backward from the aorta into the left ventricle. After entering the aorta, blood travels through different branches to supply the head, neck, upper limbs, and other parts of the body. The three branches at the top of the graphic represent the major branches arising from the aortic arch, which supply blood to regions including the head, neck, and upper limbs. Although both the aorta and pulmonary arteries are major blood vessels that carry blood away from the heart, they serve different functions. The pulmonary arteries transport blood with a relatively low oxygen content from the right ventricle to the lungs, while the aorta transports oxygen-rich blood from the left ventricle to the rest of the body. The aorta in the graphic has been simplified to emphasize its distinct curved structure and major upper branches.

### 12. Interventricular Septum

Finally, slowly move your finger downward from the aorta, returning to the region between the two ventricles. In the lower half of the graphic, you will feel a distinct dividing structure extending downward between the left and right ventricles. Slowly trace this structure to explore how it separates the two chambers. This structure represents the interventricular septum. The interventricular septum is a muscular wall located between the left and right ventricles. It separates the two ventricles and prevents blood from directly mixing between them under normal conditions. This is important for maintaining normal blood circulation. The right ventricle pumps blood with a relatively low oxygen content to the lungs, while the left ventricle pumps oxygen-rich blood throughout the body. Without a normal dividing structure between the two ventricles, blood with different oxygen levels could mix directly, reducing the efficiency of the circulatory system. In addition to the interventricular septum, the heart also contains an interatrial septum between the two atria, which separates the left and right atria. The graphic mainly shows the relative positions of the four chambers and the division between the ventricles. In reality, the interior of the heart also contains more complex structures, including cardiac muscle, valves, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscles.
