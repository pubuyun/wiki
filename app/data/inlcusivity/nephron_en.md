### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the nephron tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. To help you distinguish arteries from veins through touch, we have designed a series of raised, ring-shaped ridges along the surfaces of the veins, while the surfaces of the arteries remain smooth. Please note that these textures are intended only to help identify different blood vessels in the graphic and do not represent the actual surface characteristics of blood vessels. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall structure of the nephron, renal corpuscle, the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, and distal convoluted tubule within the renal tubule, and the collecting duct. We will also learn how urine is formed.

### 1. Overall Structure of the Nephron

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the nephron region of the graphic. Slowly move both hands outward to explore the raised structures within the graphic. You will encounter many tube-like structures of different thicknesses that cross over one another. Some curve and coil repeatedly, while others extend vertically. Overall, these structures feel like a tangled network of tubes. Together, they represent the nephron and some of its surrounding blood vessels. The nephron is the basic structural and functional unit of the kidney responsible for urine formation. It consists of two main parts: the renal corpuscle and the renal tubule. The renal corpuscle filters blood to form glomerular filtrate, while the renal tubule regulates the water, ions, and other substances in the filtrate through processes such as reabsorption and secretion. The graphic also shows the collecting duct connected to the nephron, as well as surrounding blood vessels involved in substance exchange. Although the collecting duct is generally not considered part of the nephron itself, it plays an important role in the final formation and transport of urine. Next, we will follow the tubes in the graphic to explore these structures step by step and understand how urine is formed.

### 2. Renal Corpuscle

Now, move your finger to the upper-left region of the graphic. Here, you will encounter two vertical tube-like structures extending straight downward, positioned close to each other. The tube on the left represents the venous return pathway, while the tube on the right represents the arterial blood supply pathway. These two tubes are simplified representations of blood circulation around the nephron and do not represent the complete renal vein and renal artery. Place your finger on the right tube, which represents the arterial blood supply, and slowly trace downward along it. As you move, you will encounter a branch extending toward the right. Follow this branch until you reach a circular structure with a depression in its center. This structure represents the glomerulus. The glomerulus is a ball-shaped network consisting of numerous capillaries. Now, slowly trace around the outer edge of the glomerulus. You will notice another raised, curved, tube-like structure surrounding it. This surrounding structure represents Bowman’s capsule. Together, the glomerulus and Bowman’s capsule form the renal corpuscle. The main function of the renal corpuscle is to filter blood. When blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole, blood pressure within the glomerular capillaries pushes some components of the blood plasma through the filtration barrier and into the space inside Bowman’s capsule, forming glomerular filtrate. The substances filtered include small molecules such as water, mineral ions, glucose, and urea. However, blood cells and most plasma proteins normally cannot pass through the filtration barrier and therefore remain in the blood. The filtered blood then leaves the glomerulus through the efferent arteriole. Please note that glomerular filtrate is not the final urine eliminated from the body. It still contains many substances that the body can reuse. These substances will undergo further processing in the renal tubule.

### 3. Renal Tubule

Now, slowly move your finger toward the right from Bowman’s capsule. You will encounter a horizontally extending tube connected to the capsule. This tube is slightly thicker than some of the smaller blood vessels surrounding it and represents the renal tubule. Slowly trace this tube with your finger. You will notice that it curves repeatedly, extends downward, and then bends back upward. Meanwhile, many smaller, intertwined tube-like structures surround the renal tubule. These represent the surrounding capillaries. The renal tubule is an important part of the nephron and is mainly responsible for further processing the glomerular filtrate formed by the renal corpuscle. Through reabsorption, it returns useful substances such as water, glucose, and certain mineral ions to the blood. Through secretion, it also transfers certain substances from the blood into the renal tubule. The renal tubule is mainly divided into three parts: the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, and distal convoluted tubule. Next, we will explore these three sections in order, following the direction in which the filtrate flows.

### 3.1 Proximal Convoluted Tubule

Place your finger on the tube connected to the right side of Bowman’s capsule and slowly trace it toward the upper right. You will feel the tube repeatedly curve and bend back on itself, forming several continuous turns. Try to keep your finger on the same continuous tube and avoid moving onto the smaller blood vessels that cross around it. As you continue following the curved tube, you will eventually find a relatively straight section extending downward. This indicates that you are approaching the end of the proximal convoluted tubule. This repeatedly curved section represents the proximal convoluted tubule. The proximal convoluted tubule is the first part of the renal tubule and an important site where large amounts of substances are reabsorbed from the glomerular filtrate. When filtrate enters the proximal convoluted tubule, cells lining its walls reabsorb useful substances through processes such as active transport, diffusion, and osmosis, transferring them into the surrounding capillaries. Under normal conditions, almost all the glucose, most of the water, and large amounts of mineral ions in the filtrate are reabsorbed here. In addition, the proximal convoluted tubule can secrete certain metabolic waste products and other substances into its lumen. Therefore, the proximal convoluted tubule not only helps the body retain useful substances but also participates in regulating the composition of the filtrate.

### 3.2 Loop of Henle

Now, continue moving your finger straight downward along the tube at the end of the proximal convoluted tubule. You will notice that the previously curved tube gradually becomes relatively straight and extends downward. Continue tracing this tube until you encounter a distinct bend. You will find that the tube forms a curve resembling the letter U before extending upward again. This section represents the loop of Henle. Notice that as the loop of Henle extends downward, it crosses a horizontal structure in the middle of the graphic and enters the renal medulla region below. It then forms a U-shaped bend in the lower region before extending upward and returning toward the upper region. The loop of Henle mainly helps the kidneys establish and maintain a concentration gradient, allowing them to regulate the concentration of urine. The descending limb of the loop of Henle is highly permeable to water, allowing water to leave the renal tubule through osmosis and enter the surrounding interstitial fluid. In contrast, the ascending limb has very low permeability to water and mainly helps establish the concentration gradient in the renal medulla through salt reabsorption. This concentration gradient is essential for regulating water reabsorption in the kidneys, allowing the body to produce urine of different concentrations according to its physiological needs. The graphic uses a distinct U-shaped bend to help us recognize the shape and position of the loop of Henle.

### 3.3 Distal Convoluted Tubule

Now, continue moving your finger straight upward along the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. When the tube returns to the upper region of the graphic, you will notice that it gradually changes from a relatively straight shape into a curved structure, forming several irregular bends. This curved section represents the distal convoluted tubule. The distal convoluted tubule is the final part of the renal tubule and is mainly responsible for further regulating the composition of the fluid inside its lumen. Through selective reabsorption and secretion, it regulates the amounts of substances such as sodium ions, potassium ions, and hydrogen ions, helping the body maintain electrolyte balance and acid-base balance. In addition, certain regions of the distal convoluted tubule can adjust the reabsorption of water and salts under hormonal regulation. By the time the filtrate passes through the proximal convoluted tubule, large amounts of useful substances and water have already been reabsorbed. As it passes through the loop of Henle, the movement of water and salts further helps establish a concentration gradient. In the distal convoluted tubule, the ionic composition and some of the water content of the fluid undergo more precise regulation. Therefore, although the different sections of the renal tubule are connected, they perform distinct functions that collectively contribute to urine formation. After passing through the distal convoluted tubule, the fluid enters the collecting duct for further regulation.

### 4. Collecting Duct

Finally, continue tracing the distal convoluted tubule with your finger. You will notice that this repeatedly curved tube eventually connects to a relatively thick, straight tube on the right side of the graphic. Slowly move your finger straight downward along this tube, feeling how it extends continuously toward the bottom. This tube represents the collecting duct. The collecting duct receives fluid from the distal convoluted tubule and participates in the final stages of urine formation. Water reabsorption in the collecting duct is regulated by factors such as antidiuretic hormone, also known as ADH. When the body needs to conserve more water, ADH increases the water permeability of the collecting duct, allowing more water to be reabsorbed into the blood. This results in a smaller volume of more concentrated urine. When the body has sufficient water and ADH levels are relatively low, less water is reabsorbed in the collecting duct, and the urine produced is generally more dilute. In addition, the collecting duct participates in the transport of certain ions and the regulation of acid-base balance. After these processes, the final urine flows downward through the collecting ducts and enters the minor calyces through openings at the renal papillae. It then passes through the major calyces and renal pelvis before traveling through the ureter to the urinary bladder. The graphic represents the collecting duct as a relatively thick tube extending downward on the right side, helping us understand its role in urine formation and transport.
