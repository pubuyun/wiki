### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the neuron tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall outline of the neuron, dendrites, cell body, nucleus, Nissl bodies, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, and axon terminals.

### 1. Overall Outline of the Neuron

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the neuron region of the graphic. Slowly move both hands outward to explore the raised structures within the graphic. You will encounter a relatively large, irregularly rounded region in the upper-left area, with many thin branches extending from it in different directions. From this larger rounded region, a long, thin structure extends toward the lower right, reaching the lower-right area of the graphic, where it divides into several smaller branches. Together, these structures represent the neuron shown in the graphic. Neurons are specialized cells in the nervous system responsible for receiving, integrating, and transmitting information. They mainly consist of a cell body, dendrites, and an axon. Dendrites primarily receive signals, the cell body maintains the cell’s essential functions and integrates information, and the axon transmits nerve impulses to other cells. Next, we will begin with the branches in the upper-left region of the neuron and gradually explore its different structures.

### 2. Dendrites

Now, move your finger to the area surrounding the large, rounded region in the upper-left part of the neuron. Slowly explore outward from the edge of this region. You will encounter many thin branches extending in different directions. Slowly trace one of these branches with your finger, feeling how it extends outward. Then, continue exploring the other branches, noticing how they spread in different directions from the same main region. These structures represent dendrites. Dendrites are important structures responsible for receiving information in neurons. They usually extend outward from the cell body and form multiple branches. This branching structure increases the opportunities for neurons to make contact with other cells, allowing them to receive signals from multiple sources. Dendrites mainly receive chemical signals from other neurons through synapses and transmit the resulting electrical signals toward the cell body. Please note that the number, length, and branching patterns of dendrites can vary among different types of neurons. The graphic uses multiple thin, outward-extending branches to help us understand the shape of dendrites and their role in receiving information.

### 3. Cell Body

Now, slowly move your finger inward from the dendritic branches toward the center. As your finger approaches the points where these branches originate, you will encounter a relatively wide, roughly rounded main region with an irregular edge. This region represents the cell body of the neuron, also known as the soma. The cell body is the main metabolic center of the neuron and contains the nucleus and various organelles needed to maintain essential cellular activities. It synthesizes proteins, produces and uses energy, and maintains the normal structure and function of the neuron. In addition, the cell body participates in integrating signals received from the dendrites and different parts of the cell body. When the combined effects of these signals reach a certain threshold at the axon initial segment, the neuron may generate an action potential, also known as a nerve impulse, which then travels along the axon. The graphic uses a relatively large main region to help us distinguish the cell body from the thinner dendrites and axon surrounding it.

### 4. Nucleus

Move your finger to approximately the center of the cell body. Here, you will encounter a distinct, roughly circular raised structure that stands out from the surrounding area. Slowly trace the edge of this raised structure with your finger, feeling its relatively concentrated, circular outline. This structure represents the nucleus. The nucleus is an important structure within the neuron that stores genetic information. It contains chromosomes made of DNA, which carries the genetic instructions that control the cell’s life processes. By regulating gene expression, the nucleus influences neuronal growth, metabolism, protein synthesis, and other essential cellular activities. Unlike prokaryotic organisms such as bacteria, neurons are eukaryotic cells, meaning that their nuclei are enclosed by nuclear membranes. The graphic represents the nucleus as a distinct circular raised structure within the cell body, helping us understand its position and function.

### 5. Nissl Bodies

Now, slowly move your finger outward from the nucleus into the surrounding region of the cell body. You will encounter several smaller, separate raised structures. Continue exploring around the nucleus, feeling how these small projections are distributed within the cell body. These structures represent Nissl bodies. Nissl bodies are cellular structures closely associated with protein synthesis in neurons. They consist mainly of rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes attached to its surface. Neurons must continuously synthesize various proteins to maintain their cellular structures, replace cellular components, and support the reception and transmission of nerve signals. Nissl bodies are mainly found in the cell body and dendrites but are generally absent from the axon. Please note that actual Nissl bodies are not separate spherical structures. Instead, they consist of arrangements of rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes within the cell. The graphic uses several small raised structures to help us recognize the distribution of Nissl bodies within the cell body and their role in protein synthesis.

### 6. Axon

Now, slowly move your finger from the cell body toward the lower right. You will gradually encounter a long, thin, continuous structure extending from the cell body. This structure is more elongated than the dendrites. Continue tracing it toward the lower right, feeling how it extends over a relatively long distance before reaching the branching region in the lower-right area of the graphic. This structure represents the axon. The axon is an important part of the neuron responsible for transmitting nerve impulses. When a neuron receives sufficient stimulation and generates an action potential, the action potential usually begins near the axon initial segment and travels along the axon toward its terminals. Through this process, neurons can transmit information to other neurons, muscle cells, or gland cells. Unlike dendrites, which usually have multiple branches, a neuron typically has only one main axon, although the axon may branch along its length or near its terminals. Axon lengths vary considerably among different types of neurons. Some are very short, while others can extend over long distances. The graphic uses a long, thin structure extending from the cell body toward the lower right to help us understand the position of the axon and its role in transmitting information.

### 7. Myelin Sheath

Now, continue slowly tracing the axon toward the lower right. You will notice several relatively thick, segmented structures arranged consecutively along the axon. Slowly move your finger along one of these segments, feeling how it surrounds the axon. Then, continue toward the lower right to explore the other similar segments. These structures represent the myelin sheath. The myelin sheath is a multilayered, lipid-rich membrane structure that surrounds the axons of certain neurons and acts as an electrical insulator. It reduces the loss of electrical current across the axonal membrane, allowing nerve impulses to travel more rapidly along myelinated axons. In the peripheral nervous system, myelin is mainly formed by Schwann cells, while in the central nervous system, it is mainly formed by oligodendrocytes. Please note that not all neuronal axons have myelin sheaths. The graphic uses several relatively thick segments to help us understand how myelin is distributed along the axon and how it increases the speed of nerve impulse conduction.

### 8. Nodes of Ranvier

Now, continue moving your finger slowly toward the lower right along the axon. Between adjacent segments of the myelin sheath, you will encounter several narrow gaps or recessed regions. Pause at one of these gaps and feel the difference between the thicker myelin segments on either side and the narrower region between them. These gaps represent the nodes of Ranvier. Nodes of Ranvier are regions of the axon between adjacent myelin segments that are not covered by the myelin sheath. These regions contain high concentrations of voltage-gated sodium channels, allowing action potentials to be regenerated at these locations. When a nerve impulse travels along a myelinated axon, electrical signals spread rapidly between adjacent nodes of Ranvier, while action potentials are mainly regenerated at the nodes themselves. This process is known as saltatory conduction, which significantly increases the speed of nerve impulse transmission. The graphic uses gaps between the myelin segments to help us understand the positions of the nodes of Ranvier and their role in transmitting nerve signals.

### 9. Axon Terminals

Finally, continue tracing the axon toward the lower right until you reach the terminal region in the lower-right corner of the graphic. Here, you will notice that the previously continuous axon divides into several smaller branches. Slowly trace one of these branches outward, then explore the others, feeling how they extend in different directions from the end of the axon. These terminal branches represent the axon terminals. Axon terminals are important regions through which neurons transmit information to other cells. They can form synapses with other neurons, muscle cells, or gland cells. When an action potential reaches an axon terminal, it can trigger the release of neurotransmitters. These neurotransmitters enter the synaptic cleft and bind to receptors on the next cell, allowing the signal to be transmitted. For example, motor neurons can release neurotransmitters to transmit signals to muscle cells, triggering muscle contraction. Please note that not all neurons transmit information by releasing chemical neurotransmitters. Electrical synapses also exist between certain neurons. The graphic uses several small branches at the end of the axon to help us understand how neurons transmit information to other cells.

Closing: Congratulations on completing your exploration of the neuron! You have now learned about the major structures of a neuron, including dendrites, the cell body, nucleus, Nissl bodies, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, and axon terminals, as well as how these structures work together to receive, integrate, and transmit nerve signals.
