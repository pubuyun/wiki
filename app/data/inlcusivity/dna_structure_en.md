### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the DNA tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. Next, we will begin with the overall structure of DNA and gradually explore its main components to understand how DNA stores and transmits genetic information.

### 1. Overall Structure of DNA

Starting from the arrow in the lower-left corner, slowly move both hands straight upward. You will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line until you reach the bottom of the DNA structure. Then, starting from the center of the bottom of the DNA structure, slowly move straight upward. You will encounter two long, slender, spiral-shaped structures. As you move upward, these structures alternately appear on the left and right sides of the graphic, creating the impression that they are twisting around each other. You will also notice several horizontal structures connecting the two spirals. Together, these structures form the DNA double helix. DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid, a biological macromolecule that stores and transmits genetic information. It consists of two polynucleotide strands that twist around a common axis to form the characteristic double-helix structure. You can imagine it as a twisted ladder: the two outer spirals form the sides of the ladder, while the horizontal structures between them resemble its rungs.

### 2. The Two Sugar-Phosphate Backbones

Now, move your finger to one of the outer spiral-shaped structures of the double helix. Starting near the bottom of the graphic, slowly trace upward along the left outer edge. You will notice that this structure does not extend upward in a straight line. Instead, it repeatedly curves inward and outward as it rises. This represents one of the two sugar-phosphate backbones of DNA. The other backbone is located on the opposite side. You can explore it in the same way, starting from the bottom and tracing upward along the right outer edge to feel how the two backbones twist around each other. The sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA consists of alternating deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups. The two backbones are positioned on the outside of the double helix, while the nitrogenous bases face inward. In addition, the two DNA strands run in opposite directions, an arrangement known as antiparallel orientation. Please note that, to make the graphic easier to print and explore through touch, the sugar-phosphate backbones have been simplified into continuous spiral-shaped structures. Therefore, individual deoxyribose sugars and phosphate groups cannot be distinguished through touch alone.

### 3. Base Pairs

Now, keep your finger on either of the outer spiral backbones and slowly move inward toward the center of the graphic. After moving a short distance, you will encounter a horizontal structure connecting the two spiral backbones. Trace this structure toward the opposite side to feel how it connects the two outer backbones. These horizontal structures represent the base pairs inside DNA. DNA contains four types of nitrogenous bases: adenine, thymine, cytosine, and guanine, commonly represented by the letters A, T, C, and G, respectively. These bases follow specific complementary pairing rules: A pairs with T, while C pairs with G. Because the bases are located inside the DNA double helix, the horizontal structures in the graphic connect the two outer backbones, resembling the rungs of a ladder. To make the four bases easier to identify, this graphic uses the letters A, T, C, and G to represent them, with corresponding Braille labels provided for each letter. Try using your fingertips to feel the Braille labels at the base positions, identify the different bases, and explore how they are arranged in pairs. Please note that real nitrogenous bases are not shaped like letters. The letters and Braille labels in this graphic are simply representations designed to help learners distinguish between the four bases.

### 4. Deoxyribonucleotides

Now, choose one of the base pairs you have just explored. Starting from one of the outer spiral backbones, slowly move inward toward the center of the graphic until you reach the horizontal connecting structure. The basic building block of DNA is the deoxyribonucleotide. Each deoxyribonucleotide consists of three components: a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. Each base pair you have just touched consists of two bases, each belonging to a separate deoxyribonucleotide on one of the two DNA strands. Within each DNA strand, adjacent deoxyribonucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds, forming a continuous polynucleotide chain. You can imagine DNA as two long chains made up of many deoxyribonucleotides connected in sequence, with the two chains held together by complementary base pairing. Because many microscopic structures have been simplified in this graphic, you can use the outer spiral backbones and internal base structures to understand the overall arrangement of deoxyribonucleotides in DNA. However, you cannot completely distinguish the three individual components of a deoxyribonucleotide through touch alone.

### 5. Complementary Base Pairing and Hydrogen Bonds

Now, choose any horizontal connecting structure in the graphic once again. Slowly move your finger from one spiral backbone toward the other, feeling how the structure connects the two DNA strands. The two DNA strands are not joined randomly. Instead, they follow strict complementary base-pairing rules. Adenine, or A, pairs with thymine, or T, while cytosine, or C, pairs with guanine, or G. Try using the Braille labels on the graphic to identify these bases and explore the pairing relationships between A and T, and between C and G. In a real DNA molecule, complementary bases are held together by hydrogen bonds. A and T typically form two hydrogen bonds, while C and G typically form three hydrogen bonds. These hydrogen bonds help stabilize the DNA double helix while allowing the two strands to separate temporarily during processes such as DNA replication. Please note that the horizontal structures in the graphic primarily represent base pairs. Individual hydrogen bonds have not been reproduced as separate tactile features. Therefore, although you can identify the bases and their complementary pairing relationships through the Braille labels, you cannot directly distinguish the two or three hydrogen bonds through touch.

### 6. DNA Double Helix

Finally, return your finger to one of the outer spiral backbones of DNA. Starting from the bottom of the graphic, slowly trace upward along the backbone. As you move, you will notice that the backbone continuously changes its position from left to right, while the other backbone also extends around the same axis, twisting together with the first. The two strands form a double helix extending vertically through the graphic. Try using both hands to trace the two backbones upward at the same time, feeling how they twist around each other while remaining connected by the internal base pairs. The genetic information carried by DNA is primarily stored in the sequence of its bases. Different sequences of bases can encode different genetic information, while the double-helix structure helps maintain the stability of the DNA molecule. During DNA replication, the two original strands separate, and each serves as a template for the synthesis of a new complementary strand according to the base-pairing rules. The two resulting DNA molecules each typically contain one original strand and one newly synthesized strand. This process is known as semiconservative replication.

Starting from the arrow in the lower-left corner, we have explored the overall double-helix structure of DNA, its two sugar-phosphate backbones, internal base pairs, the basic components of deoxyribonucleotides, and the roles of complementary base pairing and hydrogen bonds. By feeling the Braille labels on the graphic, you can also identify the four bases—A, T, C, and G—and gain a better understanding of their complementary pairing relationships. Although the structure of DNA may seem complex, its organized components and precise arrangement allow it to store and transmit genetic information.
