### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the animal cell tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. Next, we will begin with the overall outline of the animal cell and follow a continuous exploration path to learn about its internal structures and their functions.

### 1. Overall Structure of the Animal Cell

Starting from the arrow in the lower-left corner, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the animal cell model. Keep moving upward until you reach the outer edge of the cell. Then, slowly trace this edge with your finger, feeling its smooth, nearly circular outline. The region enclosed by this outline represents an entire animal cell. Animal cells are the basic structural and functional units of the bodies of humans and other animals. Although different types of animal cells vary in shape and function, they generally contain a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and various organelles that work together to maintain normal cellular activities.

### 2. Cell Membrane

Now, keep your finger on the outermost boundary of the cell that you just explored. This continuous, smooth boundary represents the cell membrane. The cell membrane surrounds the entire animal cell and consists mainly of a phospholipid bilayer with proteins embedded within it. It acts as a selectively permeable barrier, regulating the movement of substances into and out of the cell and helping maintain a relatively stable internal environment. In addition, the cell membrane participates in communication between cells, allowing them to detect and respond to external signals. Unlike plant cells, animal cells do not have a rigid cell wall. As a result, their shapes are generally more flexible and can change to some extent depending on their functions and surrounding conditions.

### 3. Cytoplasm

Now, move your finger slightly inward from the cell membrane. You will enter a relatively flat region representing the cytoplasm. The cytoplasm includes the cytosol, the fluid component of the cell, as well as the various organelles and internal structures suspended within it. The cytosol is a water-based environment containing many dissolved substances, where important metabolic reactions take place, including glycolysis. Next, we will continue exploring inward from this region to discover the different organelles within the cell.

### 4. Mitochondrion

Now, keep your finger in the cytoplasmic region near the lower-left part of the cell and slowly move upward along the inner side of the cell membrane. After moving a short distance, you will encounter a relatively large, raised oval structure. It is located close to the left edge of the cell and extends mainly in the vertical direction. Gently move your finger across its surface to feel the short, ridge-like patterns inside it. This structure represents a mitochondrion. Mitochondria are the main sites of aerobic respiration in eukaryotic cells. They use energy stored in organic molecules to produce ATP. ATP can be understood as the cell’s directly usable “energy currency,” providing energy for various cellular activities. The ridges you can feel represent the cristae, which are folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane. These folds increase the membrane's surface area, providing more space for the reactions involved in ATP production during aerobic respiration. There are four mitochondria in this graphic, all with similar oval shapes. For now, we will explore only the mitochondrion near the cell membrane on the left side. You may explore the remaining three after completing the guide.

### 5. Centrosome

Next, keep one hand on the mitochondrion as a reference point and slowly move your other hand toward its lower right. After crossing a short, relatively flat region of cytoplasm, you will encounter two small, rod-shaped bumps. They are positioned close together and arranged approximately perpendicular to each other. This structure represents the centrosome. The centrosome is an important microtubule-organizing center in animal cells. It typically contains a pair of centrioles arranged approximately at right angles, surrounded by pericentriolar material. During cell division, the centrosome helps organize the mitotic spindle, which separates chromosomes and ensures that genetic material is correctly distributed between the daughter cells.

### 6. Peroxisome

Now, move slightly toward the lower left from the centrosome. You will encounter a separate, smooth, rounded bump that is noticeably smaller than the mitochondrion you explored earlier. This structure represents a peroxisome. Peroxisomes are membrane-bound organelles involved in the breakdown of fatty acids and other metabolic processes. They also contain enzymes that help process certain harmful substances produced during cellular metabolism. For example, the enzyme catalase can break down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, reducing the damage that excessive hydrogen peroxide could cause to the cell. There are two peroxisomes in this graphic, both represented by small, rounded bumps.

### 7. Golgi Apparatus

Now, slowly move toward the upper right from the small, rounded structure you just explored. You will encounter a group of curved, ribbon-like structures arranged in layers, resembling several curved sheets stacked together. This is the Golgi apparatus. The Golgi apparatus consists of stacks of flattened membrane-bound sacs and can be understood as the cell’s “processing and distribution center.” It further processes, modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids received from the endoplasmic reticulum. These substances are then transported to specific locations within the cell or released outside the cell through secretion. The Golgi apparatus therefore plays an important role in the transport and distribution of materials within the cell.

### 8. Golgi Vesicles

Now, remain near the Golgi apparatus and slowly move your fingers around it. You will encounter numerous small, rounded bumps scattered nearby. These represent vesicles associated with the Golgi apparatus. Vesicles are tiny membrane-bound structures that can be understood as the cell’s “transport packages.” Some vesicles deliver substances to the Golgi apparatus, while others carry materials that have been processed and sorted by the Golgi apparatus to other locations within the cell or to the cell membrane for secretion. Feel several of these small bumps with your fingertips and notice their positions relative to the Golgi apparatus. Then, move your finger to the right side of the Golgi apparatus to prepare for the next structure.

### 9. Microtubules

Now, slowly move toward the lower right from the Golgi apparatus. You will first pass over a horizontally positioned oval bump with short, ridge-like patterns inside it. This is another mitochondrion, with the same characteristics as the one you explored earlier. After passing this mitochondrion, continue moving right and slightly upward. You will encounter two long, narrow, relatively straight, rod-like bumps arranged diagonally. These structures represent microtubules. Microtubules are important components of the cytoskeleton and are made of tubulin proteins, forming hollow tubular structures. They help maintain the cell’s shape and internal organization and can also serve as “tracks” for transporting vesicles and other materials within the cell. In addition, microtubules participate in cell movement and form the mitotic spindle during cell division, helping separate chromosomes.

### 10. Lysosome

Now, slowly move straight upward from the microtubules. After crossing a short, relatively flat region of cytoplasm, you will encounter a distinct, smooth, sac-like bump. This structure represents a lysosome. Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles containing various hydrolytic enzymes that break down substances. These enzymes help the cell digest aging or damaged organelles and process certain foreign materials taken into the cell. Some of the smaller molecules produced through this breakdown can be reused by the cell. Therefore, lysosomes can be understood as the cell’s internal “recycling and waste-processing centers.”

### 11. Intermediate Filaments

Now, continue moving upward from the lysosome toward the right side of the cell. You will encounter a group of long, narrow, thread-like bumps with relatively uniform thickness. These structures represent intermediate filaments. Intermediate filaments are another important component of the cytoskeleton and are mainly composed of different types of fibrous proteins. They provide mechanical strength, helping cells resist stretching and deformation while maintaining the stability of internal structures. In certain cells, intermediate filaments also help anchor the nucleus and other cellular components. Compared with the relatively straight microtubules you explored earlier, the intermediate filaments in the graphic feel more like continuous, slender fibers. Please note that this tactile difference is mainly intended to help distinguish the two types of cytoskeletal structures.

### 12. Endoplasmic Reticulum

Now, slowly move toward the left from the intermediate filaments. Along the way, you will first pass over an oval bump containing short, ridge-like patterns. This is another mitochondrion in the graphic. Continue moving left past it until you encounter a large region of continuous, curved, and repeatedly folded membrane-like structures. This is the endoplasmic reticulum. Slowly trace these curved structures with your fingers, feeling how they connect to form an extensive membrane network. Unlike the Golgi apparatus, which consists of separate stacks of flattened membrane sacs, the endoplasmic reticulum forms an interconnected system of membranes. It can be divided into rough endoplasmic reticulum and smooth endoplasmic reticulum. The rough endoplasmic reticulum has numerous ribosomes attached to its surface and is mainly involved in the synthesis, folding, and initial processing of secreted proteins and membrane proteins. The smooth endoplasmic reticulum lacks attached ribosomes and is mainly involved in lipid synthesis, the detoxification of certain substances, and calcium ion storage.

### 13. Ribosomes

Now, continue slowly exploring the endoplasmic reticulum and its surrounding region. You will encounter many tiny, rounded bumps. These small bumps represent ribosomes. Ribosomes are structures responsible for protein synthesis and consist of ribosomal RNA and proteins. They are not surrounded by membranes. Some ribosomes are free in the cytosol and mainly produce proteins used within the cell, while others are attached to the surface of the rough endoplasmic reticulum and participate in the synthesis of secreted proteins, membrane proteins, and other proteins. Therefore, you may encounter similar small bumps in several locations throughout the graphic. Real ribosomes are extremely small, so they have been enlarged in the graphic to make them distinguishable through touch.

### 14. Nucleus

Next, slowly move inward from the endoplasmic reticulum you just explored. You will notice that these curved membrane structures are distributed around a much larger, rounded structure. Continue moving inward until you encounter a prominent, large, circular bump. This represents the nucleus. Slowly trace around its outer edge to feel its overall outline. The circular boundary you can feel represents the nuclear envelope, also called the nuclear membrane. The nuclear envelope consists of two membranes that separate the interior of the nucleus from the cytoplasm. Nuclear pores regulate the movement of specific substances into and out of the nucleus. The nucleus contains DNA, which carries important genetic information. By regulating gene expression, the nucleus influences cell growth, metabolic activities, and cell division. It can therefore be understood as an important “control center” of the cell. However, not all animal cells contain a nucleus. For example, mature human red blood cells lack nuclei.

### 15. Nucleolus

Finally, keep your finger within the nucleus and slowly move from its outer edge toward the center. You will encounter a smaller, relatively smooth, rounded bump within the larger nuclear structure. This represents the nucleolus. The nucleolus is located inside the nucleus and is not surrounded by a membrane. Its main functions are to synthesize and process ribosomal RNA, or rRNA, and participate in the assembly of ribosomal subunits. These subunits are subsequently transported into the cytoplasm, where they contribute to the formation of functional ribosomes responsible for protein synthesis. The nucleolus therefore plays an important role in supporting the cell’s protein production.

Closing: Congratulations on completing your exploration of the animal cell tactile graphic! Starting from the arrow in the lower-left corner, we have followed a continuous path to explore the cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, centrosome, peroxisomes, Golgi apparatus and its associated vesicles, microtubules, lysosome, intermediate filaments, endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, nucleus, and nucleolus. Although these structures have different shapes and functions, they do not work independently. Instead, they coordinate with one another to maintain the cell’s normal activities. Through this tactile exploration, we hope you have gained a better understanding of not only the internal structures of animal cells but also how these structures work together.
