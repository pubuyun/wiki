### Opening

Welcome to the guided exploration of the female reproductive system tactile graphic. Before we begin, place the graphic in front of you with the raised structures facing upward. Next, feel the surface of the graphic to locate the arrow. Rotate the graphic until the long, narrow end of the arrow points toward your body. Once positioned, keep the graphic in place. The arrow should now be located in the lower-left corner. Throughout this guide, “straight up” refers to the direction away from your body, while “straight down” refers to the direction toward your body. “Left” and “right” correspond to your left-hand side and right-hand side, respectively. We will explore the following structures in order: the overall structure of the female reproductive system, vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries, learning about their positions and main functions.

### 1. Overall Structure of the Female Reproductive System

Starting from the arrow, slowly move both hands straight upward. As you move, you will first encounter a horizontal dividing line. Continue moving upward past this line to enter the female reproductive system region of the graphic. Slowly move both hands outward to explore the raised structures within the graphic. You will notice a relatively large central structure, with a long, thin, curved tube extending outward from each side of its upper region. Near the outer ends of these two tubes, you will find a smaller oval-shaped structure on each side. From the lower part of the central structure, a narrower tube extends downward. Together, these structures represent the female reproductive system shown in the graphic. The female reproductive system consists of several interconnected organs that are mainly responsible for producing oocytes, secreting reproductive hormones, and providing the necessary physiological conditions for fertilization, pregnancy, and childbirth. Next, we will begin with the structure in the lower central region of the graphic and gradually move upward to explore each organ.

### 2. Vagina

Now, move your finger to the lower central region of the graphic. Here, you will encounter a long, narrow, tube-like structure extending straight upward. Slowly trace this structure with your finger, feeling how it extends from the lower region toward the center of the graphic. This structure represents the vagina. The vagina is an elastic, muscular canal that connects the cervix to the outside of the body. It is an important part of the female reproductive system and performs several physiological functions. During menstruation, shed endometrial tissue and blood can leave the body through the vagina. In addition, the vagina forms part of the birth canal through which a baby passes during vaginal childbirth. The inner walls of the vagina contain folds called rugae, allowing it to stretch to accommodate different physiological activities. The graphic uses a tube-like structure extending upward from the lower region to help us understand the position of the vagina and its role in connecting different parts of the reproductive system.

### 3. Cervix

Now, continue slowly moving your finger straight upward along the vagina. As you approach its upper end, you will encounter a relatively narrow region connecting to a larger structure above. This region represents the cervix. The cervix is the narrower, lower part of the uterus, connecting the uterus above to the vagina below. Inside the cervix is a narrow passage called the cervical canal, which connects the uterine cavity to the vagina. The cervix performs several important functions. For example, it produces mucus whose properties change throughout the menstrual cycle, influencing the ability of sperm to pass through the cervical canal. During pregnancy, the cervix usually remains relatively closed, helping to maintain the pregnancy. During childbirth, however, the cervix gradually dilates, allowing the baby to pass through the birth canal. The graphic uses the narrow connecting region between the uterus and vagina to help us understand the position and functions of the cervix.

### 4. Uterus

Now, continue moving your finger straight upward from the cervix. You will gradually encounter a much wider, larger structure located in the central region of the graphic. Slowly trace the outer edge of this structure with your finger, feeling how it widens as you move upward from its narrower lower region. This structure represents the uterus. The uterus is a hollow, muscular organ located in the female pelvic cavity. Its overall shape is often compared to an inverted pear, with a wider upper region that gradually narrows toward the cervix below. The uterine wall mainly consists of three layers: the inner endometrium, the middle myometrium, and the outer perimetrium. The endometrium undergoes cyclical changes throughout the menstrual cycle. When pregnancy does not occur, part of the endometrium is shed during menstruation. If a fertilized egg develops normally and reaches the uterus, it usually implants in the endometrium, where the embryo and later the fetus continue to develop. The myometrium consists of smooth muscle and produces rhythmic contractions during childbirth, helping to deliver the baby. The graphic uses a relatively large central structure to help us understand the position and shape of the uterus, as well as its important roles in menstruation, pregnancy, and childbirth.

### 5. Fallopian Tubes

Now, move your finger to the upper region of the uterus. Slowly move toward the left from the upper part of the uterus. You will encounter a long, thin, curved tube extending outward from the central region. Continue tracing this tube toward the outer side, feeling its curved shape. Then, return your finger to the upper part of the uterus and repeat the same exploration toward the right. You will notice that a similar tube-like structure extends from each side of the uterus. These two structures represent the fallopian tubes. The fallopian tubes are important passages connecting the uterus to the regions near the ovaries, although their outer openings are not directly attached to the ovaries. Near the outer end of each fallopian tube are finger-like projections called fimbriae, which help capture the oocyte released during ovulation. Once an oocyte enters the fallopian tube, the coordinated actions of cilia lining the tube and muscular contractions gradually move it toward the uterus. In humans, fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. If fertilization is successful, the early embryo generally continues moving toward the uterus and, under suitable conditions, implants in the endometrium. The graphic uses curved tubes extending outward from both sides of the upper uterus to help us understand the positions of the fallopian tubes and their roles in oocyte transport and fertilization.

### 6. Ovaries

Finally, continue slowly moving your finger outward along the left fallopian tube. Near the end of the tube, you will encounter a relatively small, oval-shaped raised structure. Slowly trace its edge with your finger, feeling its rounded outline. This structure represents the ovary. Next, move your finger to the region near the end of the right fallopian tube. Here, you will find another oval-shaped structure with a similar shape. This represents the right ovary. Females typically have two ovaries, located on either side of the uterus. The ovaries are important organs of the female reproductive system and perform two main functions: producing and releasing oocytes, and secreting reproductive hormones. The ovaries contain numerous follicles, each of which typically contains a developing or immature oocyte. In females of reproductive age, one dominant follicle usually matures during each menstrual cycle and releases a secondary oocyte during ovulation. In addition, the ovaries secrete hormones such as estrogen and progesterone, which help regulate the menstrual cycle, maintain the normal functioning of the reproductive system, and influence physiological activities in other tissues throughout the body. Please note that although the ovaries and fallopian tubes are located close to each other, they are not directly connected. The graphic uses oval-shaped raised structures on both sides to help us understand the positions of the ovaries and their main functions.

Closing: Congratulations on completing your exploration of the female reproductive system! You have now learned about its major structures, including the vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries, as well as how these organs work together in physiological processes such as oocyte production and transport, hormone secretion, fertilization, pregnancy, and childbirth.
