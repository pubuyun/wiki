## Lecture

### SynBio Intro Lecture

We delivered an introductory session on synthetic biology for Grade 10 and 11 students. The session covered gene circuits and protein design, introducing how biology, engineering, and computation come together in synthetic biology.

#### Target

For this introductory session on synthetic biology, we targeted high school students from Nanning, Guangxi who had already studied basic genetic engineering as part of their school curriculum. We noticed that students often viewed biology as a subject focused on memorisation rather than engineering and problem-solving, while also showing curiosity about emerging technologies.

#### Method

To build on students’ existing biological knowledge, we introduced synthetic biology through the concept of gene circuits, explaining how biological parts such as promoters, RBSs, and CDSs can be combined to create functional circuits. Students then applied these concepts in a simple gene circuit design activity, choosing a desired biological function and considering the inputs, outputs, and regulatory logic needed to achieve it.

Since students were also curious about emerging technologies, we introduced the connection between artificial intelligence and synthetic biology through examples of AI-assisted de novo protein design and computational tools such as *RFdiffusion* and *ProteinMPNN*. This gave students a glimpse of how AI is used in synthetic biology and broadened their understanding of the field.

#### Reflection

This activity showed the value of interactive learning in connecting biological concepts with practical applications. However, students’ prior knowledge varied: those with biology competition experience found introductory concepts simple, while others struggled with advanced topics. This highlighted the need to consider students’ prior knowledge rather than relying only on their grade level.

In response to students’ interest in more advanced topics, we developed a follow-up lecture, [Protein Design Lecture](#protein-design-lecture), to help them explore synthetic biology in greater depth.

### Protein Design Lecture

#### Target

During the after-class discussion following our SynBio Intro Lecture in Nanning No.3 High School, several students approached us to ask about our project. As we introduced it, they became particularly interested in the protein-engineering workflow and expressed that they wished to learn more about the methodologies used to design proteins in real-world SynBio research. Feedback collected through sticky notes also showed the same expectations. In response, we designed a lecture focused on protein design.

#### Method

We began with fundamental concepts and methods such as intermolecular forces, alanine scanning, sequence comparison and conserved-motif analysis. We then introduced rational design through two published cases: improving lipase selectivity for omega-3 enrichment and enhancing glucanase stability and catalytic efficiency. These cases demonstrated how structural evidence can guide mutation design and how candidate designs can be iteratively tested and refined through the *DBTL* cycle.

For AI-assisted de novo protein design, we used a text-to-image analogy to explain diffusion models and introduced how new protein structures and sequences can be generated according to specific design constraints. Our *RFdiffusion3*–*LigandMPNN*–*RoseTTAFold3*–molecular-dynamics workflow provided a real example of how de novo binders can be generated and computationally evaluated.

### Online SynBio Market And Career Analysis Lecture

This online session introduced students to the synthetic biology industry and its career opportunities. We explored different career paths and how scientific research can be turned into commercial products, helping students better understand the field before choosing their future studies.

#### Target

During our previous session on protein design at Nanning No. 3 High School in Guangxi, some students became particularly interested in studying synthetic biology at university but were uncertain about the industry's development and career prospects. This inspired us to organise an online session analysing the SynBio industry and its career opportunities.

This session targeted high school students considering SynBio-related university majors or careers, including entrepreneurship, to help them better understand the field and make more informed decisions about their future.

#### Method

We analyzed the synthetic biology industry from the perspectives of industry growth and career opportunities. With real-world data and company cases, students gained a more realistic and well-rounded understanding of the industry. For example, we introduced the industry's reported *CAGR* of 17.4% to illustrate its growth potential. We also explored career opportunities across the upstream, midstream, and downstream sectors, including job roles, responsibilities, and reference salary ranges. Finally, we used Cathay Biotech, a leading Chinese biotechnology company, as a case study to show how synthetic biology can be applied in industry.

## Workshop

### Primary School SynBio Intro Workshop

We introduced the basic concepts of synthetic biology to Grade 5 students at Keyuan Primary School in Shenzhen through everyday applications and hands-on activities. Students created clay models of organisms they imagined and extracted DNA from strawberries.

#### Target

The workshop was held at Shenzhen Nanshan Foreign Language School Keyuan Primary School for Grade 5 students. While these students had gained basic general science knowledge through their regular lessons, they had not received a formal introduction to biology. Our conversations with teachers and review of the curriculum confirmed this gap. However, the students were highly curious and enthusiastic. At this early stage of scientific learning, they were receptive to new knowledge, so we introduced synthetic biology in simplified and accessible forms.

#### Method

To accommodate primary school students' cognitive characteristics and limited prior knowledge of biology, we replaced lengthy textual explanations with intuitive visual aids, making abstract synthetic biology concepts easier to understand. We also introduced relatable real-world examples, such as fluorescent cats and mice with mammoth characteristics, and displayed physical samples of synthetic spider silk protein provided by Link Spider, allowing students to observe tangible applications of synthetic biology.

The session also included hands-on activities such as strawberry DNA extraction and free clay modelling. During the clay modelling activity, students were encouraged to create models of organisms they imagined could be designed through synthetic biology. Free from constraints, they could brainstorm freely and express their creativity through their designs.

#### Feedback

We were impressed by the enthusiasm and creativity of the primary school students throughout the session. We invited participants to explain the ideas behind their clay creations. Some groups proposed imaginative ideas, such as: “We want to create fluorescent mosquitoes so that people can spot and eliminate them more easily in the dark!” and “We combined plant and chicken genes. Traditional egg-laying chickens may not be very efficient and can experience discomfort. We think a ‘seed chicken’ could improve productivity while potentially reducing pain for the chickens.” These ideas showed that the workshop had successfully sparked students' enthusiasm for synthetic biology.

In addition to open discussions, we designed a short quiz to assess students' understanding of synthetic biology. Surprisingly, the final quiz achieved a high overall accuracy rate, with ten teams tied for the top ranking.

#### Reflection

The success of this workshop reminded us that making SynBio education accessible requires more than simplifying scientific explanations. By adapting both the content and format to learners' backgrounds, we can create more opportunities for diverse audiences to engage with SynBio.

Furthermore, while the clay modelling activity encouraged students to imagine new organisms and explore the possibilities of synthetic biology, it inspired us to develop the Bacteria–Fungi Clay Kit to help students learn about the structures of microorganisms and their roles in synthetic biology through hands-on creation.

#### Method

We organised a two-day hands-on workshop in which students carried out a series of connected procedures, including DNA transformation, recovery, plating, colony PCR, gel electrophoresis, and induction.

Before the workshop, we introduced essential laboratory safety rules, including the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) and the prohibition of food and drinks in the laboratory.

We also included short quizzes in the workbook to help students assess their understanding of newly introduced concepts and identify areas requiring further explanation.

#### Feedback

According to our post-workshop survey, most participants responded positively to the experience, leaving comments such as “The instructors are so patient” and “I successfully learned how to use a micropipette!” Some also expressed interest in attending more laboratory sessions like this.

#### Reflection

Through this workshop, we realised that fragmented explanations could make it difficult for students to develop a coherent understanding of the overall workflow. In future sessions, we could allow more time for explanations and encourage tutors to check students’ understanding at each stage through questions, providing additional guidance when needed.

We could also provide pre-reading materials to familiarise students with key concepts before the workshop, helping them follow the explanations more easily and participate more confidently in the experiments.

#### Target

#### Method

Centered on the topic “When synthetic biology can eliminate human suffering, should we prioritize optimizing the functions of human life, or preserving the original state of human life?”, we recruited eight student debaters schoolwide. Since most participants had little synthetic biology background, they completed pre-debate research to learn core concepts and associated controversies. We measured the debate’s educational impact using pre‑and post‑debate votes, self‑reported understanding scores, and interviews with debaters and audience members.

## Seminar

### SynBio-vocab Learning Seminar

#### Target

Research in biology often requires researchers to access and understand English-language literature, especially in a rapidly developing and internationally connected field such as synthetic biology. For Chinese learners who do not study biology in English, understanding scientific literature can be challenging. Although translation tools can help, their translations may sometimes be inaccurate, imprecise, or unnatural, creating additional barriers to understanding scientific concepts and conducting research.

With this in mind, our English-learning seminar primarily targeted families in nearby communities. Parents may encounter difficulties in learning English themselves or supporting their children's English learning. These challenges can be particularly relevant for families whose children are considering biology as a future major, as English proficiency may affect their ability to engage with university-level study and scientific literature. More specifically, biological terminology can be difficult to understand when learners are unfamiliar with the English roots and affixes used to form scientific words.

#### Method

During the seminar, participants took turns sharing the difficulties and obstacles they encountered when learning English. We also exchanged learning experiences and strategies so that we can learn from one another. In addition, we introduced and distributed a biology vocabulary booklet as a supplementary resource, focusing on one specific challenge in learning English for biology: understanding subject-specific terminology.

Rather than simply listing biological terms alongside their Chinese translations, the booklet was designed to help readers understand how scientific vocabulary is formed and how it relates to biological concepts. For example, when introducing *lipase*, we explained that it is an enzyme that breaks down lipids. The root *lip-* is associated with lipids, while the suffix *-ase* commonly indicates an enzyme. By explaining the meanings of common biological roots and suffixes, the booklet helps readers recognize patterns in scientific vocabulary and makes unfamiliar terms easier to remember.

In addition to explaining word formation, the booklet provides Chinese explanations and visual aids to support comprehension. For example, when introducing vocabulary related to cell structures, we provided a diagram of a cell to help readers connect the terms with the structures they describe. Finally, the booklet contains short quizzes that allow readers to assess their own understanding of the biological concepts covered.

### Lab-Themed Murder Mystery Game

#### Target

We recognised that high school students usually learn synthetic biology through passive lectures with few interactive opportunities to reason and discuss. We therefore designed a synthetic biology mystery role-play game — a role‑playing crime deduction game where players receive character scripts and uncover the culprit by sharing clues and reasoning together — which puts core biology ideas directly into the game’s clues and tasks for students to use what they have learned while working in groups.

#### Method

Before the deduction activity, students completed a hands-on PCR and gel electrophoresis experiment to accumulate basic operational experience. Afterwards, students participated in our structured PCR-themed mystery game with four players and one host. Each participant received a character script defining their role and specific misoperation in a failed PCR trial. After brief role introduction, students analyzed real experimental clues including gel images, PCR protocols and reagent records to identify the error causing missing DNA bands. Specifically, participants calculated the theoretical extension time based on plasmid maps and compared it with the actual program settings to spot parameter mismatches. Through collective discussion and evidence-based reasoning, students determined which operational mistake led to the experimental failure, actively applying their PCR and electrophoresis knowledge throughout the process.

### Long Videos

#### Target

Our target audience is mainly **young viewers**who watch long-form videos for entertainment rather than formal learning. If we present scientific or historical information as a long sequence of facts, viewers can easily lose interest.

At the same time, long-form videos give us more room to explain complicated topics properly. We therefore wanted to make use of that extra space without making the video feel like a lesson. Our approach was to build the content around stories, people, conflicts, and memorable events, while introducing scientific ideas naturally along the way.

#### Methods

For our long-form content, we treated storytelling as the main structure. Instead of pausing the video for separate scientific explanations, we introduced the science when the story actually needed it.

We used this approach in our video on Craig Venter and the Human Genome Project. Rather than going through the history of genome sequencing step by step, we built the video around Venter’s career, the major turning points in the project, and the competition between his team and the publicly funded Human Genome Project.

We also used humor, music, archival materials, and memorable moments from Venter’s career to keep the video from feeling too heavy. When scientific concepts became important to the story, we explained them directly. For example, we introduced the basic idea of whole-genome shotgun sequencing when explaining why Venter’s team was able to work faster than the public project.

The conflict between the two projects also gave us a natural way to raise a broader question: should important scientific knowledge, especially genomic information, remain public, or can it be controlled by private organizations? We wanted viewers to think about this question as part of the story rather than as a separate discussion added at the end.

For future improvement, we will optimize how advanced concepts are presented to audiences with different levels of scientific knowledge, potentially incorporating more visual storytelling, interactive elements, and progressively structured explanations. We could also incorporate short quizzes or surveys to assess readers’ understanding, identify concepts that require clearer explanations, and refine our content accordingly.
