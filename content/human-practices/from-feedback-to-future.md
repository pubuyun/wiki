## Intro

Through conversations with stakeholders from different backgrounds, we gained insights and perspectives that helped us move beyond our initial ideas and **consider the project in a broader light**. Looking ahead, we take stock of **how far** their feedback has brought us and what it **asks of us moving forward.**

## Stakeholder Assessment

To put the breadth of our Human Practices work into perspective, we considered the different ways stakeholders relate to Expelliodor.

We first grouped them into **four categories**:

- Users & Beneficiaries

- Experts & Professionals

- Companies & Industry

- Society & Community

We then used **Mendelow's Matrix** to map stakeholders according to their **levels of interest in and impact** on our project.

### Users & Beneficiaries

We began by looking at **Users & Beneficiaries**. We collected consumer feedback from both **individual potential customers** and **institutional collaborators** to understand their perspectives on Expelliodor's current design.

#### Individual users

For individual users, we **surveyed** potential customers to gather their perspectives on Expelliodor's current design. Before collecting their feedback, we introduced Expelliodor and the values behind its design: sustainability, inclusive and accessible design, and body autonomy. We also briefly outlined the mechanism of our proposed approach to underarm odor care, which aims to protect the native underarm microbiome without blocking sweat glands.

The survey combined **rating questions** with **open-ended responses**, allowing us to identify what appealed to potential users, what didn't, and what could be improved. All responses were kept confidential and used solely for our project research.

The survey showed that potential customers valued Expelliodor's odor-control function and microbiome protection **(12)**, sustainability **(8)** and inclusive design **(8)**. However, safety was the biggest concern, with **14** respondents worried about safety and skin irritation. Open-ended responses also raised concerns about allergies and side effects from artificial proteins. These findings, coupled with survey results of **26.45%** of responses expressing unwillingness towards using synthetic biology products from Stage 1, Cycle 1 highlighted the need for clearer public understanding of synthetic biology, prompting us to develop [educational initiatives](/education) to address these concerns.

### Experts & Professionals

For **Experts & Professionals**, we returned to **modeling expert Mr. Li Jun**, whose earlier advice had helped shape our protein-design strategies. We shared our modeling work and experimental findings to seek his assessment of our progress and the improvements needed for further development.

Mr. Li recognized our integration of deep learning, molecular-dynamics simulations, and experimental validation. He also responded positively to our [**Characterize–DBTL framework**](/model), which translates an understanding of biological mechanisms into explicit design requirements. In particular, he valued our attempt to connect computational metrics with experimental performance rather than focusing only on generating designs.

His feedback also highlighted where this work remained incomplete. Although we had examined relationships between computational scores and experimental results, the small number of tested candidates limited the conclusions we could draw. This reinforced an important lesson: **a promising computational score is not, by itself, evidence of biological function**, and evaluation criteria need to be checked against experimental outcomes.

Mr. Li recommended expanding our experimental dataset, refining free-energy assessments, and using experimental feedback to guide further optimization. These suggestions gave us a clearer direction for improving our screening criteria and subsequent designs. He also saw potential for our workflow to develop into a more broadly applicable protein-engineering platform, provided its reliability is strengthened through continued testing and refinement.

For **Companies & Industry**, we reached out again to **Mr. Deng from KleanK**. As KleanK specializes in conventional aluminum-salt deodorants, we sought his advice on product development and market positioning.

Mr. Deng's central criticism was that **a novel concept must be supported by experimental evidence**. As he put it, "You have 'FAB,' but not 'E'—evidence." Although he recognized the difference between our protein-based approach and conventional odor-control products, he questioned whether this difference translated into better performance. He emphasized the need to demonstrate efficacy quantitatively, compare results against relevant alternatives, and establish what practical benefit the product could deliver. Our laboratory assays provided preliminary evidence of protein activity, but further validation would be needed to establish the performance of a finished product.

He also highlighted the need to move beyond laboratory testing, emphasizing that **"The product is intended for people. It is not intended for the microorganisms in your experiments."** He challenged us to consider whether our laboratory results would translate into real-world benefits for users, while raising questions about formulation compatibility, production yield, scale-up costs, and storage stability. These concerns highlighted the gap between demonstrating protein activity in the laboratory and establishing a product’s practical efficacy and feasibility.

Finally, Mr. Deng challenged us to evaluate our packaging and marketing ideas against actual consumer preferences. He noted that sustainable and refillable packaging cannot compensate for insufficient efficacy, while some consumers prefer discreet packaging to avoid revealing their deodorant use. This highlighted a tension between our commitment to inclusive, sustainable design and commercial considerations such as affordability, product performance, and repurchase. **Our key takeaway was not to deprioritize these values, but to recognize that they must be supported by a product that works.**

We then turned back to **Society & Community** to evaluate the potential social impacts of Expelliodor.

We did this by revisiting anthropologist **Ms. Mary Ann**, whom we identified as a key stakeholder using Mendelow's Matrix. We shared our **"Expel the Odor, on Your Terms" philosophy**, which emphasizes personal choice and body autonomy, alongside our ideas for accessible packaging.

She responded positively to "**Expel the Odor, on Your Terms,**" appreciating its emphasis on personal choice and the idea that underarm odor care should respect individual preferences and body autonomy. However, our conversation took an unexpected turn when we showed her an advertisement from a company promoting inclusive design. We had been thinking about how to make Expelliodor easier for people with different physical needs to use, but her response challenged us to look **beyond the product itself.** As she put it, "They have gone to all this effort to make something accessible so they can sell a product, rather than working to make people's lives more accessible."

Her criticism caught us off guard. We had been thinking about how to make our product more inclusive, but she asked a bigger question: what good is an accessible deodorant if the world outside remains inaccessible? As she pointed out, people should not only have **"the right to sweat profusely"**; they should also have **"the right to be able to get where they want to go."**

The conversation made us reconsider where our contribution could begin. We decided to retain accessible packaging in our product design while extending our efforts into science education, an area where we could take practical action as high school students. We [created sign-language biology videos, developed 3D-printed tactile biology models with audio guides for blind learners, and designed hands-on biology lessons for neurodivergent children.](/inclusivity)

## Future Plans

Although we prioritized certain stakeholders, we drew on input from **everyone** we engaged with to shape our future plans.

We first classified these plans into **four categories** based on their main objectives: Scientific Research & Safety, Product Development, Marketing, and Public Education. Then, we divided each category into short-term and long-term goals based on their expected timelines and the further testing or development required.

Each plan is linked to feedback from specific stakeholders and their corresponding cycles, showing how their input informs our project's next steps.

### Scientific Research & Safety

#### Short-term

- Conduct an assay with *Staphylococcus hominis* under appropriate protection **(Inspired by Professor Meng from Stage 3 Cycle 3)**

- Develop an online workflow platform for de novo protein safety screening **(Inspired by Mr. Yang from Stage 4 Cycle 3)**

- Conduct staining tests for our formulation on different clothing **(Inspired by Mr. Maz from Stage 5 Cycle 4)**

#### Long-term

- Assess protein stability under relevant storage conditions to assess its suitability for practical deodorant applications **(Inspired by Mr. Deng from Stage 3 Cycle 2)**

- Test water-soluble polymers and trehalose for their effects on protein aggregation **(Inspired by Mr. Su from Stage 4 Cycle 2)**

- Evaluate PP packaging compatibility under temperature cycling and light exposure **(Inspired by Mr. Su from Stage 4 Cycle 2)**

- Collaborate with BSL-2 laboratories to evaluate protein immunogenicity using dendritic cell assays, T-cell proliferation assays, cytokine-release assays, and 3D skin models **(Inspired by Mr. Yang from Stage 4 Cycle 3)**

### Product Development

#### Short-term

- Develop a specialized product line for adolescents, tailored to their changing body odor needs during puberty and with a relatively more affordable price. **(Inspired by an anonymous teammate from Stage 2, Cycle 1)**

- Explore bio-based packaging alternatives and conduct compatibility tests to assess their suitability for our formulation **(Inspired by Mr. Cheng from Stage 5 Cycle 6)**

- Investigate newer dispensing systems that do not use conventional aerosol propellants **(Inspired by Mr. Li from Stage 5 Cycle 6)**

#### Long-term

- Explore co-development partnership with KleanK, progressing toward formulation optimization, pilot testing, large-scale consumer validation, and potential market entry **(Inspired by Mr Deng from Stage 3 Cycle 2)**

- Take into consideration the environmental impact of our formulation and not just packaging **(Inspired by Ms. Zhang from Stage 5 Cycle 6)**

- Conduct a life cycle assessment (LCA) and life cycle costing (LCC) to evaluate environmental impacts of packaging **(Inspired by Director Lu from Stage 5 Cycle 6)**

- Research and select responsible packaging suppliers **(Inspired by Mr. Li from Stage 5 Cycle 6)**

### Marketing

#### Short-term

- Offer bulk-purchase discounts to institutions who purchase in larger quantities **(Inspired by Hotel owner and Gym owner from Stage 2 Cycle 3)**

- Promote our product through influencers and social media platforms such as Douyin **(Inspired by KleanK from Stage 4 Cycle 1)**

- Explore refillable packaging and offer a 5% discount to encourage packaging reuse **(Inspired by Mr. Wood from Stage 5 Cycle 4)**

- Contact deodorant companies whose advertisements may portray people with underarm odor negatively to encourage more inclusive marketing **(Inspired by Ms. Okungbowa from Stage 5 Cycle 4)**

- Investigate regional consumer preferences and regulatory requirements of sustainable packaging **(Inspired Ms. Chen from Stage 5 Cycle 5)**

#### Long-term

- Explore promoting the product to European consumers through substantiated environmental claims and its 100% aluminum-free formulation **(Inspired by Mr. Wood from Stage 5 Cycle 4)**

### Public Education

#### Short-term

- Develop public promotional materials around inclusive user experiences and not insecurity **(Inspired by KleanK from Stage 4 Cycle 1)**

- Develop and share educational content on sustainable packaging on social media **(Inspired by Ms. Chen from Stage 5 Cycle 5)**

- Prepare educational seminars, flyers, or handbooks adapted to different communities **(Inspired by Ms. Troy from Stage 5 Cycle 7)**

#### Long-term

- Test the product with volunteers with disabilities and older adults, using their feedback to improve our inclusive designs **(Inspired by Unilever from Stage 5 Cycle 8)**

- Share experiences and findings from inclusive packaging development to encourage other companies to adopt more accessible designs **(Inspired by Unilever from Stage 5 Cycle 8)**

## Reflection

How can we "Expel" the odor?

Perhaps, by the end of this iGEM season, the question itself matters less than what it has led us to understand.

Throughout our 45 Human Practices initiatives, we brought together perspectives spanning personal experiences of axillary odor, medical treatment, protein safety, product formulation, commercial feasibility, and the sociocultural attitudes surrounding odor.

However, listening to many voices did not mean we had heard them all. There will always be perspectives we did not encounter, questions we did not think to ask, and consequences we did not anticipate. Recognizing this is uncomfortable, but the greater risk lies in mistaking the perspectives we gathered for a complete picture. We began to understand that acting responsibly means not only listening to others, but also recognizing what we may still have failed to hear.

Recognizing the limits of our understanding also led us to question the way we had been taught to approach problems. As high school students, we are accustomed to approaching science through hypotheses, variables, experiments, and evidence. These methods give us ways to navigate scientific uncertainty, yet Human Practices introduced us to a different kind of uncertainty: uncertainty about what should be solved, whose perspectives should guide us, and what consequences our solutions might carry. We began to realize that knowing how to solve a problem did not necessarily mean knowing whether our solution was the right one.

From questioning social stigma to reflecting on responsibility regarding gaps in AI-protein protection, we came to see how our work must be continually reshaped by the people it seeks to serve. Science often begins with the desire to solve a problem. Yet a solution never exists in isolation. It enters a world that already belongs to people, carrying their experiences, expectations, and emotions.

We may not have resolved every question our journey raised, but we have learned that good and responsible science begins with the willingness to ask them.

Perhaps this is what science, at its best, strives to do: not exist apart from society, but remain in conversation with it; not simply offer solutions to the world, but let the world shape how those solutions are brought to life.
