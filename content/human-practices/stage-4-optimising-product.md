## Cycle 1 Second Meeting with KleanK & Evonik

### Observe

After making some progress in our experiments, including validating the activity of our precursor binder, expressing the PepTSh binder and engineered enzyme, and successfully biosynthesizing and verifying bornyl acetate using GC-MS, we began to consider the **formulation** and **product format** of our solution, along with questions regarding its **marketing** and **packaging**. Following their earlier suggestion to reach out once we had experimental results, we contacted **KleanK** again for further guidance.

### Determine

This time, we visited **Yinwei Care**, the company behind the KleanK brand, in Guangzhou, where we met with representatives from the company's technical and marketing teams, specifically **Mr. Deng** **Yongnan**, Director of the Medical R&D Department, the company's CEO **Mr Lu Haoran**, product managers **Wang Hao** and **Wang Yu**, as well as marketing manager **Zeng Qianwen**.

Following our second discussion with KleanK, we also connected with **Evonik**, a **global specialty chemicals company** with expertise in areas including personal care ingredients and formulation technologies. :fn-ref{id="3"}

### Outcome

KleanK shared that cosmetic **formulations** generally consist of six types of ingredients: active ingredients, carrier ingredients, structural and emulsifying agents, sensory modifiers, fragrances, and preservatives. They also introduced us to **T/GDICST 001—2023, *Guidelines for the Verification of Cosmetic Stability Tests***, a cosmetics industry standard issued by the Guangdong Society of Cosmetic Science and Technology.

[Cosmetic stability test guidelines — Chinese version](https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6133/wiki/hp/ihp/human-practices-stage-4-cosmetic-stability-guidelines-zh.pdf#page=1&view=Fit)

[Cosmetic stability test guidelines — English version](https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6133/wiki/hp/ihp/human-practices-stage-4-cosmetic-stability-guidelines-en.pdf#page=1&view=Fit)

It provided standardized technical guidance on several aspects of cosmetic stability assessment, including **packaging compatibility**, **photostability** and **microbiological stability**.

Regarding **packaging materials**, as KleanK primarily works with conventional aluminum-based deodorants, they recommended that we **seek advice** from companies experienced in **protein-based cosmetic products** to identify suitable packaging that could help ensure the activity and stability of our engineered proteins.

KleanK also emphasized the importance of protecting our AI-designed proteins and recommended developing a **patent strategy** covering the protein sequence and potential variants to reduce the risk of circumvention.

The discussion also covered **marketing considerations**. For instance, **online e-commerce** is currently a major sales channel for deodorant products **in China**, with **Douyin** accounting for a particularly **large proportion** of online sales, suggesting that short video content could be an important marketing channel.

Furthermore, KleanK emphasized the importance of **consumer privacy**. For example, their company had developed **peel-off labels** that allow users to remove visible product information, giving them greater discretion when using deodorant products. This may reflect consumers' desire to **avoid drawing attention** to their deodorant use, perhaps due to concerns about being perceived as having unpleasant underarm odor. Given the prevailing **negative attitudes toward underarm odor**, individuals may feel **embarrassed** about their condition and seek to avoid others discovering it.

Evonik **expressed interest** in our mechanism-based approach to odor control and **provided technical support** to help us consider its potential development for practical applications. However, due to confidentiality considerations, we are unable to disclose further details of our discussions.

### Refine

Based on the insights provided by KleanK, we began designing the formulation of our own product based on the six major types of ingredients typically found in cosmetic formulations. We also conducted [thermostability testing for our proteins](/engineering/cgtase#cgtase-cycle-2) inspired by KleanK's provided cosmetic stability standards.

To address **regulatory and intellectual property** considerations, we began preparing for future applications for novel cosmetic ingredients and [patent protection](/human-practices/stage-4-optimising-product#cycle-4-patent-expert). For **a novel cosmetic ingredient registration**, we reviewed the relevant regulatory requirements and started organizing the technical documentation and sequence data needed for submission. For our **AI-designed protein patent protection,** we also considered potential sequence variants as part of our patent strategy, with the aim of securing broader protection for our design.

Furthermore, we incorporated **positive and supportive messages** into our product packaging to promote a more accepting perspective on underarm odor. To accommodate both our values and practical marketing considerations, these messages were designed as **peel-off elements**,  as suggested by KleanK, allowing consumers to choose whether to engage with them while keeping the main packaging simple and marketable.

For [**marketing**](/entrepreneurship#marketing-strategies), we plan to collaborate with influencers and promote our product on social media platforms such as Douyin to reach consumers in the Chinese market. At the same time, we recognized that marketing should not create additional anxiety or shame around underarm odor. We therefore plan to develop our promotional content around a **comfortable** and **inclusive** user experience. Furthermore, we recognized the importance of public education in improving understanding of underarm odor and fostering greater acceptance of people who use deodorant products.

## Cycle 2 Skincare Product Expert and Cosmetic Formulation Expert

### Observe

In our second meeting with KleanK, we raised several questions regarding the design, formulation, and packaging of protein-based cosmetic products.

However, as their expertise primarily lies in conventional aluminum salt-based deodorants, they suggested we consult stakeholders with more specialized expertise in **protein-based cosmetics**.

### Determine

Therefore, we consulted **skincare product expert** **Ms. Zhu** to understand how protein-based ingredients could be incorporated into a cosmetic product, their **stability** in the underarm environment, potential **safety concerns**, and considerations for **large-scale production**.

Additionally, we interviewed **Mr. Su Rongsheng**, a **cosmetic formulation expert** with over 35 years of experience in the industry, to obtain more specialized advice on the **formulation** and **packaging** of protein-based cosmetic products. We presented our preliminary formulation for his review and sought his advice on the packaging, storage, and testing required to develop our laboratory protein samples into a viable consumer product.

### Outcome

Mr. Su explained that a successful cosmetic formulation should balance four essential properties: **stability, safety, efficacy, and usability**.

In terms of **stability**, Ms. Zhu mentioned that in aqueous systems and emulsions, peptides can degrade easily through hydrolysis, particularly under extreme pH conditions, while high concentrations of ethanol in alcohol-based formulations can denature protein structures.

She also explained that **protein activity** can be affected by the conditions of the underarm environment. **Sweat**, **oil**, and changes in **pH** can all affect **protein folding** and reduce their function. In particular, skin and bacterial **proteases** may enzymatically **degrade peptides**, while fluctuations in sweat pH, which the expert identified as approximately 4.0–6.0, may cause proteins to **unfold or precipitate**. The expert therefore highlighted the importance of **protecting proteins from water and environmental stress** through approaches such as **liposomes**, **solid lipid nanoparticles**, or **microencapsulation**. Mr Su additionally recommended adding the active proteins only after the other ingredients had cooled to prevent heat-induced damage.

He also stated that **solubilizers** should only be used when necessary because they may destabilize proteins. **Water-soluble polymers and trehalose** may also help reduce protein aggregation, although their effects require experimental validation.

Finally, we presented him with our **proposed packaging materials** and asked for his assessment of **polypropylene (PP)**, which we selected as our preferred packaging material. He explained that PP is generally suitable for protein-based formulations when the material is of high quality and relatively pure, but emphasized that commercial plastics may contain additives and impurities that can affect formulation stability. He therefore recommended experimentally testing the compatibility of our formulation with PP packaging under conditions such as temperature cycling and light exposure.

On **efficacy**, both experts considered our three complementary strategies, the precursor binder, PepTSh binder, and engineered CGTase feasible and promising. Mr. Su, after viewing our proposed formulation, found our preliminary formulation broadly feasible as it included the major functional components typically used in cosmetic formulations.

He also advised against a freeze-dried format, as it is more complex to use and costly so may be less appealing to customers. Instead, he suggested a gel-based format, as it could distribute the proteins more evenly across the underarm and improve contact with odor-related molecules.

For **safety**, Ms Zhu recommended conducting skin irritation and toxicity tests for our active protein. She also emphasized avoiding a spray-based format, as proteins that have not undergone the relevant safety testing could pose a potential biological risk if inhaled into the lungs. She also noted that our AI-designed *de novo* protein could possibly be more prone to trigger immune or allergic responses and could be subject to stricter regulatory requirements than conventional cosmetic ingredients.

Finally, in the aspect of **usability**, Mr Su also noted that the thickeners we choose should provide a light, non-sticky skin feel.

### Refine

In terms of **stability**, we revised our proposed production process so that the active proteins would be added only after the other ingredients had cooled. For further formulation development, we decided to compare thickeners based on both protein stability and skin feel. We ultimately replaced Tween 20, Tween 60, and Tween 80 with CO40, as it can achieve terpene ester solubilization using the lowest possible amount of solubilizer, thereby minimizing potential interference with protein activity. In future development, we plan to carry out testing for:

1. Experimental validation of the effects of water-soluble polymers and trehalose on reducing protein aggregation

2. Packaging compatibility testing of PP under temperature cycling and light exposure to assess its compatibility with our formulation.

In the aspect of **efficacy**, our PepTSh binder candidate TB2 achieved a 26.70% reduction in thiol concentration, while our CGTase enzyme achieved a 46% inhibition rate against odor molecules generated from 2 mM precursor compounds within one hour. However, our precursor binder candidates achieved more modest reductions of approximately 7.9% to 11.6%. This highlighted the need to further optimize our precursor binders, prompting us to focus on improving their binding affinity in the [next engineering cycle.](/engineering/precursor-binder#precursor-binder-cycle-2)

We also eliminated the freeze-dried format, choosing a gel-based roll-on format instead.

For **safety**, based on Ms. Zhu's recommendations, we eliminated a spray based format as it posed a safety risk. We also identified skin irritation and toxicity testing as important for evaluating our active protein because AI-designed proteins may be more likely to cause immune or allergic responses and face stricter regulatory requirements.

On **usability**, we took skin feel into consideration when picking thickeners to help ensure comfortable application.

## Cycle 3 Immunology Expert

### Observe

During our interview with skincare expert Ms. Zhu, she raised concerns that our AI-designed *de novo* protein could potentially be **more prone to trigger immune or allergic responses** when used as a long-term topical ingredient.

### Determine

Therefore, we interviewed **Professor Yang Jianbo,** an immunology expert and researcher at the Hangzhou Institute of Medicine, Chinese Academy of Sciences. We consulted him to better understand the potential immunogenicity and sensitization risks of our AI-designed protein and identify appropriate methods for evaluating these risks.

### Outcome

Professor Yang explained that a *de novo* protein **does not necessarily have higher immunogenicity** simply because it is artificially designed. Instead, risks depend on factors such as its sequence and structure, T-cell and B-cell epitopes, similarity to human proteins, and tendency to aggregate.

He also highlighted that repeated topical exposure may lead to skin irritation, delayed hypersensitivity, or chronic inflammation; while shaving or friction may damage the axillary skin barrier and increase protein penetration and sensitisation risk.

For **dry lab safety** screening, he recommended combining multiple *in silico* prediction methods, including T-cell epitope prediction, similarity analysis against human proteins and known antigens, and protein stability prediction.

Computational predictions should then be **supported by wet-lab validation**, such as dendritic cell assays, T-cell proliferation assays, cytokine-release assays, and 3D skin models, to avoid random noise and model biases from AI models.

### Refine

Based on Professor Yang's advice, we conducted a more comprehensive [in silico safety screening](/safety-and-security#protein-safety) of our own *de novo* proteins. We integrated two prediction methods: **T-cell epitope prediction using NetMHCIIpan 4.3** and **sequence similarity-based allergenicity screening using AllergenOnline**. We then organised the results from different tools into a clear safety assessment table, allowing us to compare the potential risks of different protein candidates.

However, we were not satisfied with simply applying these tools to our own proteins. We wanted to make this safety-screening process **more accessible to the broader iGEM community**. We therefore plan to develop a **simplified workflow platform** that explains the key steps of *de novo* protein safety screening in simple language, from selecting appropriate prediction tools to interpreting their results. By simplifying this process, we hope to make protein safety assessment easier and more practical for future iGEM teams. Professor Yang also offered to provide wet-lab results to help us further validate the platform once it is operational.

In terms of wet lab validation, we plan to collaborate with BSL-2 labs to test the proteins' immunogenicity with in vitro human cells.

## Cycle 4 Patent Expert

### Observe

Inspired by our second meeting with KleanK, we began exploring **patent protection** for our AI-designed proteins. We sought to determine which aspects of our work could be applied for patent protection to prevent third parties from circumventing our claims through minor modifications to the patented proteins or their designs.

### Determine

Therefore, we interviewed **Mr Wang**, CTO of Link Spider, a synthetic biology company founded six years ago that has filed **20** patent applications, **7** of which have been granted.

### Outcome

The purpose of patents, stated by Mr Wang, is to **"support, collaborate with and accelerate inventors' and entrepreneurs' applications to secure intellectual property protections".**

Mr Wang explained that there were two patent strategies, **defensive** and **offensive**. A **defensive** **patent** is generally used for new technology. Developers can break down the invention's formulation into multiple patents. This creates a broad scope of protection and makes it difficult for third parties to copy the technology. In contrast, **offensive patents** target technical areas where competitors already exist. Applicants focus on identifying features that distinguish their inventions from existing patents, using these patents to establish a foothold in the market.

Mr Wang also introduced us to two patent examination tracks: the **prioritized examination track** and the **accelerated examination track**. Under the **prioritized examination track**, applicants can receive the first review within **6 months**. Its main benefit is a shortened timeline without overly strict examination standards, giving applicants enough time to prepare arguments and supplementary evidence across multiple rounds of responses. Comparatively, the **accelerated examination track** shortens the review period to **1–3 months**, allowing patents to be granted more quickly. However, the examination criteria are **extremely strict**, and the risk of rejection is high. If the application is rejected, its details may become publicly accessible, potentially limiting future protection.

Examiners assess patent applications based on **novelty** and **inventive step**. They first determine whether the same invention has already been disclosed in prior art. They then evaluate whether existing references provide sufficient technical motivation for a person skilled in the art to arrive at the proposed invention. In addition, the invention must demonstrate a meaningful technical effect to support its patentability. Minor improvements over existing technologies, such as a mere **10%** increase in performance, often fail to pass this standard.

We also inquired if our AI workflow qualifies for patent protection. Mr Wang stated that **AI parameters and workflows are hard to patent** because AI-generated outputs have uncertainty. Due to poor reproducibility of AI results, examiners may rule input parameters as non-claimable.

Finally, we discussed the **challenges** that rapid advances in AI pose to the current patent examination system. For example, tools such as ProteinMPNN can introduce minor changes to a protein sequence while preserving its key functional sites. Such modifications may reduce sequence similarity below the threshold covered by an existing patent, potentially allowing third parties to reproduce the protein's function while circumventing sequence-based patent claims.

### Refine

Based on the insights provided by Mr Wang, we **drafted and wrote core [patent documentation](/entrepreneurship#patent)** covering the DNA sequences, amino acids, and corresponding functions of our most effective protein candidates via the **accelerated examination track**.

## Cycle 5 AI4S Expert

### Observe

Following our discussion with patent expert Mr. Wang about the challenges of patenting AI-assisted processes, we sought to further explore the reliability and broader implications of AI-assisted scientific research. We wanted to assess the **feasibility** of our proposed [CDBTL (Characterize–Design–Build–Test–Learn) framework](/model), particularly how introducing an independent Characterize stage could improve the traditional **DBTL cycle. We also aimed to understand the** limitations and future development of AI for Science (AI4S), including the reliability of AI-generated results in real biological environments and how contributions should be fairly allocated among model developers, users, and experimental researchers.

### Determine

To explore these questions, we interviewed two professionals experienced in AI4S-related research, **Mr. Huang** and **Mr. Li.** We introduced our CDBTL workflow and discussed its feasibility, benefit allocation in AI-assisted research, and the limitations and future development of AI4S.

### Outcome

Mr. Huang **supported** our decision to introduce Characterize as an independent stage. He explained that **"the outputs of current AI models are still highly dependent on their training datasets"** and compared Characterize to **"preparing an appropriate set of prompts before using a large language model."** This highlighted the importance of clearly defining biological problems and design requirements before generating candidate proteins. However, he also emphasised that **"an exit mechanism and successful screening criteria are also very important."** If repeated adjustments fail to produce reliable structures, researchers should reconsider the feasibility of their approach rather than continuing indefinitely.

Regarding credit allocation, Mr. Huang believed that recognition should **depend on the actual contributions** of model developers, model users, and experimental researchers within each project. However, modern AI models may rely on data contributed by hundreds or thousands of researchers, making individual recognition difficult. He therefore suggested that **relevant laws and regulations still require improvement**, while **recognising contributions at the research-team level** may be more practical under current circumstances.

When discussing the limitations of AI4S, Mr. Huang pointed out that **"there may be a lack of sufficiently high-quality training data."** Many existing models rely on protein structures obtained through cryo-electron microscopy or X-ray crystallography, which may not fully represent proteins' dynamic behaviour in biological environments. This reinforced our understanding that computational predictions cannot replace wet-lab validation.

Mr. Huang also suggested adding an **additional structural evaluation step** to our workflow. Specifically, he recommended **calculating the RMSD between diffusion model generated structures and those produced by subsequent structure prediction models.** Large structural differences may indicate difficulties in protein expression or folding, potentially affecting the intended biological function.

### Refine

Based on the experts' feedback, we decided to further refine our CDBTL workflow by **introducing clearer exit criteria** and **quantifiable design standards**. These improvements will help us determine when candidate proteins should proceed to the next stage and when the current design should be reconsidered, reducing ambiguity and subjective judgement.

Following Mr. Huang's suggestion, we also recognised the value of **comparing designed and predicted protein structures** using RMSD as an additional assessment of structural consistency.

Finally, our discussions highlighted the unresolved challenges surrounding ownership and benefit allocation in AI-assisted research. We **recognised the importance of developing clearer regulations and guidelines** to ensure fair recognition of scientific contributions as AI continues to advance.

## Moving On

By the end of **Stage 4**, we had refined our formulation and product format, completed preliminary protein safety screening, and identified priorities for further testing. Expert guidance also helped us consider intellectual property protection and the responsible use of AI in our research.

However, developing a practical deodorant required more than technical refinement. Discussions about consumer privacy and odor-related insecurity encouraged us to consider the wider implications of our product. This led us to **Stage 5**, where we explored how our project could support psychological wellbeing while addressing environmental responsibility and accessibility.
