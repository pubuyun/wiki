**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We are the iGEM team from Shenzhen College of International Education. iGEM is a leading competition in synthetic biology. We started this project to participate in the competition, and our topic is controlling underarm odor.

Let me explain why we chose to target this particular odor-producing pathway. We learned from a paper that a major source of underarm odor is a thiol molecule abbreviated as 3M3SH. It produces a persistent, pungent, irritating, cumin-like smell commonly referred to as bromhidrosis. Its mechanism differs from ordinary sweat odor, which mainly comes from short-chain fatty acids produced by bacterial metabolism and has a sour smell.

This pathway mainly takes place in bacteria, but there is an earlier step: a human allele determines whether a certain precursor molecule is secreted. Bacteria, mainly Staphylococcus hominis, then metabolize the precursor through a series of steps to produce the odor molecule. Fundamentally, having the relevant allele means that the precursor may be secreted, leading to odor production. We want to address this persistent, genetically linked underarm odor.

We have proposed three approaches. The first is to design a protein binder that binds the precursor molecule. Once bound, the precursor cannot be recognized by the transporter or transported into the bacterium, so it will not be metabolized into the odor molecule. The second approach modifies the precursor so that the transporter cannot recognize it, preventing subsequent breakdown. The third directly designs a binder for the transporter, effectively acting as an inhibitor that blocks it from transporting the precursor. All three approaches therefore focus on precursor uptake.

The precursor is essentially a dipeptide with a distinctive side chain. We can use this side chain to achieve relatively high specificity when designing binders or enzymes.

The illustration for the first approach shows a protein binder binding the precursor, preventing its transport. In the second approach, we engineered an enzyme called CGTase. The general idea is to link the precursor to cyclodextrin, a type of sugar. The resulting product also cannot be recognized by the transporter. Finally, the transporter inhibitor was designed based on the transporter's conformational changes.

At our previous discussion, our experiments were not yet very far along. All three approaches have now made some progress. First, we have successfully expressed the precursor binders and observed some effects. We validate this by measuring free thiol content. In theory, the control without any binder should give a higher reading. Our results suggest that all the binders have some effect, but we will further optimize the validation methods and conduct a second round of protein optimization and iteration.

The engineered enzymes have also been expressed. We used point mutations to modify some binding sites so that the enzymes can recognize the precursor. Next, we will use thiol detection to validate their overall effects and consider further iterations.

The transporter binder has also been successfully expressed. We used a protein interaction assay to test whether it could bind to the transporter, and the results also showed some effect. We will continue optimizing both the experimental methods and the proteins in the next round.

We also want to produce terpene esters for fragrance through synthetic biology, and we have successfully produced bornyl acetate. These are the GC-MS spectra. Our results are broadly consistent with the reference standard, providing substantial confirmation that we produced this type of fragrance compound through fermentation. That is roughly where our experiments stand. We will continue optimizing the protein designs and experiments. Thank you.
