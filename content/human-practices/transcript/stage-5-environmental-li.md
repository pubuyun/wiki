**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think that, at present, achieving circular use still often remains at a theoretical level. Your idea is actually quite good. One thing to consider is the needs of your product. If it is something simple, such as a single plastic package, then of course it is relatively straightforward. If other functions are required, then you need to consider everything together. My current suggestion is still to focus on the material itself. For example, if the entire package uses aluminum, could new environmental risks arise during recycling? After recycling, could certain harmful substances accumulate and eventually be passed back to consumers? These are all issues that need to be considered. In terms of recyclability, aluminum is relatively good. Aluminum recycling is fairly convenient, and the processes for reprocessing and reuse are also mature. However, when recycling aluminum products, pure aluminum is relatively easy to recycle. In actual products, though, there are often spray coatings, plastic coatings, resins, and surface treatments such as anodizing, all of which increase the burden of recycling and treatment. For example, under normal conditions, if recycling involves high-temperature treatment or pyrolysis, plastic components may react at high temperatures and produce pollutants, potentially causing new environmental pollution. Therefore, all of these issues need to be considered.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

We previously learned that your main work involves monitoring volatile organic compounds, particularly in relation to air pollution, correct? So we would also like to ask some related questions. For example, if our materials use plastic or recyclable packaging, what types of volatile organic compounds are mainly emitted during their production processes?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

Plastic production itself can indeed generate a certain amount of volatile organic compounds. There are many different types of plastics, and different plastics use different raw materials and production processes. For example, some plastics are produced through monomer polymerization. During production, organic raw materials, solvents, or additives may be involved, and all of these can become sources of volatile organic compounds. In addition, various ingredients are added during the processing of plastic products. These may include processing aids and other additives, as well as coatings and adhesives used in surface treatment. These can also lead to VOC emissions.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

What about metallic aluminum, which we just mentioned? Could other volatile substances also be generated during the production of aluminum packaging?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

Yes. As I mentioned earlier, producing aluminum packaging does not simply involve using aluminum alone. There are usually coatings and possibly surface treatments as well. In many cases, the source of pollution is not necessarily the aluminum itself, but rather these surface-treatment materials, coating materials, or other organic components.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Suppose we do not use these treatments - for example, if we reduce spraying and resin use and directly use relatively pure aluminum packaging. Would that result in less pollution?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

In theory, yes, it would reduce pollution to some extent. However, you also need to consider the actual requirements of the product. If the aluminum receives no surface treatment at all and the product needs to come into direct contact with food or other contents, problems such as corrosion and substance migration may occur. If it is not handled properly, some substances may enter the product and ultimately be absorbed by the human body. So you still need to make decisions based on the actual market requirements of your product. Many products undergo surface treatment because of consumer needs, product stability, appearance, or other functional requirements.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Now let us return to the issue of the atmospheric environment. At present, do you think people underestimate the environmental impact of volatile organic compounds, or VOCs?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think they do. For example, when people talk about environmental pollution, especially air pollution, the first things that often come to mind are PM2.5 and particulate matter. But in fact, people may be overlooking VOCs. VOCs are important precursors in many secondary atmospheric pollution processes. They can participate in atmospheric chemical reactions and further influence pollution problems such as secondary particulate matter. Second, some volatile organic pollutants are directly toxic themselves. For example, some substances may be carcinogenic or may cause other adverse effects on human health. In addition, some volatile substances may have broader impacts on the atmospheric environment. Therefore, once VOCs are emitted into the environment, they can actually be associated with a whole range of environmental problems.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Based on your current understanding, are companies now actively controlling VOC emissions from factories? If they are, what measures do they generally take?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

Chinese companies, particularly in recent years, have been paying increasing attention to controlling VOC emissions. Relevant emission regulation and emission standards are also gradually improving. Many companies are required to install appropriate pollution-control facilities. Large companies in particular may install relatively advanced treatment equipment. During production, companies need to collect and treat pollutants according to the requirements of local governments and relevant emission standards, and then discharge them only after the relevant standards have been met.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

At present, what do you think will be the biggest core challenges for the industry in controlling VOC pollution in the future?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think there are several aspects. One is that precise monitoring and precise control of VOCs are relatively difficult because there are so many types of VOCs and their sources are highly complex. The second issue is economic cost. Pollution control requires consideration of both inputs and outputs. Some large companies may have the financial capacity to invest in treatment equipment. But for some small companies, or companies with relatively limited profit margins, this can be a major burden. Therefore, pollution control is not only a technical issue; it also involves a company's financial capacity. In addition, once policies are formulated, how they are actually implemented and enforced is also very important.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

I remember that one of our previous questions also mentioned that spray products use different propellants. Some traditional propellants may be associated with VOC emissions. We have learned that there is now a relatively new propulsion method: compressed-air spraying. This essentially uses compressed air, or another inert gas, as the driving force to expel the liquid from the container. If compressed air were used as the propellant in the future, would it provide significant environmental benefits?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

From an environmental perspective, I believe it has certain advantages. First, because you are using compressed air, it would generate fewer volatile organic compounds than some traditional organic propellants. Second, it may also reduce certain health risks. When volatile chemicals are reduced, the corresponding sources of exposure are also reduced. So from this perspective, I think it is a relatively good approach.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

In our previous questions, we also mentioned wastewater treatment systems. Suppose our protein product enters the municipal wastewater system through bathing. How efficiently can existing wastewater-treatment processes remove proteins?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

Municipal wastewater treatment plants already contain many microorganisms. For ordinary biodegradable proteins, the problem is usually not particularly serious because they can be degraded by microorganisms. However, if your protein is unusual - for example, if it is toxic to microorganisms, has antimicrobial properties, or is itself highly stable and difficult to degrade - then it could pose a significant challenge to existing wastewater-treatment systems. In particular, if it affects the original microbial communities in the wastewater-treatment system, then this issue would need to be assessed separately.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Suppose our protein actually enters the natural environment, for example a body of water. How should we carry out environmental monitoring?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

This issue should be considered before the product truly enters the environment on a large scale. In other words, during the product-development stage, you should already consider what will happen after it enters the environment. First, you need a detection method. At a minimum, you need to be able to determine whether the substance is present in the environment and what its concentration is. Then you should also carry out some simulation experiments to observe how it changes in the environment. For example, whether it degrades, how quickly it degrades, whether residues remain, and whether it affects organisms. Ideally, these methods should be established before the product is truly applied on a large scale.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

The next section of our questions mainly focuses on carbon emissions and sustainable development. Are there any common misconceptions in the carbon-reduction measures currently adopted by companies?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think one relatively common issue is that some companies may offset their own emissions by purchasing carbon allowances, carbon credits, or carbon sinks. But in reality, this does not truly reduce the emissions from the company's own production processes. In other words, the emissions may be offset in accounting terms, but the production process itself has not actually changed. So I think that if so-called carbon reduction is achieved only by purchasing these instruments, it does not mean that the company has genuinely reduced its emissions.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

From your professional perspective in environmental engineering, are there any genuinely effective carbon-reduction measures?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think you need to look at the entire production process. First, starting with raw materials, you should choose materials with lower environmental impacts. Second, in terms of production methods, you should consider how to reduce energy consumption and improve efficiency. Then, at the final treatment stage of the product, you also need to consider what measures should be taken. So you should not look at only one stage. The entire process should be considered, from raw materials and production through to final waste disposal.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

As we mentioned, our product is a protein produced using synthetic biology. If we want to demonstrate that it is environmentally friendly, what tests could we conduct or what relevant certifications could we apply for?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

First, if you want to demonstrate that the product is environmentally friendly, you need to carry out relevant tests. For example, whether the product causes pollution and what impacts it has after entering the environment. Second, if you want it to be regarded as a green and environmentally friendly product, you could also consider obtaining relevant environmental certifications. In addition, you could consider certifications related to cleaner production and green products. Overall, you cannot simply say that it is environmentally friendly; you need test results or certifications to support that claim.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Many antiperspirants currently on the market contain aluminum. Compared with traditional aluminum-containing antiperspirants, where do you think the greatest potential environmental advantages of a new biological product might lie?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think it still depends on the entire production method of your product. If the product is obtained through biological production methods, then you can first compare its environmental impacts in terms of raw materials and production processes. In addition, after the product is discarded, you also need to examine how it behaves after entering the environment. If your new product can reduce the use of certain traditional chemicals or metal components and is more easily degraded in the environment afterward, then that could be an advantage. But ultimately, this still has to be demonstrated with actual data. You cannot directly assume that a product must be more environmentally friendly simply because it is a 'biological product.'

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

I would also like to ask whether there is a unified industry standard for evaluating a product's environmental impact. For example, a rating system similar to energy-efficiency grades for electrical appliances?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

I think that would be very difficult. Environmental pollution is not a single-dimensional issue. For example, some substances may have little effect on human health but may affect the ecological environment. Some substances may not be highly toxic themselves, but they may be precursors of other pollutants. Therefore, it is difficult to evaluate environmental impact using a single unified indicator. I think at least several dimensions could be considered. One is the impact on human health. Another is the impact on the ecological environment. You can then further consider different environmental media such as air, water, and soil.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Our final questions mainly focus on the freeze-drying process and its environmental impacts. To preserve our protein product, we are currently considering making it into a freeze-dried protein for long-term storage. Could the production of freeze-dried protein create new environmental problems?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

First, freeze-drying consumes energy. Because you need to cool the product to a relatively low temperature and then carry out freeze-drying, energy consumption increases. The second issue is refrigeration equipment. Refrigeration equipment itself uses refrigerants. If the refrigerant used poses environmental risks, then a leak could affect the environment. In the past, some refrigerants were also associated with problems such as ozone-layer damage. In addition, if transportation and storage still need to be maintained at low temperatures, the cold chain will further increase energy consumption and carbon emissions.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

If we use aluminum cans to store freeze-dried protein while also providing reusable bottles that consumers refill themselves, what environmental advantages and potential limitations do you think this packaging model would have?

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

In terms of advantages, first, it can extend the life cycle of the packaging. If a bottle can be reused, it can reduce the use of single-use packaging materials. Second, because freeze-dried products are usually relatively stable, extending their shelf life may also reduce product expiration and waste. So from these perspectives, there are advantages. But there are also limitations. The first is consumer acceptance. Whether consumers are willing to refill and operate the system themselves is one issue. The second is whether it is convenient in actual use. If the process is too complicated, consumers may be unwilling to use it. In addition, you need to consider hygiene, contamination, and incorrect operation. If consumers repeatedly refill the container themselves and the container is not cleaned properly, new problems may arise. So in theory, this model can reduce packaging waste, but whether it is truly environmentally friendly ultimately depends on the entire system.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

I would like to add something briefly. Producing freeze-dried protein does indeed involve relatively low temperatures. However, in general, the storage stage does not necessarily require extremely low sub-zero temperatures. At present, we may be considering 2 to 8 degrees Celsius. Although refrigeration is still required at this temperature, the cost of cooling may not be as high as imagined. Furthermore, if we later optimize the stability of the protein, room-temperature storage may also become possible. So our main concern is whether the process of producing freeze-dried protein itself would create any environmental problems. At the moment, we have not studied the details very deeply. If necessary in the future, we may ask for your assessment again.

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

Yes. If room-temperature storage can be achieved, then from the perspectives of storage and transportation, it would indeed reduce a great deal of energy consumption. That would also be a very important advantage.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

Yes. So when considering packaging and product formats, we also want to know how we should calculate the pollution and environmental costs generated during production.

**Mr. Li Guanghui**

That would require relatively precise accounting. You need to consider the different stages, including production, transportation, storage, and sales. For example, if a product requires low-temperature storage, its energy consumption will differ across regions. In hot regions, more energy is needed to maintain low temperatures. But in relatively cold regions, the situation is different. Therefore, it is best to conduct a relatively comprehensive assessment of such environmental costs. You can also compare them with traditional products and traditional packaging methods, and then determine how much real benefit your improvements actually provide.
