**GB-SCIE2026**
What are the most common surgical treatments for axillary osmidrosis? What are their underlying principles and general procedures?

**Dr. Yang**
There are currently three main categories.

The first is a minimally invasive procedure—a small-incision sweat gland excision. We make a small incision of about 1-2 cm in the axilla, elevate the skin flap, and then directly excise or scrape away the apocrine gland tissue. This is considered a relatively reliable and effective surgical technique. As for the procedure itself, we first prepare the skin, then administer local anesthesia by injection. After that, the skin is incised, the skin flap is separated, and the apocrine sweat glands are removed. Finally, the incision is sutured and a pressure dressing is applied. One characteristic of this procedure is that no skin is removed. This is different from the third procedure. It is mainly suitable for patients with moderate to severe body odor who are seeking a definitive treatment. The recovery period is approximately two to three weeks. After about one month, most patients can resume normal daily activities. A key feature is that it uses a very small incision, causes minimal damage to the skin, and does not involve skin excision. We usually close the incision with cosmetic sutures to achieve a better aesthetic outcome. Pressure dressing is especially important because it helps prevent hematoma formation.

The second type is radiofrequency or microwave ablation. To explain it simply, it uses thermal energy to coagulate the apocrine glands. Heat is applied to damage, coagulate, and destroy the sweat glands. This is considered a physical treatment. The procedure usually involves topical anesthesia. After anesthetizing the puncture sites, a radiofrequency electrode is inserted through a targeted puncture point. Heat is then delivered to destroy the sweat glands. After the procedure, we usually provide cooling treatment with ice packs or cold compresses. The trauma is minimal. This method is suitable for patients with mild to moderate body odor. The first procedure was mainly for moderate to severe cases, whereas this one is more suitable for mild to moderate cases, especially patients who are concerned about scarring or hesitant about surgery. The recovery period is about one to two weeks. Since the procedure is less invasive, recovery is quicker and it generally does not affect daily work.

The third type is the spindle-shaped incision procedure. The first one involved a very small minimally invasive incision. The third procedure is the traditional method that was commonly used in the past, although it is rarely performed nowadays. With this approach, we make an incision along the natural skin folds of the axilla and directly excise a section of skin. A small amount of skin is removed-not a large amount, but enough that the procedure is significantly more invasive and results in more noticeable scarring. This technique is generally reserved for very severe cases, recurrent cases, or special circumstances. The recovery period is longer, usually more than one month.

For severe cases, we generally currently prefer minimally invasive gland excision. For mild to moderate cases, radiofrequency ablation can be used. Traditional spindle-shaped excision is now rarely performed.

Our department performs approximately 200-300 of these procedures each year. Personally, I perform around 100-150 cases annually. It is one of our basic surgical services.

**GB-SCIE2026**
Do you think the surgical outcomes are significant?

**Dr. Yang**
Regarding surgical outcomes, the traditional spindle-shaped incision procedure is now rarely performed. The procedure we mainly perform is the minimally invasive approach. For patients with moderate to severe body odor, it is currently the most effective and most significant treatment available. The success rate of sweat gland excision is approximately 90%.

**GB-SCIE2026**
Is the recovery period also the time it takes for the treatment to become effective?

**Dr. Yang**
For minimally invasive surgery, the effect is seen very quickly. Since the sweat glands are physically removed, the wound generally heals within one to two weeks. Because the glands have been removed, the reduction in odor is very obvious. For radiofrequency ablation, the effect usually becomes noticeable about one week after the procedure. Because fewer sweat glands are destroyed compared with surgical excision, the effectiveness may be slightly lower.

**GB-SCIE2026**
How satisfied are patients with the outcomes of these procedures?

**Dr. Yang**
Overall, among various types of surgery, patient satisfaction with these procedures is relatively high-around 85% to 90%. The small proportion of dissatisfied patients are usually those who still notice a slight residual odor after treatment. Others may be dissatisfied because of postoperative scarring, pigmentation, or darkening of the skin, which affects appearance. In some cases, inadequate postoperative care can lead to complications such as hematoma formation. Those situations may lower patient satisfaction somewhat.

**GB-SCIE2026**
Are there any other adverse effects that patients may experience after surgery?

**Dr. Yang**
Any surgery carries risks and the possibility of complications. Axillary osmidrosis surgery is no exception. There are several complications that may occur.

The first is hematoma formation. Hematomas are usually related to inadequate pressure dressing. If the dressing is not applied properly, postoperative bleeding may occur. The incidence is probably around 5-10%. In most cases, mild drainage and continued pressure dressing are sufficient, and the condition improves.

The second is infection. Postoperative infection is a potential complication of any surgery and requires preventive measures. The infection rate is approximately 3%. Because the surgical site is located in the axilla, which is a skin fold rather than a flat skin surface, and because there are bacteria, sweating, and other factors in that area, the risk of infection can be relatively higher.

Another possible complication is skin necrosis. This mainly occurs when the blood supply to the skin flap is affected during dissection. Combined with factors related to wound care and dressing changes, some patients may develop areas of skin necrosis. Although this is relatively uncommon, it is still seen occasionally. The probability of necrosis is quite low. Out of 100 cases, perhaps one case may experience it-around 1%.

Scarring is another common issue. To varying degrees, scars may occur in approximately 10-15% of patients. Since surgery involves an incision, some degree of scarring is normal, although the severity varies.

Finally, another relatively common issue is altered sensation. Abnormal sensation occurs because the skin flap dissection may damage the small nerves beneath the skin. When these nerve endings are disrupted, patients may experience changes in sensation, including touch, pain, and temperature perception. As time passes, these symptoms often improve because the nerve endings gradually regenerate and recover.

**GB-SCIE2026**
What kinds of postoperative care are required after surgery?

**Dr. Yang**
Postoperative care is mainly focused on preventing the complications we just discussed, so it is quite targeted.

For example, after surgery, pressure dressing is essential. It is usually maintained for three to five days to prevent hematoma formation and reduce complications. At the same time, patients should limit arm movement. With pressure dressing in place, patients naturally move less, which helps prevent displacement of the skin flap and reduces the risk of hematoma. The wound should also be kept clean and dry. Keeping the area dry is especially important because the axilla tends to sweat. Patients should avoid getting the wound wet and should return for regular dressing changes.

In addition, strenuous exercise should be avoided as much as possible. Patients should also avoid spicy and irritating foods, and they are generally advised to stop smoking and avoid alcohol. These are standard postoperative recommendations. After suture removal, which usually occurs around seven days after surgery, patients-especially female patients who are often particularly concerned about scarring-should take measures to reduce scar formation. Appropriate anti-scar products may be used if necessary. Avoiding strenuous exercise can also help reduce the risk of infection and recurrence.

**GB-SCIE2026**
When you say patients should keep the area dry, does that mean they cannot shower during this period?

**Dr. Yang**
Generally speaking, while the pressure dressing is in place and before the sutures are removed, patients should not shower normally. Even when washing, they should protect the area and avoid getting it wet. As long as the wound remains dry, that is sufficient.

**GB-SCIE2026**
If body odor returns after treatment, what secondary treatment options are available?

**Dr. Yang**
Recurrence can vary in severity. Procedures such as microwave and radiofrequency treatments generally have a somewhat higher chance of recurrence compared with surgery.

For mild recurrence, we usually recommend non-surgical options such as antiperspirants or topical treatments. Antibacterial agents may also be used to suppress bacterial growth. As we know, body odor results from secretions produced by the apocrine glands, which are then metabolized by bacteria on the skin surface. We can address this either by reducing gland secretion or by controlling sweating and bacterial growth. These measures can help reduce recurrence.

For moderate to severe recurrence, a different approach may be necessary. For example, if a patient previously underwent radiofrequency ablation, we may switch to minimally invasive sweat gland excision, which generally provides better results. If the patient previously underwent minimally invasive excision, we can perform an additional small-incision procedure to remove any remaining sweat glands.

We can also combine treatment with botulinum toxin injections. Botulinum toxin works by blocking sympathetic nerve signaling. Sympathetic nerves transmit signals through acetylcholine, which stimulates apocrine gland secretion. By injecting botulinum toxin, neurotransmitter release is inhibited, thereby reducing sweat gland activity and secretion.

For severe and persistent recurrence, the final option is surgical skin excision. In those very serious cases, we may return to the traditional approach and remove a portion of the skin.

**GB-SCIE2026**
What are the biggest pain points and limitations of current surgical and non-surgical approaches?

**Dr. Yang**
There are some similarities between the limitations of surgical and non-surgical treatments, but there are also important differences, so I will discuss them separately.

For non-surgical treatments such as antiperspirants and antibacterial products, the biggest issue is that they treat the symptoms rather than the root cause. They do not remove or destroy the apocrine glands. Instead, they simply suppress sweating or bacterial growth on the skin surface. Because of this, the effects are temporary, and symptoms can return quickly.

The second issue is that some products can be quite irritating. Some products may cause skin allergies, redness, itching, dryness, or pigmentation. In addition, for patients with moderate to severe body odor, these products are often not effective enough to meet social and lifestyle needs, especially for those who have high expectations for odor control.

As for the limitations of surgical treatments, they are somewhat different. First, surgery is invasive. It carries risks such as bleeding, infection, scarring, skin flap necrosis, and the other complications we discussed earlier. Second, there may still be some residual odor after surgery. Complete elimination is difficult to guarantee, although the results are generally much better than non-surgical treatments.

Third, surgery requires a recovery period. Patients need to restrict their activities during recovery. This can be inconvenient, especially for certain groups such as working professionals or students. Young people in particular tend to be more active, so recovery restrictions can be bothersome.

Another issue is scarring. Although the degree of scarring varies, surgery inevitably leaves some scars. Many patients, especially women, are very concerned about this. Therefore, I believe one of the key areas for improvement in surgical treatment is how to maintain a curative effect while reducing trauma, scarring, and associated risks.

**GB-SCIE2026**
From a surgeon's perspective, what requirements do you think a protein-based product like ours would need to meet in order to become an effective supplement or alternative to surgery?

**Dr. Yang**
I think your idea of a protein-based treatment is excellent and has great potential for development. The target of your approach is either the precursor molecules or the transporter proteins involved in odor production. In other words, the intervention occurs upstream in the process. Unlike treatments that reduce sweat gland activity by blocking sympathetic nerve signaling and acetylcholine transmission, your strategy works by interrupting the pathway earlier and preventing odor formation from occurring in the first place. I think this is a very promising concept and product.

To be honest, I am personally looking forward to seeing a product like this become available. As physicians, whether we perform surgery or other treatments, those interventions inevitably involve some degree of trauma. We do them because we want to relieve patients' suffering. In a sense, surgery is something we do because we have no better option. Doctors treat disease, but ideally there would be no disease to treat at all. The best situation would be one in which doctors become unnecessary because people no longer need treatment. That is, in a way, one of the ultimate goals and aspirations of medicine.

As for your protein-based approach, I think it is a very exciting concept. I believe it has strong scientific merit, excellent practical potential, broad applicability across different patient groups, and the advantage of leaving no scars. It would also be convenient to use. Overall, I think it is a very, very promising idea.

During the validation stage, are there any non-human methods we could use to evaluate whether the product might cause allergic reactions—for example, methods that do not involve human or animal testing?

**Dr. Yang**
I understand. The first thing to recognize is that side effects and allergic reactions vary greatly between individuals. Some people are allergic to pollen, some to eggs, some to milk, and some to fabrics. Everyone is different. Therefore, allergic reactions are not necessarily caused by a problem with the protein itself. The issue is not that the protein is inherently unsafe. Rather, the question is whether a particular individual will react to it.

As students, one possible approach would be computational modeling or large-scale data analysis. Computer simulations could certainly be useful. For example, if you know the exact composition of the protein you are designing, you can examine whether its components already exist in nature. If they do, that would make things easier. You could investigate whether those components have documented rates of allergic reactions within the general population. Relevant information and supporting evidence may already exist in databases or the scientific literature.

If the product already exists, then testing would not necessarily be a problem. As I mentioned earlier, allergic reactions generally do not involve life-threatening risks in this context. For example, when people use hair dye, the instructions often recommend applying a small amount behind the ear or in another discreet area first. If redness, swelling, itching, blisters, or other allergic reactions occur, then that person should not use the product. The same principle could be applied here. You could conduct tests and collect data from a certain number of samples—perhaps 100 or 200 cases-and then perform statistical analysis on the results.

I think the appropriate price depends first on the results of clinical testing and how long the product remains effective. For example, if I only need to use one unit of the product and that solves the problem, that is very different from a product that needs to be used continuously. The duration of use has a major impact on what price people are willing to accept.

If one or two units are enough to address the problem, then I think a price of around 150 RMB would be completely reasonable. Around 150 RMB would not be a problem at all. And I don't mean that one or two units would permanently solve the issue forever. For example, if I only need to use one unit every six months, then paying around 150 RMB each time would still be acceptable. That would amount to around 300 RMB per year, which I think is still reasonable.

At the same time, the product should not be too cheap either, because it would still be a specialized product. On the other hand, if it becomes too expensive, that may also create problems. For example, if a single unit costs five or six hundred RMB and one unit is not sufficient, requiring repeated use every six months, then patients may begin comparing it with procedures such as microwave treatment or surgery. I think around 150 RMB would be a relatively appropriate price point.
