**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

Does an entirely new AI-designed protein necessarily have a higher risk of immunogenicity or sensitisation? What factors should we pay the most attention to?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

This is currently one of the major concerns surrounding AI-designed proteins.

An AI-designed protein may be completely new and may not exist in nature, but this does not necessarily mean that it will have higher immunogenicity. Its immunogenicity could be higher or lower depending mainly on the sequence and structure of the protein and how these are optimised.

Protein immunogenicity is closely related to the recognition of T-cell epitopes and B-cell epitopes. Therefore, when designing a new protein, several factors should be considered.

First, we need to consider whether the protein contains epitopes that may be recognised by T cells. We should also consider its similarity to proteins naturally present in the human body.

Another important factor is the tendency of the protein to aggregate. During protein design, computational and AI-based tools can be used to optimise these characteristics and reduce potential immunogenicity as much as possible.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

For a protein that is applied to the skin every day over a long period of time, what are the main immunological safety risks?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

If this is a product that is repeatedly applied to the axillary skin, there are several risks that need to be considered.

The first is skin irritation and hypersensitivity. A person may not show an obvious reaction after the first exposure, but after repeated exposure or after several days, an inflammatory response may develop. In immunological terms, this may involve a type IV hypersensitivity reaction, which is a delayed response.

The second concern is the cumulative effect of long-term use. Repeated exposure over a long period may potentially lead to chronic inflammatory responses.

Therefore, both relatively short-term skin irritation and longer-term chronic inflammation should be considered during safety evaluation.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

Could damage to the skin barrier caused by shaving, friction, or other factors in the axillary area increase protein penetration and sensitisation risk?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Yes, it can.

The axillary area contains a large amount of hair, and hair removal may cause damage to the skin barrier. Once the skin barrier is damaged, the product or protein may penetrate more easily into deeper layers of the skin.

This could increase the possibility of hypersensitivity or allergic reactions.

The skin in the axillary area is also relatively sensitive, and physical friction may further damage the skin barrier. Therefore, skin barrier damage is an important risk factor that needs to be considered.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

If the protein undergoes degradation, denaturation, or aggregation because of sweat, changes in pH, temperature, or protease activity, could the resulting products have greater immunogenicity than the original protein? How should these changes be evaluated?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

This is certainly possible.

The axillary area is a relatively special environment. Its local temperature may be slightly higher than that of some other areas of the skin, which may affect the protein during long-term exposure.

The axillary area also contains many sweat glands. Sweat secretion can cause changes in local pH, which may influence protein stability.

In addition, there are many microorganisms in the axillary area. These microorganisms may produce proteases that can degrade the protein. The resulting degradation products may therefore differ from the original intact protein.

Protein degradation or denaturation may also result in protein aggregation. These aggregates or degradation products could potentially generate new forms of immune stimulation and alter the immunogenicity of the product.

To evaluate this, several types of stability experiments can be performed, including temperature-stress experiments, pH-stress experiments, exposure to sweat-like conditions, and exposure to relevant proteases.

These experiments can help determine the stability of the protein under conditions similar to those found in the axillary environment.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

If the protein is produced through recombinant expression, could substances other than the protein itself, such as endotoxins, host-cell proteins, or residual nucleic acids, also cause immune or allergic reactions? How can we distinguish reactions caused by the protein itself from those caused by production impurities?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Yes. If the protein is expressed in E. coli, for example, the final product may contain endotoxins, host-cell-derived impurities, residual DNA, components from the culture medium, or other contaminants.

Immune reactions caused by these contaminants are different from immune reactions caused by the target protein itself. If these impurities are not properly controlled, they may create false-positive immune responses during testing.

Therefore, these impurities should be minimised as much as possible during production and purification.

Protein products need to meet appropriate quality-control standards. Protein purity should be high, and endotoxin levels should be kept within strict limits.

Other possible contaminants, including host-cell proteins, DNA, residual antibiotics, metal ions, and other substances, should also be controlled during the production process.

By controlling these impurities, it becomes easier to determine whether an observed immune response is genuinely caused by the designed protein itself.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

During the dry-lab design stage, we may compare the designed protein with known allergens or human proteins. How reliable are these prediction methods for de novo proteins?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

At present, multiple computational approaches can be used to evaluate these risks.

One of the most important is T-cell epitope prediction. There are several different tools available for this purpose, so I would recommend using multiple tools rather than relying on only one.

If three or four different algorithms produce similar results and reach a consistent conclusion, then the prediction can be considered more reliable than the result from a single method.

Similarity to human proteins can also be evaluated by searching against human protein databases. Known antigen sequences can also be used for sequence comparison.

These methods are useful, but their reliability cannot be considered perfect. At present, they can generally provide a moderate to relatively high level of confidence.

The physicochemical stability of the protein can also be predicted computationally.

However, these AI and computational tools are still continuously developing. Using multiple algorithms to cross-check the results can improve reliability, but experimental validation is still essential.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

During the wet-lab stage, what methods could be used to validate the immunogenicity and sensitisation risk of an AI-designed protein?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Even if a protein has been evaluated using multiple AI and computational tools, it ultimately still needs to be validated experimentally.

The first level is in vitro cell-based testing. These experiments are relatively fast, relatively low-cost, and can be performed at a relatively high throughput.

For immunogenicity, there are several important experiments.

The first is a dendritic cell maturation assay. This can be used to evaluate whether the de novo protein activates antigen-presenting cells.

Another is a dendritic cell uptake assay. This evaluates whether the protein can be taken up by antigen-presenting cells before being processed and presented.

T-cell proliferation assays can also be used to determine whether the protein causes specific activation and proliferation of T cells.

Cytokine-release assays can be used to evaluate whether exposure to the protein causes the release of immune-related cytokines.

For proteins that may have allergic effects, mast-cell activation assays may also be considered, although these may be used less frequently than the other assays.

Another important level of evaluation involves 3D models or organoid-like systems. These are closer to the real tissue environment than conventional two-dimensional cell culture.

For topical products, reconstructed 3D skin models can be used for in vitro skin irritation and sensitisation testing.

Full-thickness 3D skin models containing additional cellular components, including immune-related cells, may provide an even more complete system for evaluation.

Finally, animal experiments may be considered when necessary. These could include skin toxicity, skin sensitisation, or phototoxicity testing.

Overall, product safety can be evaluated at several levels: cell-based assays, 3D skin or tissue models, and, when necessary, animal experiments.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

What information should be provided with the product to help consumers reduce the risk of allergic reactions? For example, should we include potential allergy warnings or recommend a local skin test before use?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

For a product intended for long-term topical use, the information provided to consumers is very important.

Before using the product, consumers should be informed that the active ingredient is a newly designed protein that may not exist naturally and that adverse skin reactions may still be possible.

A local patch test could also be recommended before regular use.

For example, a small amount of the product could first be applied to an area such as the arm or behind the ear rather than directly to the axilla. After a certain period of exposure, the product could be washed off, and the area could then be observed for approximately 24 to 48 hours.

If redness, swelling, or other adverse reactions occur, the user should stop using the product.

The product information should also clearly list its ingredients, including the active ingredient and other components such as preservatives, fragrances, and excipients.

The instructions should specify how often the product should be used and how much should be applied each time.

Users should also be warned to avoid contact with the eyes and mucosal surfaces.

The suitable user population should also be clearly defined. For example, if safety has not been established for children or pregnant individuals, this should be stated clearly.

The product should also not be applied to damaged or broken skin.

Overall, the key information should include pre-use warnings, product composition, recommended frequency and dosage, suitable user groups, and contraindications.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

If the protein forms a complex with the target bacterium or with the precursor molecule, could this complex produce a different immunological response from the free protein?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Yes, this is possible.

If the designed protein interacts with bacterial components, metabolic products, or precursor molecules, it may form a new complex or molecular form.

This new form could potentially increase immunogenicity, but it could also reduce immunogenicity. Without experimental evidence, we cannot know in advance which direction the effect will take.

The assays discussed earlier can be used to evaluate this, including dendritic cell maturation assays, dendritic cell uptake assays, T-cell proliferation assays, and cytokine-release assays.

These experiments can help determine whether the new complex produces stronger or weaker immune stimulation compared with the original free protein.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

Could processing, storage, or mixing the protein with preservatives, fragrances, or other cosmetic ingredients change its immunogenicity?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Yes. This is similar to the issue of protein degradation discussed earlier.

Preservatives, fragrances, surfactants, changes in pH, metal ions, temperature changes during transportation, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles may all affect protein stability and may cause protein denaturation.

Once the protein is denatured, its immunological properties may change. This could potentially create new immune responses or allergic reactions, although in some cases it may also reduce immunogenicity.

Therefore, formulation compatibility and protein stability should be evaluated experimentally.

When developing the formulation, it would also be preferable to select excipients that are relatively compatible with the protein and to avoid unnecessary components that may increase protein denaturation or irritation.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

For a topical protein product used in the axillary area, under what circumstances should we be concerned about the protein entering systemic circulation?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Under normal circumstances, the probability of a topical protein entering systemic circulation is relatively low, but it cannot be completely excluded.

The process would generally begin with local penetration through the skin.

The risk may increase when the skin barrier is damaged. The axillary area contains many hair follicles and sweat glands, which may provide potential routes for local penetration.

Once the protein enters deeper local tissue, it may first cause a local immune or inflammatory response.

From there, it could potentially reach the regional lymphatic system and, in rare cases, eventually enter systemic circulation.

Therefore, this can be evaluated at three levels: local, regional, and systemic.

At the local level, skin inflammation can be monitored.

At the regional level, changes in draining lymph nodes can be evaluated.

At the systemic level, blood-based measurements can be used to assess immune responses, including routine blood parameters and immunological indicators such as IgG, IgE, and eosinophils.

Although systemic exposure is generally expected to be uncommon, it is still important to monitor the local, regional, and systemic levels to determine whether the protein remains local or causes broader immune effects.

**GreatBay-SCIE 26**

You mentioned several AI and computational approaches for predicting protein properties, including physicochemical stability, similarity to known antigens, similarity to human proteins, and other characteristics.

Is there currently a platform that integrates multiple prediction methods and provides a combined confidence result, or do researchers generally need to submit the sequence to different tools and manually compare the results?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

Integrating different prediction methods is something that many researchers are currently working on.

There are tools for T-cell epitope prediction, protein similarity analysis, comparison with human proteins, and prediction of protease cleavage sites, among other functions.

Some protein-design platforms can already perform several of these analyses within the same workflow.

However, the reliability of the final results still requires human interpretation and adjustment.

Most importantly, computational prediction still needs to be validated experimentally.

No matter how complete or ideal an AI prediction appears to be, for a newly designed protein that may not exist in nature, whether it can actually be safely used still needs to be determined through experiments.

Therefore, combining computational prediction with experimental validation is very important.

We are considering building a protein immunogenicity prediction platform that integrates multiple existing tools. Other teams could submit a protein sequence once, and the platform would automatically run different prediction methods, compare the results, and provide an integrated output. Do you think building such a platform would be valuable?

**Professor Yang Jianbo**

I think this would be very valuable.

If you are able to use AI tools effectively and integrate several existing prediction functions into one platform, this could be a very useful project.

If you are able to complete some preliminary development, there may also be opportunities to combine the computational work with laboratory platforms to perform initial experimental validation.

If dry-lab prediction and wet-lab validation can be connected, the platform could become even more useful and provide stronger support for evaluating newly designed proteins.

I think this is a very meaningful direction.
