**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Hello, Dr. Xia. We are students from Shenzhen College of International Education participating in iGEM. We plan to develop a synthetic biology project to address underarm odor. Before we begin, we would like to confirm the privacy arrangements: do you consent to us taking notes and making audio and video recordings of this interview?

**Dr. Xia**
No problem.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
All questions and answers will be used only for our GreatBay-SCIE 2026 iGEM project this year, without commercial use. Would you be willing to have the interview content, your image, and your institutional affiliation presented on our team's final website? The website will introduce the people who helped with the project. Thank you.

Let us introduce the project. We understand that a key odor molecule in underarm odor is 3M3SH, produced when underarm staphylococci metabolize an odorless precursor secreted by the human body. We currently have three approaches. First, we design protein binders that specifically bind the precursor. Second, we engineer enzymes to attach the precursor to a larger molecule so that it cannot enter the bacteria. Third, we design transporter inhibitors that specifically bind to transport proteins on the bacterial cell membrane.

We have also found some consumer demand for fragrance, so we are considering adding terpene esters to provide a scent. This interview will cover five main areas.

First, target groups. What is the formal medical name for genetically linked underarm odor? Is there a specific term?

**Dr. Xia**
The common Chinese term is huchou, while medically we generally call it axillary bromhidrosis. It would be considered a skin condition. Strictly speaking, though, it does not really affect physical health; its main effects are social anxiety and difficulties in social interactions.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
So it is still considered a dermatological condition. Does it present differently between individuals? For example, can age, sex, race, or ethnicity affect how noticeable the odor is?

**Dr. Xia**
Yes, they can. From the people we encounter, Black people generally have more noticeable body odor and commonly use scented products. To us, the resulting smell is not necessarily pleasant because it is not simply fragrance, but fragrance mixed with body odor. The fragrance dilutes the unpleasant odor, but the combination may still be unpleasant.

White and Asian populations also experience it. There are certainly individual and population differences, but not everyone has it. Some Asian people have it and others do not; the same is true of White people.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Among the patients you usually see, are differences by sex and age particularly noticeable?

**Dr. Xia**
We see quite a lot of cases. I mainly work in dermatology and cosmetic dermatology. Clinically, I do not see an obvious difference between men and women, although I do not have statistics here.

For age, a parent recently brought in a four- or five-year-old child. I found that unusual because the child had not developed much yet, and the odor was indeed mild on examination. At the other end, people in their forties and fifties also come for surgery. Patients aged fifteen to twenty-five are relatively common, and there are also quite a few in their thirties and forties. Overall, those aged fifteen to thirty are relatively more common.

I do not think there is an obvious sex difference. Inheritance is not limited to one sex either. If the mother has it, not all her children necessarily will. It is not a sex-limited condition.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Do you grade the severity during consultations? Are there grading criteria?

**Dr. Xia**
Yes, there are grading criteria. Mild cases generally require close proximity to detect the odor. Sometimes it is not noticeable at a normal social distance. We may collect a little sweat with a cotton swab and smell it; that would be a relatively mild case.

Moderate cases release an odor with slight movement. For example, if you are sitting within about a meter, it becomes fairly noticeable when the person moves. Severe cases produce a strong smell in the surrounding area, sometimes quite pungent. It becomes obvious as soon as the person comes near you.

Overall, assessment is mainly based on odor. We also ask about the history, such as whether the parents or other family members have it. Special laboratory tests are generally unnecessary.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
So the doctor mainly assesses it by smelling it directly?

**Dr. Xia**
Mainly by smell, with some visual examination too. Some people may have discoloration or colored traces on their underarm hair, but that alone is not enough to make a judgment. The main assessment is still the odor.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Are there any common misconceptions about underarm odor among patients who come to see you?

**Dr. Xia**
I think clear misconceptions are relatively uncommon now. People who come for a consultation usually care about the issue. We are also in the information age, and they can learn about it through tools such as DeepSeek and Doubao, so they already have some understanding.

They usually try washing or buying topical products before asking whether there is a permanent treatment. In the past, people might have used folk remedies. Now they are relatively better informed, so I do not think obvious misconceptions are particularly common.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
You mentioned that patients use deodorants. Based on their feedback or your own knowledge, which products are relatively useful?

**Dr. Xia**
I have seen products such as Xishilan Xialu and Huchoujing. Some may contain traditional Chinese herbal ingredients, while others contain different ingredients. However, the ingredients and claims of some products are not particularly clear. They are also sold in pharmacies.

Quite a few people develop contact dermatitis and discomfort after using them. Some also disinfect the area directly with alcohol, thinking that it both disinfects and has a smell of its own.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
What factors do patients usually consider when seeking treatment at a hospital, such as price or side effects?

**Dr. Xia**
Do you mean surgery specifically, or their overall considerations when seeking medical care and treatment?

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Their overall considerations when seeking care and treatment.

**Dr. Xia**
First, the main problem is odor, so that is what they most want to resolve. Many hope for a permanent solution so they will not need products afterward and can live like everyone else, without being looked down on or feeling embarrassed.

Beyond completely removing the odor, if they undergo surgery, they want no scar or as little scarring as possible. The smaller the incision, the better, ideally with no scar. Some ask whether a topical medicine could solve it, but they generally understand that a permanent cure through topical application alone is unlikely.

Besides surgery, we use injections. For example, sclerosing agents cause a local reaction that closes the relevant sweat glands or ducts. Botulinum toxin injections reduce sweating, making sweat less likely to reach the surface and therefore reducing opportunities for bacterial breakdown.

There are also microneedling and microwave methods. Microwave treatment is noninvasive and aims to heat the local apocrine glands to around sixty degrees Celsius while minimizing damage to the epidermis. Microneedling is invasive and also works through heating.

Prices differ. Microwave treatment is relatively expensive, around RMB 20,000-30,000, while microneedling may cost close to RMB 10,000. But the most expensive method is not necessarily the most effective.

Surgery can be performed under direct visualization or without direct visualization. We try to minimize the incision, but discuss with patients whether they care more about thorough removal or avoiding scars. If avoiding scars is especially important, topical products or injections can be considered. If they want the most thorough removal possible, operating under direct visualization is a relatively effective approach.

Some methods use a very small incision and instruments such as a rotary cutter, curette, or so-called water jet. Some people online say that because the skin has been made very thin, the removal must be complete. But if you cannot see, you cannot be so certain. Some areas may have been adequately treated, while others may have been missed.

There are now methods using a small incision with endoscopic assistance, but visibility is not necessarily very clear. By comparison, turning the tissue over and treating it under direct visualization makes removal more straightforward. Every method has advantages and disadvantages, including microneedling, microwaves, medication, injections, and potentially your future research-based products.

Some treatments have short-term effects. For example, with methods that work by closing ducts, the ducts may reopen later, requiring repeated treatment and reinforcement. Botulinum toxin generally lasts around three or four months. Its effect usually starts to wear off after about three months, and sweating resumes.

I am less familiar with other medicines and the various topical products.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We have discussed surgery, botulinum toxin, sclerosing agents, microwaves, and other methods. Botulinum toxin requires repeated treatment, and our protein product may also need continued use. In comparison, what advantages do you see in our product? Are there improvements we could make or risks we should consider?

**Dr. Xia**
Botulinum toxin requires injections, is relatively expensive, and only lasts three or four months. People can be afraid of injections, especially because it is not just one injection but many separate injection sites. Even brave people may fear needles, so a topical product would have an advantage.

If it also has a price advantage, that would be even better. Even without a clear price advantage, some people would prefer a topical product. If applying your product could provide three or four months of benefit, and it really achieved that, some people might not need surgery. But I think reaching that effect could be difficult.

The advantage of a noninvasive topical product is clear, especially for younger people. Some children develop a noticeable odor by twelve or thirteen because of heredity, but are not yet well suited to surgery. We generally operate on patients aged seventeen or above. In younger children, some glands have not developed yet. Even if developed glands are treated, other glands may develop later.

A topical product could be better in this situation. Children may be very frightened of injections, and some cry out intensely. So the advantages are clear, but the main question is whether the effect can be achieved: will the protein break down easily, wash off after application, or remain effective under external environmental conditions? If it could truly last as long as injections, it would certainly be a very good product. Three or four months would be a substantial advantage.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
You mentioned that sweat and everyday washing might remove the protein product. What other aspects of the underarm skin environment could affect its effectiveness and stability?

**Dr. Xia**
There are certainly others. For example, the local pH varies: some skin is mildly acidic and some more acidic. Moisture levels also differ. Some people sweat particularly heavily, some wear thick clothing, and everyone has different habits.

Moisture, high temperatures, and pH can all affect it. At certain temperatures, proteins may denature and gradually lose activity. More sweat may also dilute the product or carry it away, reducing the effective concentration and affecting performance.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Our protein product differs from conventional aluminum salts, which work by blocking sweat glands. What safety requirements should it meet when applied to the underarms?

**Dr. Xia**
Strictly speaking, nothing can be guaranteed never to cause an allergic reaction in anyone. Potential allergy and irritation are possible. Some people are allergic to eggs, others to alcohol or shrimp; individuals differ. The same product may cause no reaction in one person but an allergic reaction in another.

Any product therefore has some potential for skin irritation; the probability just varies. Sensitization, irritation, cytotoxicity, and long-term safety all need consideration. You also need to consider whether a protein product could be absorbed and whether it could cause liver or kidney toxicity, although absorption may be very limited. Your product design and accompanying information need to address these issues too.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Based on your knowledge of existing products, which formats, such as roll-ons, sprays, or dressings, would best suit a protein product and patients' habits?

**Dr. Xia**
Roll-ons and sprays are relatively convenient. A roll-on stays in close contact with the underarm skin and could work well if it is nonirritating and provides a lasting effect. A spray is convenient when someone is rushing to work: lift the arm slightly, spray, and leave. It is easy to use and feels relatively dry.

Patches tend to be less breathable and may have lower acceptance, although that depends on the material and design. More advanced materials that manage moisture while remaining breathable could also work well, similar to materials that allow water to pass through rather than being completely sealed.

Sprays have drawbacks too. The product may transfer to clothing, reducing how long it lasts. Convenience, breathability, and duration all need to be considered together.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We may need to consider how to keep the ingredients on the skin surface.

**Dr. Xia**
If they can stay in place without making the area feel unventilated, while remaining comfortable, that would certainly be better. That requires technological support.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We mentioned that proteins might cause sensitization. We have also learned that some treatments or topical products can cause itching or underarm darkening. How could a protein product be optimized to avoid these side effects?

**Dr. Xia**
If the protein product does not undergo further breakdown or additional chemical reactions and simply remains on the skin surface, it is theoretically like wearing a layer of clothing. If it contains no hormones, I think it would be unlikely to induce pigment production or pigmentation.

As for irritation, it depends on whether the product suits most people and has a low sensitization potential. Appropriate tests would certainly be needed, such as human testing or patch testing, to observe whether skin contact causes irritation.

You could also design a simple preliminary-use procedure in which users apply a small amount to a small area of skin, observe for irritation or allergy, and then decide whether to use it. Because individuals differ, the instructions could advise a small-area trial first, with an observation period specified according to the product's requirements, to reduce unexpected adverse reactions as much as possible.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Next are the market and future prospects. We have discussed many options at different prices. If our product requires long-term use, what price range do you think patients would find easier to accept?

**Dr. Xia**
Yours is a high-tech product. If we consider it alongside cosmetics or ordinary topical skincare products, a price from around RMB 50 to a little over RMB 100, perhaps around RMB 120, might not feel too costly to use.

My current understanding is that it would not provide a long-lasting effect after just one application, but would require repeated use, perhaps daily or every few days. If one application could last a month, two months, or even several months, that would of course be better. The longer it lasts and the better it works, the higher the price could be.

For a product that needs daily use, keeping the price below RMB 100 would generally be preferable. Some functional products sell for RMB 150 or RMB 200-300, but more than RMB 300 may feel expensive to Chinese consumers. Overall, a price below RMB 200 would be relatively easier to accept, although it still depends on how long each application lasts.

If it is used daily, it is best not to make it too expensive. If it feels comfortable, works for a long time, and completely removes the odor, price acceptance may differ. For fragrance, I think a light scent would be suitable and pleasant, rather than a heavy fragrance mixed with and masking body odor. Personally, I find that mixture of fragrance and body odor uncomfortable.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Next, we would like to ask about fragrance substances. Some people use perfume to mask body odor. Have you seen patients with skin problems caused by perfume, fragrance ingredients, or other scented substances?

**Dr. Xia**
Certainly. Cosmetics can cause contact dermatitis, and fragrance substances themselves may be irritating. Just because something provides a scent does not mean it will cause no reaction when sprayed onto the body. Contact with the skin can certainly trigger an allergy. We see many cases of cosmetic dermatitis.

There are also many commensal bacteria on the skin. If a product changes their ecological or physiological environment, it may affect the skin microbiome. Such changes may contribute to impaired barrier function or infection risk. So, besides sensitization, you need to consider effects on the microbiome and skin barrier.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We surveyed consumers about whether they wanted fragrance. After learning that some perfumes or fragrance ingredients might cause skin problems, we considered using terpene esters to provide a scent. Could their antibacterial effects disrupt the balance of the underarm microbiome?

**Dr. Xia**
If they have broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, they may affect normal bacteria as well as harmful ones. Our bodies have normal microbiota. For example, the gut contains bacteria involved in vitamin production, as well as pathogenic bacteria. Broad-spectrum activity does not distinguish between beneficial and harmful bacteria, so long-term use may disrupt the normal microbial balance.

At lower doses, the effect may be smaller; at higher doses, it may be greater. You need to consider how to control the concentration and minimize the impact on the microbiome.

On fragrance, I have another thought. If your product really eliminates the odor, why must you add fragrance to the underarms? Previously, when better products were unavailable and the problem could not be fully resolved, fragrance was needed to mask or counteract it. If the unpleasant odor is genuinely gone, is added fragrance necessarily needed?

Once there is no body odor, users can freely choose other scented products and apply them wherever they like. The fragrance does not necessarily have to be part of the deodorant. That is how I see it.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Our questionnaire asked whether people still wanted fragrance after odor removal and, if so, which scent they preferred. Around 40% wanted the product to have no scent and simply remove the unpleasant odor. Other groups of approximately 30% and 20% selected floral or woody scents.

We may therefore offer an unscented option without terpene esters, alongside options containing these substances for people who want fragrance.

We also wanted to know whether terpene esters are commonly used on the skin, particularly the underarms.

**Dr. Xia**
To be honest, in clinical practice we rarely directly tell patients to buy topical products. When they come to the hospital, we more often discuss treatments such as microneedling, microwaves, lasers, injections, and surgery. We have not conducted a specific survey of which products they buy themselves, so I have not investigated in depth how commonly these substances are used.

However, I think designing different products for different groups is the right approach. Another question is whether your survey targeted people with underarm odor or everyone. Would the results change if you surveyed only people affected by the problem? Designing options according to different needs is a sensible direction.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Have you seen other skin reactions or skin conditions after long-term use of terpene ester products?

**Dr. Xia**
As I said at the beginning, theoretically no product can be guaranteed to have zero sensitization potential. The difference is in the probability. Why is skin testing performed for penicillin, for example? Because it is relatively likely to cause an allergy. Some medicines, such as erythromycin, generally do not require skin testing because allergies are less common, but that does not mean they are impossible. Gentamicin can also cause allergic reactions, including severe ones.

You cannot assume that a product is completely nonallergenic just because allergy testing is not required. The situation you describe is certainly possible; it is a question of how likely it is.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Regarding allergy, you mentioned trying a small amount on another area of skin to observe whether the product causes a reaction.

However, our competition has a restriction: as a high school team, we are not allowed to conduct human experiments, and the microorganisms we can use are also limited. Under these conditions, are there other approaches we could use during protein design to avoid or reduce allergic side effects?

**Dr. Xia**
You do not necessarily need specialized patch-testing materials. Since you are developing a product, its instructions could tell users to apply a small amount to one area first and observe whether there is any reaction before using it.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
So you mean informing them in the instructions beforehand?

**Dr. Xia**
Yes. Try a small amount in one area, observe for discomfort, and then apply it to the intended area. That would be better.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Thank you, Dr. Xia. That covers most of our questions today. Is there anything else you would like to add about our project or this interview?

**Dr. Xia**
Overall, underarm odor still troubles many people, and demand for treatment is high. Particularly in summer, people often queue for surgery. If you can develop an accessible product that meets people's needs, it could relieve a great deal of everyday distress and embarrassment. That would be very meaningful.

Ultimately, underarm odor is linked to genetic factors. Apocrine gland secretions reach the skin surface and are broken down by local bacteria, producing substances with distinctive, pungent smells.

My understanding of your technology is that it binds the relevant molecules first or interferes with their transport, preventing bacteria from carrying out subsequent reactions with them. It is something like a competitive approach.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Yes.

**Dr. Xia**
I think this is an imaginative, high-tech product that could be well received. You will need to work through the details gradually to make it more scientifically sound and suitable for the public. As a dermatologist, I have high hopes for products that help people with body odor, especially younger people, for whom it could be a real benefit.

As for allergy, I do not think it is possible to eliminate the risk completely. It would be best to explain first use clearly in the instructions, such as applying a small amount in one area and observing for irritation before using it on the intended area. More user-friendly, scientifically informed, and detailed instructions would be better.
