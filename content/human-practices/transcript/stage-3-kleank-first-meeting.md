**JingZhou company**
Today, we would like to talk with you about the points our colleague just mentioned. What stage has your technology reached? Looking ahead, are you hoping to commercialize it, or do you have other plans? Would there be any opportunities for us to work together? Those are roughly the things we wanted to discuss. There is relatively little information on your WeChat official account, which is why we arranged this conversation. Have I explained that clearly?

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Sure. Let me briefly introduce the problem our project aims to address. During our preliminary research, we found that one source of underarm odor is linked to genetics. In people carrying a particular gene, a substance is cleaved during bacterial metabolism, ultimately producing a thiol called 3SSSH. This is the main source of the smell commonly referred to as bromhidrosis. It is quite pungent and is independent of sweating. Generally, it can continue to be produced as long as metabolism is taking place, so the odor is persistent.

We also looked closely at your products. Your advertising says that the odor molecule you target is isovaleric acid, a short-chain fatty acid. This appears to be produced through the metabolism of sweat, so its main source may be sweating, or what we usually call sweat odor. Our project mainly aims to address the bromhidrosis I just mentioned: persistent underarm odor associated with genetics.

That is the background. We then studied the mechanism in more detail. We have also explained it on our WeChat official account. Have you had a chance to read it? If so, I will not go through it again in detail.

**JingZhou company**
Yes, we have read it. We are curious whether you are still at the design stage or have already completed the development.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Our dry-lab design work is largely ready, and we are now mainly carrying out experiments. The first step is to express our binder, and the next is to validate its binding efficacy. Later, we may optimize it, for example by finding ways to immobilize it on the skin surface or improve its stability. At the moment, our experimental work is still at the stage of preparing for expression and validating binding performance.

**JingZhou company**
I see. Will all three pathways be pursued at the same time, or are these three separate directions?

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We will pursue all three directions in sequence. At present, we are mainly working on the binder and binding agents. We are also preparing the other two approaches, including DNA synthesis and plasmid construction.

**JingZhou company**
Understood. I may have two more questions. First, do you have an initial assessment of which of these three pathways would be most effective? Will you try all of them, or focus on one particular direction?

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We will try all of them, but we have also consulted different experts. They have different views depending on their technical backgrounds. For example, a cosmetics expert we spoke to recently thought that bacteria might relatively easily develop resistance to the protein inhibitor. They could express other transport proteins or take up the precursor molecule through other routes, counteracting the inhibitor's effect.

He also thought that the binding-agent approach was, after all, a relatively conventional chemical process. One binding agent can only correspond to one precursor molecule, so a relatively large dose would be needed for it to work, making it less efficient. He considered engineered enzymes a potentially more effective and even longer-lasting approach. However, maintaining the enzyme's stability and activity is also challenging. Since we are engineering an enzyme, successfully developing it in the first place also requires some luck, time, and experimental validation. That is a substantial challenge as well.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Speaking of aluminum salts, we noticed that the aluminum salt ingredients in your products seem different from more conventional ingredients such as aluminum oxide. What is the main difference between your products and traditional ones?

**JingZhou company**
Traditional products in China mainly use aluminum chloride. Because they contain more chloride ions, the pH of the formulation is lower than ours. Ours is relatively better in this respect. We use aluminum-zirconium salts with amino acid buffering, so the pH is relatively higher and the irritation is lower. Issues you mentioned, such as yellowing clothes and dryness, are essentially formulation problems that can be addressed with other ingredients.

Overall, aluminum-zirconium salts could be considered the third generation. The first generation mainly used aluminum chloride. The second used aluminum chlorohydrate, which provided some buffering. This is the third generation. It is more widely used abroad. Innovation in China is relatively limited: if one company uses aluminum chloride, others tend to use it too. That is why we chose this approach, and we are still optimizing it.

We also have some aluminum-free options in reserve. Magnesium hydroxide can be used for adsorption, for example, or zinc phenolsulfonate, which can both reduce sweating and inhibit bacteria. These could be part of the next generation. We have some groundwork in place, but fundamentally these ingredients provide antibacterial and antiperspirant functions, corresponding to the first two steps you mentioned. If your approach can actually be achieved, I personally think it could be better.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
One question we have during product development is whether consumers actually need an antiperspirant effect. Our product will not reduce sweating, so we would not be able to meet that need for people who have it. However, our research suggests that most current approaches still use aluminum salts or substances such as methenamine. They either block sweat glands or use formaldehyde, so they still involve chemicals that may have some effects on the skin.

**JingZhou company**
We have been weighing this issue too. After our research and interviews, we still focus on the problem that matters most to the customer. For some people, odor has the greater impact. The same is true of the foot antiperspirant spray we are making. Although it uses antiperspirant ingredients to inhibit bacteria and reduce the production of precursor substances, thereby eliminating odor, the fundamental reason people buy it is to address foot or underarm odor.

There is also a particularly large market for this abroad. In Europe and North America, these products are essentially called deodorants rather than antiperspirants. In China, cosmetic products are now used in more specific settings. Many people sweat heavily. For example, people socialize at the gym, and after exercising they may have a slightly sour smell, but not the kind associated with bromhidrosis. What they want is simply a product that reduces sweating and adds a little fragrance.

Of course, this is our own view. If you focus on bromhidrosis, the need will be particularly strong, but the target population will be much smaller than the population looking for antiperspirants, because Chinese people and people in Europe and North America have different sweat gland structures. These products sell very well abroad, while the Chinese market is still at an early stage. Annual e-commerce sales may be a little over RMB 2 billion, which is not particularly large. In China, antiperspirants and deodorants are grouped together within cosmetics because there is no separate category of products specifically for deodorizing.

So that is a good question. Following your approach, people who need the product may keep buying it. In the other situation, I might buy product A today, try it, and then decide tomorrow that I want some fragrance, so I start choosing between B, C, and D. A couple of years later, I may want another function. Personally, I do not think their need to reduce sweating is such a pressing problem.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**

**JingZhou company**

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
I see. Apart from aluminum salts, are other ingredients in your products, such as persimmon tannins, added to improve skin condition or inhibit bacteria, as you mentioned? Were those among your considerations?

**JingZhou company**
We mainly use persimmon tannins to adsorb odor molecules that have already formed. For odor that has already been produced, we use persimmon tannins to adsorb it. To prevent odor from forming, we use antiperspirants to reduce sweat secretion. There is also CPC, an antibacterial ingredient that reduces bacterial growth in that area. So there is a preventive effect, and if odor has already formed, we adsorb it. That is the overall logic.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
If antibacterial agents are used to kill underarm bacteria, even if not in large quantities, could that have any negative effects on the skin barrier?

**JingZhou company**
The skin microbiome is still not particularly well understood in dermatology or the cosmetics industry. That includes the effects of different bacteria and what changes in their abundance actually mean. So we cannot be sure whether the overall effect on the skin microbiome is beneficial or harmful. What we do know is that killing bacteria can reduce odor production.

The effect on the skin barrier is generally manageable. The irritation is not mainly caused by those antibacterial ingredients; it may be more related to the relatively low pH. Antiperspirants are generally at pH 3-4, which may irritate the underarms. Also, if these metal salts cause inflammation during use, the underarm skin may darken. That may be one of the bigger issues with this type of product.

There is not much difference. The two formulations have very similar bases. This is partly a marketing consideration: we need to distinguish the products for the two groups. At the time, we made slight adjustments to the salts in the two formulas to differentiate them. Their overall performance and safety are similar.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Thank you. We also wanted to ask about product formats. Most of the products we see being promoted are roll-ons. What advantages do roll-ons have over other formats?

**JingZhou company**
Roll-ons are a little better. They are generally emulsion-based, which feels better when applied. With a spray, it can be difficult to control where it goes, and sometimes you spray it elsewhere. Sticks are wax-based, and some people do not like that greasy feeling.

We have tried all these formats and ultimately felt that an emulsion-based formulation was better. The ingredients themselves are also water-soluble. That is something you may need to consider in the future too: the solubility of your ingredients and what kind of formulation they would suit.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Most of our work on the ingredients is still computational validation, but some of the binding agents we have expressed are also water-soluble. We assessed this computationally, and the experimental results so far suggest that they are reasonably water-soluble too. Since they are proteins, we can also gradually optimize them if we want to improve their water solubility.

**JingZhou company**
It is also fine if they are not water-soluble. You can improve solubility with solubilizers, which are used in cosmetics.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Since our product is protein-based, we would also like to ask whether we might face more difficulties with storage.

**JingZhou company**
We do not have much experience with that. For cosmetic ingredients that really are this type of protein, they are generally made into freeze-dried powders. Broadly, the products you see fall into two groups. One includes products advertised as containing collagen, but if you look closely, they actually contain small peptides. The other group contains actual proteins, which are often freeze-dried and then reconstituted before use. That could be an issue you need to address later through product or packaging design.

If you are really using proteins, there are several things to consider. The first is storage conditions. Your product will eventually be kept in a warehouse, and few warehouses maintain a constant temperature, such as 25 degrees Celsius, because that costs more. Products may simply be left there, and temperatures can reach the thirties or forty degrees in summer.

There is also shipping. Temperatures may be even higher during transport across the country, although the exposure may be shorter. In our ingredient development work, we have encountered temperatures of forty or fifty degrees lasting two or three days, so you need to consider heat resistance and stability. Also, could any metals in the formulation affect the protein? For example, even adding a little fragrance could cause it to denature.
