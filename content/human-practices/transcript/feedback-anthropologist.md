**Interviewer**

Last time, we discussed stigma and the sociological background of body odor. Today, we have a few follow-up questions.

During our last interview, you mentioned that many deodorant brands take advantage of people's insecurities about their body odor.

**Interviewee**

How else would you sell it?

**Interviewer**

That is a good point. Magnifying those insecurities is how many beauty products are sold these days. But when we reflected on our own project, we realized there was a contradiction. I think we touched on this last time.

**Interviewee**

What is the contradiction?

**Interviewer**

The contradiction between wanting to sell our product and our philosophy of portraying body odor as something natural. In theory, would that reduce people's motivation to purchase deodorant?

**Interviewee**

No, not at all, because nothing we do is natural. The whole point of culture is that it is second nature. Everything we do, think and say is mediated through culture.

Let me give you an example involving my cats. I have a cat called Gulu, who has emotional problems. They put her on antidepressants, but she was staggering around the house. I thought, “This is ridiculous. It is not going to work.” I apologized to Gulu and took her off the medication.

Then we got her an emotional-support kitten, [name unclear], who is exceptionally cute. But what does he like to do? He likes to bite Gulu's tail. So the solution to the problem has made Gulu's problem worse.

The point is that the kitten is learning how to be a proper cat. Whenever he goes for Gulu's tail, Gulu swats him. They do not use their claws; he has already learned not to. Gulu also presses him down and licks him to exert dominance.

He is being taught culture. He is being taught that his natural urge to bite tails, toes or fingers is unacceptable.

If “natural” is a code for “cannot be helped,” that is still not enough simply to excuse something. We do not pick the kitten up and throw him across the room, but Gulu jumps on top of the refrigerator, where he cannot reach her. He stares at her longingly, then sees my feet and changes his plan of attack.

**Interviewer**

I think I understand. Would you say that we could portray body odor as something like acne? It is normal, but some people may still want to get rid of it.

**Interviewee**

One of the purposes of modernity is to replace nature with what we can do.

We can build large, air-conditioned houses, so we are supposedly better than people who cannot. We can change our outfits frequently, so we are supposedly better than people who have to weave their own cloth. The idea of modernity is to assert humanity as what is right and dominant.

You can see this in sexism. One of its central features worldwide is equating women with nature: men are presented as rational and thinking, while women are presented as natural and feeling. Things are separated in this way.

You can also see it in fat-shaming. It is not simply about overeating or having too much sugar in your bubble tea. Being fat is interpreted as showing a lack of discipline: “You would not have to be fat if you controlled what you ate or went to the gym.”

Everything about how the body expresses itself becomes a site where the self is constructed. You do not have to smell. You do not have to wear dirty clothes.

Of course, this overlooks or deliberately suppresses the fact that some people cannot help how they smell. Some people cannot afford washing machines. There are many circumstances that prevent people from controlling their bodies.

But modernity demands absolute control over the body. The people we consider most beautiful are often those who appear to exercise the greatest control over it. Think about muscles: the work, food and exercise required to develop them.

Deodorant is part of this expectation that you must control your body all the time to be attractive to others and demonstrate your willingness to participate.

The problem is that bodies fall apart. For me, that is where the contradiction lies. The natural body becomes something you have to overcome to prove that you deserve to participate in modern society.

I am much more aware of this than you are. You may remember this conversation in forty years and think, “Now I understand what she meant.”

When you are young, you may be ashamed of your acne but have little sympathy for someone who can no longer comfortably walk up five flights of stairs. My body is falling apart. I can hide some of that by taking an elevator, just as someone can hide body odor with deodorant.

That is where I would locate the contradiction. Whatever I said before, you can ignore it. Put that on your website: she says it does not matter what she said before, because she may deny it or admit she was wrong.

**Interviewer**

For our advertising and marketing, we are focusing on personal comfort, freedom of choice and giving people the option to manage their body odor when they want to.

**Interviewee**

How modern of you.

**Interviewer**

We are trying to put positive messages on our packaging. How can we distinguish marketing that simply uses positive language from marketing that genuinely respects people's freedom?

**Interviewee**

You cannot.

**Interviewer**

We cannot?

**Interviewee**

You cannot, because you do not control how other people receive your messages.

Reception theory concerns the difference between what I say, how you receive it and what you subsequently do with it. That process changes the meaning.

For example, there have been interesting studies about bilingualism and the language people use under extreme distress.

Similarly, when you take something I say and put it on a website, you change its meaning. What I said was intended to answer your question here and now, rather than to appear on your website.

There is a gap between our conversation today and the website you later make. Legal language is supposed to protect you from whatever happens in that gap.

There may be good intentions, bad intentions or ignorance. What I consider the most important part of the conversation may not be what you consider the most important part.

The fact that you use something in advertising or marketing also shapes how people receive it. People receive information through both its content and its context. Both are present simultaneously, and context is often more important than the words.

Whatever you do, if you advertise deodorant, you contribute to people's insecurities. That is not going to change in the current context.

We may eventually enter a different era in which what we cannot change is left alone. But we do not live in that world.

**Interviewer**

After our previous interview, we spoke with several school teachers about different user needs.

Someone from Botswana told us that, in certain cultural contexts, a man's natural body odor can be associated with masculinity rather than embarrassment or unpleasantness. Do you have any thoughts on that?

**Interviewee**

Why would that not be true? Of course, the way people smell can have sex appeal. It simply does not appeal to everyone, and if it is not a sexual or romantic context, who cares?

Some people like the way athletes smell. That does not necessarily mean they want that smell next to them on the subway.

Again, it comes back to context, and it is not only cultural. I would expect those same people not to want to smell very musty when they go to class, because they want their teachers to treat them in a particular way.

You would not necessarily wear your school T-shirt when going to a bar to look for a girlfriend. Context matters.

**Interviewer**

What contexts or settings do you think we should use in our advertising?

**Interviewee**

“Be pleasant,” perhaps. Rather than simply using nice language, conventional advertising would play on insecurities. I might use something like, “Treat your neighbor with the respect they deserve.”

**Interviewer**

That would be a conventional way to market the product, but it clashes with what we are trying to advocate. Fortunately, iGEM does not judge us by how much product we sell, so we also want to consider our underlying philosophy.

**Interviewee**

Then perhaps, “Decide when you want to be sexy and when you do not.”

That is positive, right? Or, “When is your body odor your biggest weapon?”

That is the kind of question you are asking.

**Interviewer**

Our previous slogan was “Expel Your Odor,” which relates to our brand name, Expelliodor. Do you have any recommendations for a new slogan?

We want to change it because it sounds too forceful. We do not want to make decisions for people; we want customers to choose whether to manage their body odor.

**Interviewee**

My sense is that people who do not care are not going to buy your product. People buying deodorant already care.

Some people do not care, some cannot afford deodorant, and others have different reasons for not using it. Those people are not necessarily going to buy your product.

For the people who might buy it, I would probably focus on health.

Did you read the article about the origins of deodorant that I sent you? It described people effectively putting poison on their bodies so women would smell nice. Smelling nice was treated as more important than avoiding harm.

Historically, that returns to your earlier point: women were not supposed to smell, while men could. That was what made the article interesting.

**Interviewer**

Which geographical location was the article discussing?

**Interviewee**

I think Philadelphia, or one of the northeastern cities.

**Interviewer**

That is interesting. Earlier in the project, we interviewed a traditional Chinese medicine specialist. They also mentioned a history of applying a mercury chloride compound to the underarms.

**Interviewee**

The traditional white makeup used by Japanese geishas was also poisonous.

Nature is not necessarily about health. It is about making yourself conform to an idea of what human beings think they like.

**Interviewer**

Other than judgment from others, are there barriers that might prevent someone from choosing whether to use deodorant?

**Interviewee**

I stopped using it because I felt it was too toxic. When I started using it, my underarms became red and developed rashes.

Many products considered part of health care or grooming can be harmful. Think about dyeing your hair. Maintaining bright black hair as you grow older requires repeatedly applying hair dye. There are healthier options now, but older products could be very harsh.

Personal safety is why I stopped using deodorant, and it is also why I do not dye my hair. That is a reasonable reason to stop.

If your product is demonstrably safe, I might be convinced to buy it. I am American enough that I would be mortified if I smelled, but I am also selfish enough that I am not going to poison myself just to make the person next to me on the subway happy.

That is where I draw the line.

**Interviewer**

That seems like a reasonable middle ground.

This is no longer one of our formal interview questions, but we are trying to connect this discussion to our next activity, which concerns inclusive packaging.

By inclusive packaging, we mainly mean features designed for people with disabilities, such as an assistive design that makes the product easier to handle. Let us show you.

**Interviewee**

Someone in a wheelchair has bigger problems than how they smell.

**Interviewer**

I see the irony, but—

**Interviewee**

When you said “inclusive,” I thought you meant you were willing to sell it to people who did not need it as well.

**Interviewer**

We have been thinking about this for two nights, but we have not found a clear logical connection between these two topics. Do you have any ideas about connecting the sociological or anthropological discussion to inclusive packaging?

**Interviewee**

Is this your design?

**Interviewer**

No. It is from Unilever, a large company.

This is absolutely horrific.

Horrific? Really?

**Interviewee**

Yes. They have gone to all this effort to make something accessible so they can sell a product, rather than working to make people's lives more accessible.

Unilever sucks.

Would it not be better if cities were accessible, so that after putting on deodorant, people could actually go out and do more things? They could go to the movies, for example. Going to a movie in Shenzhen in a wheelchair requires a lot of effort.

Everyone has the right to sweat profusely.

**Interviewer**

They also have the right to get to those places.

**Interviewee**

Exactly. How do they get there?

**Interviewer**

Perhaps other places are more accessible. China seems especially inaccessible.

**Interviewee**

China is not that unique. Chinese people are people. I will die on that hill.

There are inaccessible places everywhere. Human beings can be terrible, especially when they spend time inventing accessible products instead of making the world accessible.

You could redefine inclusivity as, “Anyone who sweats can use our product.”

But as you said, there is a genetic component to what you are addressing, which makes inclusivity in your case delicate.

Good luck with that. If genetics is part of the basis of your product, you need to think carefully about what inclusivity means.

We have already heard comments about white teachers smelling the most. Unless your argument becomes, “Smell like us, because we smell better,” you need to consider what message you are sending.

Perhaps I should think about it further.
