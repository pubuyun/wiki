**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
What Traditional Chinese Medicine treatments for underarm odor are commonly used in clinical practice today?

**Mr. Hu**
Traditional Chinese Medicine, or TCM, mainly takes two approaches to body odor, specifically underarm odor. One is internal treatment, such as herbal decoctions or pills taken orally. The other is external treatment, applying Chinese medicines locally.

For internal treatment, TCM generally associates the condition with "liver fire," or, more specifically, stagnation of liver qi transforming into heat. Longdan Xiegan Decoction is therefore used to clear liver fire in this type of condition. According to TCM theory, stagnated liver qi produces internal heat and damp-heat. This is one TCM interpretation of underarm odor.

External treatment mainly involves several toxic medicinal substances, such as Mituoseng and Qingfen, as well as alum. These are applied externally.

Other herbs may also be included, such as Zaojiao, or soap pod. In ancient times, soap pod was used like soap. It could be boiled in water to wash the underarms, after which powdered medicines such as Mituoseng and Qingfen were applied. Continued use over a period of time might have some effect.

Another method uses hot cooked rice, with the medicine placed inside the rice and held under the arm. This might also have some effect.

So the two main TCM approaches are internal and external treatment.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Through what mechanisms do these medicines or treatments work? For example, do they have antibacterial effects?

**Mr. Hu**
Internal treatment mainly regulates the body's metabolism; that is one way to describe it in modern terms. Longdan Xiegan Decoction, for example, contains around ten medicinal herbs and mainly clears liver fire. TCM considers the liver and gallbladder to have an interior-exterior relationship, so liver fire and gallbladder fire are connected.

Longdan Xiegan Decoction clears heat and also contains ingredients that promote water elimination to remove dampness. In modern terms, this means promoting urination and expelling dampness through urine. That is the explanation for internal treatment.

From a modern perspective, external treatments may work by damaging some of the sweat glands. These are toxic medicines, as mentioned earlier. Mituoseng is lead oxide, a lead-containing compound, while Qingfen is mercurous chloride. These substances may have a damaging effect on sweat glands.

The active component of alum is hydrated potassium aluminum sulfate. Its main effect may be to reduce sweating. Less sweating may also reduce the secretions associated with underarm odor, and it may have some pore-constricting effect. That is roughly the idea.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
What side effects do these medicines have?

**Mr. Hu**
Looking at the situation today, TCM is not particularly effective for underarm odor. The medicines I mentioned require repeated use. People are now more aware that some are toxic and that the dose is difficult to control, so these methods are used relatively less often.

Some relatively less toxic medicines used in TCM dermatology, such as Baixianpi and Baifuzi, may also be considered.

These medicines have side effects and toxicity. The first problem is that the dose is difficult to control. Small amounts may not cause problems, as similar substances have also been used in many cosmetics. For example, there have been practices using Mituoseng, which contains lead, in making preserved eggs. Lead-containing substances were also used in cosmetics in the past to give the face a shiny appearance, so they certainly had an effect. However, the dose is very difficult to control, and excessive amounts may cause side effects.

Nowadays, most TCM practitioners would suggest surgery or other treatments that destroy sweat glands for underarm odor. When patients come to us, we may prescribe some oral medicine, which can sometimes reduce the odor slightly.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
How much does a complete course of treatment usually cost?

**Mr. Hu**
For medicines such as Longdan Xiegan Decoction or Danggui Longhui Pills, a decoction may cost around RMB 100 per dose, with a course generally lasting about three weeks. Pills may cost around RMB 30 per day and be taken for approximately one month.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
How familiar do you think the TCM field is with synthetic biology?

**Mr. Hu**
In theory, TCM and synthetic biology belong to two different academic systems, so the TCM field is relatively unfamiliar with synthetic biology.

However, people are researching related questions in Chinese medicinal chemistry. Many TCM universities are studying substances such as Mituoseng and Qingfen and these treatment approaches, including how modern methods might make their use more effective and standardize dosage control. However, I have not yet seen large-scale clinical reports.

**Mr. Hu**
For example, Mituoseng, alum, and Qingfen. Those are the main active ingredients.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We also noticed that these seem to be mainly inorganic chemicals, rather than biologically derived substances such as artemisinin. They may therefore be less suitable for extraction or production through synthetic biology.

**Mr. Hu**
You could look into purslane. It has also attracted considerable attention recently.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
So Chinese medicinal herbs still contain many active ingredients?

**Mr. Hu**
There are many methods, but they are used relatively less often nowadays and are not very common. As I said earlier, the main substances remain inorganic ones such as Mituoseng, Qingfen, and alum.

There are also many unusual formulas recorded in works such as Bencao Gangmu and Qianjin Fang. I vaguely remember one called "Spider Powder," in which spiders were wrapped in yellow clay and burned, then ground with Qingfen into a medicinal powder. There are quite a few methods for treating the underarms. If you search on Baidu or other platforms today, most of the information still centers on these substances.
