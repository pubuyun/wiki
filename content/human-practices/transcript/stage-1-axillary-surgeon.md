**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Hello, Doctor. We are the iGEM team from Shenzhen College of International Education, working on a synthetic biology project concerning body odor and axillary bromhidrosis. Before the interview, do you consent to us taking notes and making audio and video recordings? All questions and answers will be used only for our team project, without commercial use. Let us begin.

First, a brief introduction. We understand that a key molecule in underarm odor is 3M3SH, produced when staphylococci metabolize an odorless precursor secreted by the human body. We have designed three approaches targeting this pathway. First, protein binders specifically bind the precursor to prevent bacterial uptake. Second, engineered enzymes act on the precursor to prevent its breakdown into odor molecules. Third, proteins specifically bind to transporters on the bacterial surface, preventing the precursor from entering the bacteria.

We hope to understand patients' needs and the characteristics of existing approaches from clinical and surgical perspectives, so that we can improve our product. First, what procedures for underarm odor do you commonly perform? What are their principles and how are they carried out?

**Dr. Yang**
First, thank you for interviewing me. I work in facial aesthetic medicine and am an associate chief physician. Underarm treatment is also part of my daily work, and I do quite a lot of it. There are currently three main types of procedure.

The first is minimally invasive sweat gland removal through a small incision. A one-to-two-centimeter incision is made in the underarm, the skin flap is turned over, and the apocrine gland tissue is excised or scraped away under direct visualization. This is a procedure with relatively well-established effectiveness.

The process involves preparing the skin, administering local anesthesia by injection, incising the skin, separating the flap, removing the apocrine glands, and finally suturing and applying a pressure dressing. Unlike the third procedure I will describe, it does not remove skin. It is mainly suitable for moderate or severe cases and patients seeking a definitive treatment.

Recovery takes approximately two to three weeks, and normal daily activities can generally resume after about a month. We try to keep the incision small and use cosmetic sutures after gland removal to achieve a better appearance. Compression dressings are crucial for preventing hematomas.

The second category is radiofrequency or microwave ablation. Put simply, physical heat coagulates and destroys the apocrine glands. Taking radiofrequency as an example, the planned puncture sites receive topical anesthesia, then a radiofrequency electrode is inserted to heat and destroy the glands. Ice or cold compresses are used afterward.

This causes relatively little trauma and suits mild-to-moderate cases or people concerned about scars or surgery. Recovery generally takes one to two weeks, which is relatively short, and it usually has little effect on everyday work.

The third is surgery through a fusiform incision, the most traditional approach, which is rarely performed now. A fusiform incision is made along the underarm skin lines, and some skin is removed. Although the amount removed is not large, the trauma is greater and scarring is more noticeable. It is generally reserved for a small number of particularly severe, recurrent, or special cases. Recovery usually takes more than a month.

Currently, severe cases generally receive minimally invasive removal, while mild-to-moderate cases may receive radiofrequency ablation. Traditional fusiform-incision surgery is now uncommon.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Approximately how many of these procedures does your department perform each year?

**Dr. Yang**
This is a routine procedure in our department, usually performed on an outpatient basis without hospitalization. The department performs around 200-300 procedures a year, and I personally perform approximately 100-150.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
What is the usual price range for a procedure to address underarm odor?

**Dr. Yang**
It varies between hospitals. Hospitals are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary. Tertiary hospitals generally charge more, while secondary hospitals charge somewhat less. Ours is a Grade A secondary hospital, and we generally charge RMB 3,000 per side.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Do you consider the results of surgery significant?

**Dr. Yang**
We rarely perform the traditional fusiform-incision procedure now and mainly use minimally invasive removal. For moderate-to-severe bromhidrosis, this should currently be considered the most effective approach, with the most noticeable results. The effectiveness rate of gland removal can generally reach around 90%.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Does the recovery time you mentioned also indicate when the surgery begins to take effect?

**Dr. Yang**
Minimally invasive surgery takes effect quickly because the glands have been removed. The wound generally heals substantially within one to two weeks, and the reduction in odor is very noticeable.

Radiofrequency ablation generally takes effect around a week after the procedure. Since it destroys somewhat fewer glands, its effect may be slightly less pronounced.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
How satisfied are patients with the results?

**Dr. Yang**
Compared with other procedures, satisfaction is relatively high, around 85-90%. The small number who are dissatisfied mainly report a slight residual odor or cosmetic issues such as postoperative scars, pigmentation, or darkening of the skin.

Sometimes inadequate postoperative care leads to hematomas or other complications, which also affects satisfaction.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Next, we would like to discuss postoperative issues. Besides hematomas, what adverse effects can occur?

**Dr. Yang**
Every operation carries risks and can cause complications. The main ones for underarm odor surgery are as follows.

First, hematomas. If the pressure dressing is not applied properly, postoperative oozing can lead to a hematoma. I would estimate the probability at around 5-10%. Mild cases can usually be improved through drainage and compression.

Second, infection, which needs to be prevented in any operation. I estimate the infection rate at around 3%. The underarm is a recess rather than a flat area of skin. Bacteria, sweating, and other factors may increase infection risk there.

Skin or flap necrosis is also possible. Separating the flap may affect its blood supply, and factors related to dressing changes may also contribute. However, the probability is low, perhaps one case in a hundred, or around 1%.

Scarring of varying degrees can also occur, perhaps in around 10% or even 15% of cases. An incision has been made, so some scarring is possible.

Another relatively common issue is altered sensation. Flap separation may damage subcutaneous nerves and interrupt nerve endings, reducing sensations such as pain and temperature. As the nerve endings regrow and recover over time, sensation can gradually improve.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
What postoperative care do patients need? Besides cold compresses and pressure dressings, what else should they consider?

**Dr. Yang**
Care is mainly directed at preventing the complications we just discussed.

First, the pressure dressing generally stays in place for three to five days to prevent hematomas and help limit movement. The wound should also be kept clean and dry, protected from water, and have its dressing changed regularly. This is particularly important because the area tends to sweat.

Patients should minimize exercise and avoid strenuous activity. Dietary precautions include avoiding spicy or irritating foods and stopping smoking and alcohol. These are routine precautions.

We generally remove sutures after about seven days. Afterward, patients particularly concerned about scars and appearance may use an appropriate anti-scar product to reduce scarring, while continuing to pay attention to infection and activity restrictions.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
Does keeping the area dry mean that patients cannot bathe during this period?

**Dr. Yang**
While the area is bandaged and before sutures are removed, they should generally avoid getting the wound wet when bathing. If they wash the rest of the body, they should protect this area from water.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
If the odor recurs, what options are available for further treatment?

**Dr. Yang**
Recurrence also varies in severity. Microwaves and radiofrequency differ from surgical removal, and recurrence may be somewhat more likely afterward.

For mild recurrence, topical antiperspirants and antibacterial agents are options. Bromhidrosis involves apocrine secretions and the action of bacteria on the skin surface, so besides treating the glands, we can control it by reducing sweating and inhibiting bacteria.

For moderate or severe recurrence after radiofrequency ablation, we can switch to minimally invasive gland removal for a better effect. If the previous procedure was minimally invasive removal, a further small incision can be used to remove remaining glands.

Botulinum toxin injections can also be combined with treatment. They reduce gland secretion by blocking the release of relevant neurotransmitters. Sympathetic nerves transmit signals through acetylcholine, and botulinum toxin interferes with that step.

For persistent, particularly severe recurrence, the traditional approach of removing some skin may ultimately be needed.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
We have heard about many surgical and nonsurgical options. What do you see as the main limitations and unmet needs in each category?

**Dr. Yang**
Some limitations are similar and others differ, so we can consider them separately.

Nonsurgical options, such as antiperspirants and antibacterial agents, first address symptoms rather than the underlying cause. They do not remove or destroy apocrine glands but act on the surface to reduce sweating or inhibit bacteria, so the odor can return easily. Second, some products are relatively irritating and may cause allergy, redness, itching, dryness, or pigmentation. Third, they may be less effective in moderate-to-severe cases and struggle to meet higher expectations in public and social settings.

Surgery causes tissue trauma, so it carries risks such as bleeding, infection, scarring, and flap necrosis. Residual odor and recurrence are also possible afterward. A complete cure cannot be guaranteed, although surgery is generally more effective than nonsurgical methods.

Recovery also requires limiting activity, which can be inconvenient for workers, students, and active young people. Scarring is another concern. It varies in severity, and some patients, particularly some women, care a great deal about it.

The main improvement needed in surgery is therefore to reduce trauma, scarring, and other risks while striving for definitive odor control.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
From a surgeon's perspective, what conditions would our protein formulation need to meet to become an effective complement or alternative to surgery?

**Dr. Yang**
I think it is a very good idea with considerable potential. The target is the precursor molecule or transporter, so it blocks an earlier step. Blocking sympathetic nerve release and acetylcholine transmission to limit apocrine secretion is a very good concept. I am very much looking forward to products like this.

As doctors, whether we perform surgery or other treatments, many involve tissue trauma. They are measures we take because we have to relieve patients' suffering. Ideally, there would be no illness and no need for treatment, or even for doctors. That is also a physician's dream and aspiration.

So I think a protein formulation is a very promising product concept. The next step is to see how to make it work. I think it has potential, a scientific basis, and practical value. It could suit different groups, avoid scars, and be convenient to use.

**GreatBay-SCIE 2026**
That concludes our main questions. Do you have any further suggestions or hopes for our project?

**Dr. Yang**
I would like to say a little more about the product. When I first heard about the protein formulation, I thought it was very interesting. If it reaches the market successfully and solves the problem, surgery might no longer be necessary. I think its logic and principles have a scientific basis.

It could meet particular needs, such as those of people with contraindications to surgery, people who are anxious about or resistant to surgery, those with postoperative recurrence who do not want another operation, and mild-to-moderate patients who do not need surgery. Constantly using antiperspirants and antibacterial products can be inconvenient and only provides temporary control. A product like this would be very welcome.

I have some personal suggestions for the next stage of development. First, safety must be the highest priority. This does not only mean life-threatening risks. Skin irritation, allergy, redness, swelling, erosion, and pigmentation are also safety issues that need attention.

Second, the effect should be consistent. For a product, I think an improvement of around 60% would be acceptable. It does not need to achieve 100% effectiveness in everyone. Clinically, a 60% effect can already be considered fairly good.

Third, it needs to be convenient. Your product should be relatively convenient and could have different formats, such as injections or topical application. A topical formulation would be preferable. Injections are not necessarily unsuitable, but they are more complicated, so ease of use matters.

You also need to consider resistance. Targeting the precursor or transporter may offer an advantage in avoiding the induction of bacterial resistance, but that still requires continued validation during development. There is a long way to go.

Cost control is also important. It cannot be too expensive; ordinary people need to be able to afford it. Clinical validation matters too. We generally use several stages, such as three phases of clinical trials, before gradually introducing a product into use.

I also look forward to your future. You are addressing a real-life problem and pursuing practical research outcomes. Your future is promising, and I hope you continue progressing in research and innovation.

First, you need to examine the period of use and treatment duration during clinical trials. Whether one tube solves the problem or repeated use is needed will certainly affect price acceptance.

If one tube costs RMB 500-600, one tube is insufficient, repeated use is needed, or another application is needed six months later, patients will compare it with microwaves or surgery. Personally, I think around RMB 150 is worth considering.
