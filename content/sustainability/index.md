## Introduction

In 1987, the United Nations Brundtland Commission defined sustainability as "meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." :fn-ref{#1} With the rapid development of science and technology, growing resource consumption and environmental pressures have highlighted the need to consider sustainability alongside technological progress. For Expelliodor, this means looking beyond the effectiveness of our product and considering its social, environmental, and economic implications.

Therefore, Expelliodor integrates sustainability considerations into both our product development and educational activities. Guided by the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), we identified four goals closely related to our project: SDG 12 Responsible Consumption and Production, SDG 6 Clean Water and Sanitation, SDG 13 Climate Action and SDG 4 Quality Education.

## SDG 12 Responsible Consumption and Production

> “First, adopt low-toxic or non-toxic raw materials and avoid highly persistent ingredients. Second, design closed, automated production workflows. Third, fully collect and treat pollutants generated in manufacturing to reduce environmental impacts and meet emission regulations.”
>
> — Mr. Lu, Environmental Monitoring Center (EMC) director
>
>

### Targets

12.2 By 2030, achieve the sustainable management and efficient use of natural resources.

12.4 By 2020, achieve the environmentally sound management of chemicals and all wastes throughout their life cycle, in accordance with agreed international frameworks, and significantly reduce their release to air, water and soil in order to minimize their adverse impacts on human health and the environment.

### Identifying the problem

A deodorant’s environmental impact is not only created during use. Raw material sourcing, ingredient production, packaging and disposal can all generate emissions or waste.

During washing, **deodorant ingredients** may enter domestic wastewater. Their subsequent environmental effects depend on their chemical properties, concentrations and removal during wastewater treatment. We therefore wanted to investigate alternatives to aluminum salts and broad-spectrum antimicrobials without assuming that every conventional ingredient presents the same environmental risk.

We also noticed **packaging as another challenge**. A material may be technically recyclable, but mixed components, product residues and local collection arrangements can prevent its effective recovery. Choosing an environmentally responsible product therefore requires consideration of the complete formulation and packaging system. For a protein-based product, the challenge is even greater: proteins are prone to oxidation and tend to stick to the inner walls of packaging, which means a truly sustainable package must balance high product protection with high recyclability.

### Interviewing stakeholders

To better understand these issues, we interviewed Mr. Lu, a director of Environmental Monitoring Center in Jiangsu. He encouraged us to consider ingredient hazards alongside manufacturing practices, including closed production workflows and the collection and treatment of pollutants. His advice showed us that selecting biological ingredients alone would not establish the sustainability of our product.

Furthermore, we interviewed Ms. Chen from Amcor, a world-leading packaging company, who also underscored the environmental benefits of single-material packaging. She explained that such designs ‘do not require multi-layer separation at the recycling stage’.

We also consulted her on the potential packaging challenges specific to our protein-based product. She explained that ‘protein ingredients tend to stick to package inner walls’ and ‘roll-on ball structures commonly trap residual liquid inside the assembly’. To address these issues, she suggested applying ‘tailored hydrophobic or hydrophilic inner coatings to minimise protein residue.’

These discussions encouraged us to consider resource use and chemical management across the product’s life cycle, rather than focusing only on the choice of the active biological ingredient.

### Achieving the goal

For Target 12.2, our project explores microbial production of protein actives and the fragrance compounds geranyl butyrate and bornyl acetate. This provides an alternative production route that could reduce reliance on particular petrochemical feedstocks or plant-extraction processes. However, its resource efficiency will depend on fermentation yield, feedstock requirements, energy consumption and purification.

We also chose PP as the material for our package. By adopting an all-PP construction except for a hydrophilic layer, our packaging requires fewer raw materials and enters recycling streams most cleanly without the need for separation. Further assessment will consider packaging weight, material composition, cost, formulation compatibility and local recovery options. We aim to minimize unnecessary components and investigate opportunities for simpler material combinations and reuse.

To reduce resource consumption and packaging waste, we plan to implement a refillable soft-pack system, building on Ms. Chen's advice. We will offer lightweight stand-up pouches for refilling instead of manufacturing a new rigid bottle with every purchase. This model could cut plastic use per application by roughly 85% and reduce transport weight and carbon emissions.

Additionally, to reduce our reliance on petrochemical feedstocks, we are actively transitioning our formulation. While our active proteins and fragrance esters are already bio-based, we plan to replace petrochemical-derived excipients such as 1,3-butylene glycol and 1,2-hexanediol, with 100% bio-based, fermentation-derived grades. :fn-ref{#2}, :fn-ref{#3}, :fn-ref{#4} Because the chemical structures are identical, this supplier swap ensures formulation stability while significantly improving the renewability of our product.

For Target 12.4, our odor-control strategy targets precursor uptake rather than relying on broad-spectrum inhibition of underarm bacteria. Our protein actives and biosynthesized fragrances are intended as alternatives to selected conventional ingredients, but their biological origin does not automatically establish their safety or biodegradability.

To avoid using complex multi-layer plastics, we kept the PP structure but added a high-barrier silicon-oxide coating. This protects the protein from oxidation while keeping the material fully recyclable. To address Ms. Chen's warning about product residue, we incorporated a hydrophilic inner-wall treatment and a smaller-radius ball design. This significantly reduces protein adsorption and shrinks the air-liquid interface (the main route of protein inactivation), minimizing residual product left in the bottle.

Furthermore, after talking with Ms. Zhang, we are addressing our most environmentally persistent excipient - CO40 (PEG-40 hydrogenated castor oil). Because PEG-type solubilisers are ethoxylated from petroleum-derived ethylene oxide, are rated persistent by the OECD, and are flagged for ethylene-oxide / 1,4-dioxane residues, :fn-ref{#5}, :fn-ref{#6} we plan to screen plant-based alternatives (e.g., polyglyceryl esters, sucrose esters) in the medium term.

Following the environmental and marine experts' advice, we plan to assess ingredient degradation and potential ecotoxicity, while also examining fermentation waste and wastewater management. These evaluations will help us determine whether our design reduces environmental burdens or shifts them to another stage of production.

### Interactions

#### Positive

- SDG 6.3 and 14.1

If our ingredients demonstrate lower environmental hazards and suitable degradation behavior, their use could reduce risks associated with substances entering wastewater and eventually reaching aquatic ecosystems.

- SDG 13.2

Improving fermentation efficiency, minimizing unnecessary packaging and selecting lower-carbon production inputs could support climate-conscious product development. These benefits require process-specific assessment.

- SDG 3.9

Reducing exposure to ingredients demonstrated to cause adverse effects could support safer product use. This potential contribution depends on evaluating the finished formulation rather than assuming that proteins or biosynthesized fragrances are inherently harmless.

#### Negative

- SDG 6.3 and 6.4

Fermentation and purification consume water and generate wastewater. Without appropriate treatment and resource management, biological manufacturing could introduce additional environmental burdens.

- SDG 8.5

A transition toward biological manufacturing may require different technical skills. Future implementation should consider training needs and safe working conditions alongside environmental performance.

## SDG 6 Clean Water and Sanitation

> "If it is a synthetic protein, the [environmental] impact is certainly smaller because it is biodegradable. For example, after it is used on the body and washed off during bathing, it will degrade in the water body, so its impact is certainly small."
>
> -Ms. Zhang, marine microplastics expert
>
>

### Targets

6.3 By 2030, improve water quality by reducing pollution, eliminating dumping and minimizing release of hazardous chemicals and materials, halving the proportion of untreated wastewater and substantially increasing recycling and safe reuse globally

### Identifying the problem

Conventional deodorants can introduce harmful chemical ingredients into wastewater during daily use and washing. Some antimicrobial ingredients, such as triclosan (TCS), have been studied for their occurrence in wastewater and potential environmental effects. Although wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) achieve a median TCS removal efficiency of approximately 90%, residual TCS may still be released into domestic water or accumulate in sewage sludge and biosolids. Reported WWTP influent concentrations of TCS range from 1.86 to 26.8 μg·L⁻¹ with effluent concentrations ranging from 0.027 to 2.7 μg·L⁻¹. Furthermore, studies have identified the formation of toxic byproducts during oxidation of TCS. :fn-ref{#7} Aluminium salts are also widely used to block sweat glands. During daily use and washing, residues may enter domestic wastewater, adding to the chemical load that must be managed during wastewater treatment. These chemicals would pollute the aquatic environment and exert toxic effects on aquatic organisms and aquatic ecosystems. They can also bioaccumulate in organisms and circulate in ecosystems, creating long-term effects without being noticed.

### Interviewing stakeholders

To better understand this problem, we interviewed a marine scientist, Ms. Zhang. She explained the potential environmental risks of conventional deodorants as "Its toxicity is quite obvious. Although the possibility of aluminum exceeding standards is not high, but it certainly poses a potential threat." She noted that “Some of these chemicals have estrogenic or androgenic effects. They interfere with the endocrine systems of marine organisms, which may affect population dynamics and so on.” She also mentioned the long-term laboratory experiments suggesting that TCS could cause endocrine disruption to model organisms like zebrafish, marine medaka, and mice. However, she emphasized that toxicity must be evaluated in relation to exposure concentration.

She also explained that the discharge of aluminium compounds, synthetic fragrances, and preservatives contained in many deodorants is mainly through “domestic sewage entering the sewer system, then the wastewater treatment plant, then rivers, and finally the ocean."

### Achieving the goal

In response, our product adopts an alternative approach to odor control. We use biosynthesized proteins to target the odor-formation pathway at the precursor uptake stage, aiming to control odor formation without directly inhibiting underarm bacterial populations.

Through this design, we seek to contribute to SDG 6.3 by minimizing the potential release of unnecessary chemical substances into wastewater while maintaining effective odor control.

During our interview, Ms. Zhang recommended testing the ingredient's degradation rate in natural water, as well as assessing its effect on aquatic model organisms. Based on this feedback, we plan to investigate the protein's degradation rate as well as its potential ecotoxicity using appropriate model organisms. These assessments will help us better evaluate the environmental impact of our products.

### Interactions

#### Positive

- SDG 12.4

By reducing the release of harmful chemicals into wastewater, we contribute to minimizing their adverse impacts on human health as well as the environment.

- SDG 14.1

By minimizing the release of hazardous chemicals into wastewater, fewer pollutants may eventually reach marine ecosystems.

#### Negative

- SDG 14.1

Our formulation currently contains phosphates that will directly contribute to nutrient loading if they enter aquatic environments through wastewater. Our formulation also contains maltodextrin; its microbial decomposition can increase biological oxygen demand (BOD) and consume dissolved oxygen. If a sufficiently large organic load enters a water body without adequate treatment, this could contribute to oxygen depletion and negatively affect aquatic ecosystems.

## SDG 13 Climate Action

### Targets

13.1 Strengthen resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards and natural disasters in all countries

> 13.1.1 Number of deaths, missing persons and directly affected persons attributed to disasters per 100,000 population
>
>

13.2 Integrate climate change measures into national policies, strategies and planning.

> 13.2.2 Total greenhouse gas emissions per year.
>
>

Our contribution is indirect: we incorporate climate considerations into product development rather than claiming to influence national policies or measure changes in national emissions.

### Identifying the problem

We considered spraying as one of our product formats. However, some conventional deodorants use volatile organic compound (VOC)-based propellants, such as propane and butane, which can react with other pollutants to form ground-level ozone, a major component of smog that can harm human health.

Beyond emissions during product use, we also considered the environmental impact of ingredient production. Replacing conventional ingredients with biological alternatives does not automatically reduce greenhouse gas emissions. A study of fermentation-derived protein reported production footprints of 5.5–17.6 kg CO₂e/kg protein across different modeled scenarios, demonstrating the importance of production conditions. :fn-ref{#9}

For Expelliodor, we wanted to investigate whether the low proposed loading of our protein actives could offer a smaller ingredient-production footprint than an aluminum-containing reference formulation. We therefore used published data for a preliminary screening calculation, while recognizing that these data do not represent our actual manufacturing process.

### Interviewing stakeholders

To investigate alternatives that could reduce propellant-related VOC emissions, we interviewed Mr. Li, an expert in atmospheric volatile organic compound monitoring and control. He noted that the environmental risks of VOCs may be underestimated, despite VOCs being 'important precursors to secondary air pollutants' and some VOCs posing 'toxic' or even 'carcinogenic' risks. Therefore, he suggested using compressed air as an alternative to conventional VOC-based propellants, explaining that it would 'generate fewer VOCs than traditional organic propellants' and 'reduce certain health risks' as 'the corresponding sources of volatile chemicals' exposure are reduced'.

Our interview with environmental engineering expert Mr. Lu encouraged us to consider the entire production process, including raw material selection, manufacturing workflows and pollutant treatment. Although this advice did not provide quantitative carbon-footprint data, it reinforced the need to assess the environmental consequences of manufacturing rather than judging a product only by its ingredients. We therefore treated our calculation as an initial assessment to guide further investigation, not as proof that biological production is inherently lower-carbon.

### Achieving the goal

Based on Mr. Li's advice, we plan to use compressed air as the propellant in our spray-format product, aiming to reduce VOC emissions associated with conventional propellants.

Using 1 kg of formulation as the reporting basis, we calculated the combined loading of our three proposed protein actives:

- CGTase: 0.10 g/kg.

- Precursor binder: 0.15 g/kg.

- *PepT<sub>Sh</sub>* plug protein: 0.05 g/kg, currently estimated.

The total protein loading is:

0.10 + 0.15 + 0.05 = 0.30 g/kg = 0.00030 kg/kg formulation.

Applying the published protein-production range gives:

0.00030 × 5.5–17.6 = 0.00165–0.00528 kg CO₂e/kg formulation.

This corresponds to **1.65–5.28 g CO₂e per kg of formulation** under the protein proxy scenario. The underlying study modeled fungal production of a different protein, so this is not a measurement of our protein-production footprint. :fn-ref{#9}

For an illustrative reference, an Australian government evaluation reports a roll-on formulation containing 14.5% aluminum chlorohydrate, equivalent to approximately 3.6% elemental aluminum. :fn-ref{#10} An industry assessment reports approximately 1.5–4.5 kg CO₂e per kg of elemental aluminum contained in aluminum-based water-treatment coagulants. :fn-ref{#11}

Applying this proxy gives:

0.036 × 1.5–4.5 = 0.054–0.162 kg CO₂e/kg formulation.

This corresponds to **54–162 g CO₂e per kg of formulation** under the aluminum-containing ingredient proxy scenario.

The lower modeled result for our proposed protein loading identifies a potential climate benefit worth investigating. However, the aluminum data concern water-treatment chemicals rather than cosmetic-grade ingredients, and the two sources do not represent matched production systems. For this screening comparison, we also assume equivalent emissions from other ingredients, formulation, packaging, distribution and disposal. These assumptions remain unverified.

We therefore do not claim a confirmed reduction for the finished product. Our next step is to collect process-specific data on fermentation, purification, ingredient sourcing and waste treatment. A future comparison will assess equivalent odor-control performance over a defined period, including application amount, frequency and shelf life. We will document the assumptions and updated results so that others can examine and improve the assessment.

### Interactions

#### Positive

- SDG 9.4

Optimizing fermentation and purification could support more resource-efficient biological manufacturing and reduce production-related emissions.

- SDG 12.2

If effective at the proposed loading, our protein-based approach could reduce the quantity of active ingredients needed for odor control.

- SDG 7.2

Using renewable electricity in future manufacturing could further reduce production-related greenhouse gas emissions.

#### Negative

- SDG 6.3 and 6.4

Fermentation and purification may require substantial water and wastewater treatment. Efforts to reduce carbon emissions must therefore also consider water consumption and discharge quality.

- SDG 15.1

Agricultural feedstocks may introduce land-use pressures, depending on their sourcing. These effects should be considered when evaluating alternatives to fossil-based inputs.

- SDG 8.3

Specialized production and purification equipment could increase startup costs and create barriers for smaller manufacturers. Future process development should consider affordability and scalability alongside carbon performance.

## SDG 4 Quality Education

### Targets

4.1 By 2030, ensure that all girls and boys complete free, equitable and quality primary and secondary education leading to relevant and effective learning outcomes.

4.4 By 2030, substantially increase the number of youth and adults who have relevant skills, including technical and vocational skills, for employment, decent jobs and entrepreneurship.

4.5 By 2030, eliminate gender disparities in education and ensure equal access to all levels of education and vocational training for the vulnerable, including persons with disabilities, indigenous peoples and children in vulnerable situations.

### Identifying the problem

Through our Education activities, we began by examining science learning opportunities in China’s general high school education system, starting with Nanning No. 3 High School. While conventional curricula provide essential scientific foundations, opportunities to engage with emerging scientific fields and develop practical, discipline-specific skills may be limited. This highlighted the importance of broadening science education beyond conventional classroom learning, in line with Targets 4.1 and 4.4.

Meanwhile, our Inclusivity work revealed challenges faced by various underrepresented groups in accessing science education. For instance, blind learners lack accessible alternatives to the visual diagrams used in biology textbooks, while children on the autism spectrum may encounter teaching methods that do not accommodate their individual learning needs. These challenges highlighted the importance of making science education more inclusive and equitable, in line with Target 4.5.

### Interviewing stakeholders

Following our first introductory synthetic biology lecture at Nanning No. 3 High School, students showed strong interest in the methodologies used in professional synthetic biology research but had limited opportunities to explore and apply these techniques themselves. They also expressed curiosity about the industry’s development and related academic and career opportunities, which informed the direction of our subsequent sessions.

For blind learners, we interviewed Ms. Guan and Mr. Chen, both of whom had attended schools for the blind in Guangdong Province. They shared their experiences of learning biology through predominantly text-based materials and highlighted the potential of tactile graphics to make complex biological structures easier to understand.

During our collaboration with a local care centre for children with special educational needs, teachers welcomed our interactive biology activities and suggested developing structured, reusable teaching resources that parents and educators could use to support continued learning beyond our visits.

### Achieving the goals

At Nanning No. 3 High School, we returned for a second, in-person session focused on protein design, introducing students to techniques used by synthetic biologists and giving them opportunities to apply these methods themselves. We subsequently organised an online lecture exploring the synthetic biology market, industrial applications, and career pathways, broadening students’ understanding of professional opportunities in the field.

For blind learners, we developed 20 tactile biology graphics with accompanying audio guidance, continuously refining their design through multiple rounds of testing and feedback.

For children on the autism spectrum, we delivered interactive, multisensory biology activities and developed *Superb Course* teaching kits covering plants, animals, bacteria, and humans.

Looking ahead, we plan to submit our tactile graphics and *Superb Course* teaching kits to relevant professional organisations for review to further ensure their scientific accuracy, accessibility, and educational suitability. Following professional review, we hope to expand their reach by distributing tactile graphics to more schools for the blind, while establishing a long-term collaboration with the local care centre to bring our teaching kits to more families and educators.

Together, these efforts contributed to SDG 4 by broadening access to scientific knowledge and career-relevant learning opportunities while accommodating diverse educational needs.

### Interactions

#### Positive

- SDG 8.5 and 8.6

Introducing scientific skills and career pathways could support young people’s continued participation in education and training. Accessible learning resources may also help learners with disabilities explore future opportunities in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM).

- SDG 12.8

Future sessions could connect SynBio with sustainable consumption and responsible innovation, helping learners consider how scientific decisions affect society and the environment.

## Future Plan

### Social

- **Expand accessible STEM education**

    - Provide more opportunities for students to explore career-relevant scientific techniques and gain practical experience, while introducing them to emerging STEM industries and related career pathways

    - Extend our adaptive educational materials beyond biology to other STEM subjects, such as chemistry, physics, and engineering, to accommodate a wider range of learning interests and needs

- **Promote education for sustainable development**

    - Integrate topics such as sustainable lifestyles, environmental protection, responsible consumption, and scientific ethics into future educational activities.

    - Continue sharing accessible content about synthetic biology, sustainable materials, plastic pollution, wastewater, and responsible consumption through social media and outreach activities.

### Environmental

- **Improve sustainable packaging**

    - Explore biodegradable packaging materials that are compatible with our protein-based product, while simplifying the packaging design to minimize waste.

- **Conduct a *life cycle assessment***

    - Conduct a life cycle assessment to evaluate the environmental impacts of different materials, formulations, manufacturing processes, and disposal methods, and use the results to guide future product development.

### Economic

- **Create a self-sustaining educational model**

    - Commercialize our biology-themed slime kit at an affordable price and use the profits to support the continued development of our educational and sustainability initiatives.

- **Share production knowledge to create opportunities**

    - In the future, we hope to teach students and community members how to produce the slime kits themselves.

    - By sharing our production methods and educational resources, participants could develop practical skills in product development, entrepreneurship, and small-scale business management.

    - Where appropriate, they could sell the kits themselves and generate income from their work, creating opportunities for youth and communities to participate in sustainable entrepreneurship.

## Conclusion

Through the exploration of more environmentally responsible approaches to product development and inclusive science education, Expelliodor contributes to SDGs 4, 6, 12 and 13. Moreover, by analyzing our interactions with other SDGs, we have identified both potential positive and negative effects, which inform our future plans. Through these efforts, we aim to create long-term social, environmental and economic benefits, ensuring that advances in synthetic biology contribute not only to technological innovation but also to a more sustainable future.
