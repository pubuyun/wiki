## Blind Community

### Learn

Mark, our leader of Inclusivity group, loves visiting Shenzhen Library. During one of his visits, he stumbled upon the Braille book section, which he had never noticed before. Curious, he flipped open many Braille books, from literary classics to science textbooks. What stood out to him was that **none of the books he examined contained a single adaptive graphic**. Instead, page after page was filled entirely with rows upon rows of Braille characters. Holding a biology textbook, Mark began to wonder how blind learners could access concepts that rely heavily on visual representations, such as cell structures and human anatomy.

To find out whether this was a genuine issue experienced by the blind community, we interviewed Ms. Guan and Mr. Chen, both of whom were educated in schools for the blind in Guangdong Province, China. Their responses revealed how limited accessible educational resources remain in China, a country with one of the largest blind populations worldwide. :fn-ref{#13}

Ms. Guan recalled that Guangzhou School for the Blind admitted only 32 students from across the province each year when she attended, and parents would cry outside the school gates, begging for their children to be accepted. She told us that similar constraints exist in other parts of China. Learning that China has an estimated 8.92 million blind people further highlighted the severity of the issue. :fn-ref{#13}

She also acknowledged that while official Braille version of textbooks exists, most science textbooks still lack tactile graphics, making visual concepts particularly difficult to understand for students like her with congenital total blindness.

##### 16. Before today, had you ever heard of the field of synthetic biology?

- Yes, those are issues. There are also very few digital learning resources available, which makes them inconvenient for us to use. Resources for certain subjects are particularly limited.

##### 18. Did you have examinations at school? What format did they usually take?

##### 19. May I ask which examination you are currently preparing for?

- I’m taking the Higher Education Self-Study Examination, majoring in Computer Science.

##### 20. Have you encountered any difficulties during your independent studies, apart from the limited availability of digital resources?

##### 21. Based on your understanding, what proportion of blind individuals have access to higher education?

- Very few. Many end up working in massage-related professions. Most probably enter higher education through special admission examinations and recruitment programmes.

##### 22. Would it be fair to say that many blind individuals do not have access to science education?

- Probably not at the university level. They may have access to it during junior or senior secondary education.

##### 23. Which scientific subjects did you personally study?

- Mathematics, chemistry, physics, and biology. I studied them during junior secondary school. However, our biology teacher didn’t explain things particularly well, so I’ve forgotten much of what I learned.

##### 24. Would you mind sharing the name of your school? Being able to contact the school might help us better understand the needs of more blind students.

- Shantou Special Education School.

##### 25. Do you think the educational content at your school was similar to that of mainstream schools?

- It was fairly similar to mainstream schools. I only attended primary and junior secondary school there.

##### 26. Do you think we have overlooked any important issues concerning education for visually impaired individuals during our conversation? Is there anything else you would like to share?

- Nothing at the moment.

##### 27. Did you conduct any experiments during your previous studies?

- We did some experiments in physics classes, including small experiments related to electricity.

##### 28. Did you encounter any difficulties during these experiments? Alternatively, how do you think we could improve their accessibility?

- I don’t think there were any particular difficulties. Perhaps the students in our class were relatively good at hands-on activities and enjoyed doing these kinds of things, which made them easier to understand.

##### 29. Have you ever encountered raised diagrams or tactile graphics in extracurricular reading materials?

- Apart from textbooks, I don’t think I’ve encountered any other Braille books.

##### 30. Did your textbooks only contain flat diagrams, without raised tactile graphics?

- Yes.

##### 31. Do you think raised tactile graphics would make the content easier to understand?

- Yes. Our teachers used to purchase physical models themselves for us to explore through touch.

##### 32. Do you have any further suggestions or thoughts you would like to share with us?

- Nothing at the moment.

##### 33. Do you happen to know the contact information of any other schools for the blind, relevant institutions, or nonprofit organizations?

Thank you very much for sharing your experiences and supporting our project!

Every suggestion you have provided has the potential to help more visually impaired individuals gain equal opportunities to access scientific knowledge.

#### Interview transcript

##### 1. What is your age and occupation?

- I’m 25 years old and work as a music instructor.

##### 2. What levels of education have you completed?

- I attended Guangzhou School for the Blind for primary, junior secondary, and senior secondary education.

- I later attended Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine.

##### 3. What methods do you currently use most frequently to access knowledge or information?

- Nowadays, I mostly use my phone. I use screen readers to browse different apps, such as Douyin and Xiaohongshu.

- I also listen to audiobooks, usually on Ximalaya.

- For reading, I use apps such as WeChat Reading.

- I still read physical Braille books, but much less frequently than when I was in school.

- Sometimes I borrow Braille books from Guangzhou Library or Guangdong Provincial Library, although the selection is quite limited.

##### 4. Did you study scientific subjects such as biology, physics, and chemistry during your school years?

- Yes.

##### 5. Based on your understanding, does China currently have a relatively standardized and systematic set of Braille science textbooks?

- Yes. There are textbooks published by People’s Education Press, including Braille editions. However, relatively few schools for the blind actually offer all three science subjects. Schools that provide a complete, systematic curriculum following compulsory education standards are generally limited to one per province, usually located in the provincial capital. Some provinces do not even have dedicated schools for the blind. Instead, they have schools serving both blind and deaf students, or schools that also admit students with intellectual disabilities. As a result, their blind student populations may have greater educational needs, and teachers may not pay sufficient attention to their education.

- Apart from education for students with intellectual disabilities, education for visually impaired students is one of the areas of special education that requires teachers to possess particularly extensive professional knowledge. It also demands greater investment in teaching materials and other resources. By comparison, students with hearing or physical disabilities can generally follow mainstream academic curricula without major difficulties.

- There are actually very few dedicated schools for the blind. Even relatively well-established institutions, such as Qingdao School for the Blind and Shanghai School for the Blind, are uncommon. Shenzhen does not have a dedicated school for the blind, only a school serving students with visual and hearing impairments. In the blind education programme I previously attended, there were only about 40 students in total. Because the student population was so small, teachers could not organize classes by grade level. Instead, students were divided into three age groups, such as ages 6–10, 10–13, and 13–16. Students within the same class often had very different educational backgrounds. Some students transferred in during their teenage years after receiving little formal education, making it extremely difficult for them to follow academic subjects. They could only focus on learning independent living skills at school.

- I think education for visually impaired students is actually very unequal. When I attended Guangzhou School for the Blind, the campus was very small, and the school admitted only 32 students from across Guangdong Province each year. At the beginning of each school year, parents would often cry outside the school gates, begging the school to admit their children. Nearly 20 years have passed since I started primary school. Although there have been improvements, they have been limited. When I was in primary school, cities such as Shenzhen, Foshan, and Huizhou did not have special education programmes for blind students, apart from those available in Guangzhou. Schools in those cities did not admit blind students either. Around 2012, more classes for blind students gradually began to appear. The situation improved somewhat, but the system remains far from comprehensive. Nevertheless, schools for the blind with complete educational programmes still follow official curriculum standards strictly in both teaching and examinations.

##### 6. What were the admission requirements for Guangzhou School for the Blind when you attended?

- The school prioritized school-age children from Guangzhou, followed by children from elsewhere in Guangdong Province, and then applicants from other provinces. There was also an entrance assessment.

##### 7. Were the physics, biology, and chemistry textbooks you used different from the mainstream textbooks published by People’s Education Press? Were they directly translated into Braille?

- There were no differences. Yes, they were directly converted into Braille.

##### 8. Looking back on your educational experiences, how difficult did you find it to understand the scientific concepts being taught?

- Looking back, I found chemistry quite difficult to understand, while physics was relatively easier. The main problem was that many experiments relied on visual observations to demonstrate their results. We did not have dedicated physics or chemistry laboratories at the time, so experiments could only be conducted using teaching aids. Teachers would bring these materials to us and let us explore them through touch, or demonstrate the experiments while verbally describing what they were doing and what changes were occurring.

- As a result, we could only rely on hearing and memorization. We could not directly understand the experimental procedures, so many concepts had to be learned through rote memorization. I genuinely found this difficult, especially in senior secondary school, when the questions became more complex and increasingly difficult to understand.

##### 9. How much do you think the absence of charts, images, or structural diagrams affects students’ understanding of scientific concepts?

- I think the impact varies from person to person, depending on their individual ability to understand the material. Some of my classmates were able to understand these concepts quite well.

- I have Grade I visual impairment and am completely blind. Some classmates with Grade II or III visual impairments still had residual vision, so they could learn these concepts very quickly. Sometimes they had already understood everything while the teacher was still repeatedly explaining the material to those of us who were completely blind. They sometimes found it difficult to understand why we needed so much more time.

- There were also classmates who had lost their sight later in life, perhaps because of a traffic accident or an eye disease, sometimes in Grade 5 or 6 or even during junior secondary school. They had accumulated years of visual experience, so they could often understand certain concepts immediately after hearing an explanation.

- However, I have been unable to see since childhood, so concepts that rely heavily on visual representations are extremely difficult for me to understand. Braille materials can only present flat diagrams rather than three-dimensional representations, which I consider a major limitation. If three-dimensional tactile exploration were possible, it would greatly improve overall understanding, especially in subjects such as mathematics.

##### 10. Have you encountered tactile graphics, raised diagrams, or other physical models that can be explored through touch?

- I have encountered tactile graphics, including those used in mathematics textbooks and examination papers. However, these are only flat representations, and the diagrams on examination papers are also printed in this format. As for the three-dimensional models you mentioned, museums such as Guangdong Museum now organize special accessible sessions for blind visitors, where they provide 3D replicas of cultural artifacts that we can touch and explore. I think this approach is excellent.

##### 11. Are the diagrams presented in examination papers sufficient for you to fully understand them?

- Not completely. I would say I can understand less than half of the information. The printed diagrams are incomplete because they can only represent the largest flat surface.

##### 12. If you could design a set of biology or other science textbooks specifically for visually impaired learners, what features would you want them to include?

- If I were designing a set of biology or other science textbooks for visually impaired learners, I would want them to include the following features.

- First, I would develop corresponding teaching aids for the content covered in each semester. For example, all the teaching aids for one semester could be stored in a large box, with separate smaller boxes for each unit. The boxes could have Braille labels or raised, tactile QR codes on their surfaces, allowing students to scan them with their phones to access learning resources.

- Learning is already challenging for blind students, so teachers generally do not prohibit us from using phones or computers. At Guangzhou School for the Blind, for example, every student was provided with a desktop computer that they could use regularly. We may actually rely more heavily on digital learning and assistive technology than mainstream students. Therefore, the QR codes must have a tactile feature that allows us to locate them. I have previously used products designed for blind individuals that had printed QR codes, but I could not even find where the codes were located. I would spend a long time trying to scan them unsuccessfully, which created another accessibility barrier. If I could feel where the QR code was, I would immediately know that I could scan it with my phone to hear an explanation of what the item was and what information it contained.

- Additionally, I would like a system where I could select a particular unit on my phone and simultaneously take out the corresponding teaching aids from the box. I could then identify each item individually, understand what it was, which step it was used in, and what its purpose was.

- For blind individuals, simply distinguishing between different objects can be difficult. Printing instruction manuals in Braille is not particularly practical because Braille takes up a great deal of space. One page of Chinese text might require three or four pages when converted into Braille. An electronic instruction manual would allow me to access information more directly. For example, it could explain what an object is and describe its appearance, such as “This is a long metal object.” That would help me distinguish between items and avoid picking up the wrong one. This approach would also allow us to learn more independently without constant guidance from teachers.

##### 13. Did the biology textbooks you used contain illustrations, or were they entirely in Braille?

- Biology textbooks generally did not contain illustrations. Diagrams were usually printed only when they were particularly important, such as in mathematics. For example, a Chinese-language examination paper that takes up ten pages for sighted students might require thirty or forty pages in Braille. To save space, they cannot print every diagram.

##### 14. Where could we obtain Braille textbooks that are currently available?

- Shenzhen Library has a Braille reading room, just like Guangzhou Library. However, I’m not sure whether Shenzhen Library has textbooks for compulsory education. Its collection may consist mainly of novels or books related to massage therapy. Many blind individuals may not have the opportunity to attend school and instead receive massage training through the Disabled Persons’ Federation when they grow up, so there are many textbooks on massage therapy. You could call the library to ask.

- I think Guangzhou Library has some senior secondary school textbooks. Guangzhou School for the Blind admits very few students into its general senior secondary programme each year, so some students attend vocational secondary school to study massage therapy while taking supplementary lessons or independently studying the general senior secondary curriculum.

##### 15. Based on your understanding, approximately what proportion of blind individuals currently have access to science education?

- I wouldn’t want to give an inaccurate estimate of the actual proportion. However, according to government figures, Guangzhou has 200,000 registered blind individuals, while Guangzhou School for the Blind admits, at most, around 40 students each year.

- There are no other schools admitting blind students. We also have some students with multiple disabilities, such as both intellectual and visual impairments. For example, when a school for the blind has no available places, these students may be referred to a school for students with intellectual disabilities. However, that school may argue that their visual impairment is more significant than their intellectual disability and that they should attend a school for the blind instead. This results in students being passed back and forth between institutions.

- Another educational approach currently available is home-based instruction, where teachers visit students at home. Usually, a teacher only provides one lesson per week. I have heard that this is available in a few places, but I don’t know much about the details.

##### 16. What do you think is the greatest shortcoming of current educational resources for blind students?

- I think the biggest problem is the extremely limited availability of Braille textbooks. At most, textbooks are printed for the compulsory education stage, while workbooks are almost nonexistent. Teachers have to search for electronic examination papers online and then ask the school’s printing office to convert them into Braille. However, senior secondary students need to complete large numbers of practice questions, and the school’s printing office simply cannot keep up. Braille materials also need to be proofread before printing.

- Teachers sometimes find electronic examination papers and let students listen to the questions on their phones or computers before writing their answers in Braille. However, this creates an even more obvious problem: we cannot access the diagrams. Electronic materials generally provide only textual information, while charts and images are completely missing.

- Another major problem with Braille textbooks is how difficult it is to locate specific information. A single semester’s mathematics textbook might consist of ten Braille volumes, and teachers do not necessarily teach the chapters in order. If you want to find a particular concept or formula, it is like searching for a needle in a haystack. It is extremely complicated. During primary school, this is more manageable because there are only a few books, and you can usually find what you need by checking the table of contents. However, by senior secondary school, you may not even know which volume contains the concept you are looking for. Fortunately, some older students have compiled electronic notes covering major concepts and passed them down from one year group to the next. This has become an informal way of compensating for the lack of accessible resources.

##### 17. Do you know how we could contact any schools for the blind, relevant institutions, or nonprofit organizations?

- Contacting schools might be difficult because so many students request to conduct research there every year. As a result, schools generally no longer allow students to approach them independently. You may need to contact them through an official letter from your school.

- I know some nonprofit organizations, but most of the ones I’m familiar with focus on adults, particularly people who have lost their sight later in life and need support rebuilding their independent living skills. There are relatively few organizations specifically supporting children because people often assume that children already have access to the Disabled Persons’ Federation and schools, so no additional support is necessary.

- There are currently not many nonprofit organizations working in this area. I think one of the biggest problems with the Disabled Persons’ Federation is that its programmes have not kept pace with changing needs. They are still offering short-term massage training courses lasting about a month. These programmes certainly help many blind individuals earn a living and meet their basic needs, which is valuable. However, they do not provide opportunities to acquire knowledge in other fields.

##### 18. If we develop accessible textbooks or educational activities in the future, would you be willing to help us test them?

- Of course. I would be very interested and more than willing to participate. This reminds me of a teaching aid I encountered during junior secondary school. It was a geography learning tool that I think was developed by a student from Sun Yat-sen University.

- It was a flat map used in Grade 8 geography classes. It included both a map of China and a world map. It was an electronic teaching aid mounted on the wall, with the regions arranged in the same way as on a standard printed Chinese map. For example, by touching it, you could understand which part of China the teacher was referring to when describing its shape as a “rooster’s head,” and what that region looked like. Each province was labelled in Braille, so you could identify neighbouring provinces and understand their geographical relationships through touch. You could explore the map with your fingers. Without pressing anything, it functioned like a flat tactile diagram in a Braille book. However, when you pressed a particular area, it became interactive and played an audio explanation summarizing the geographical information about that province from the textbook.

- The concept was actually quite simple, similar to the interactive reading pens that children use. However, I found it extremely helpful for understanding the material, and it made learning much more engaging.

##### 19. Do you think we have overlooked any important issues concerning education for visually impaired individuals? Is there anything else you would like society or educators to understand about the experiences and needs of the visually impaired community?

- There is another issue I have thought about: it is actually very difficult for blind individuals to study academic subjects online. For example, sighted students can use Bilibili, where there are many courses taught by excellent teachers. If you struggle with a particular chapter, you can find a teacher explaining that topic, and there are also many people sharing summaries of important concepts online. However, I find these video lessons extremely difficult to follow. For example, when teachers explain examination questions, they usually do not read the questions aloud. Only teachers at schools for the blind tend to read the questions first. Sighted students can look at the questions themselves, but in online lessons, teachers often simply display a question on the blackboard or screen and immediately begin solving it. We have no idea what is happening, so it becomes extremely difficult to follow the teacher’s explanation. Even when teachers are not solving questions and are simply summarizing concepts, they often put a diagram directly on the screen without describing it. We cannot see it, so we have no idea what they are talking about.

- The same problem exists with tutoring institutions. Most major tutoring institutions are unwilling to accept blind students because they believe they cannot teach us effectively. The only alternative might be one-on-one tutoring that costs 600–700 RMB per session, but not everyone can afford that. Large group classes are generally not suitable. When I needed tutoring in the past, I could only hire university students because they were more affordable and patient. They would read the questions aloud before explaining them to me. I think this is one of the reasons why it is so difficult for blind individuals to expand their knowledge beyond what they learn in school.

##### 20. Is the difficulty of the college entrance examination for blind students exactly the same as that of the standard Gaokao?

- There are two pathways for entering higher education: independent admissions and the regular summer Gaokao.

- Independent admissions are somewhat similar to the admissions process for students applying to arts programmes. You need to visit the university and complete an examination in person, provided that the university has explicitly expressed its willingness to admit blind students. The difficulty is roughly equivalent to Grade 10 coursework, and there are relatively fewer questions involving diagrams. Usually, only additional questions require students to interpret tactile graphics.

- The regular summer Gaokao, however, is extremely difficult. Another problem is that even if you achieve a reasonable score, there is a considerable risk that a university may reject your admission. Taking Guangdong Province as an example, since blind students were allowed to participate in the regular summer Gaokao in 2015, I know of only one female student who achieved a particularly strong result through the standard academic examination. She scored 520 points in the humanities track and was admitted to Guangdong University of Finance. Although other blind students have taken the examination in subsequent years, many have only been able to enter junior colleges or private undergraduate institutions. They may also encounter problems such as admission withdrawals and disputes with universities. As a result, teachers generally do not encourage students to take the regular summer Gaokao unless they are exceptionally strong academically. Teachers may also worry about how unsuccessful outcomes could affect their school’s performance indicators. Overall, it is a relatively risky pathway.

- It is also important to note that independent admissions and the regular summer Gaokao use the same registration system, so students cannot register for both. You cannot secure a place through independent admissions as a backup and then attempt the regular Gaokao. Therefore, most students are extremely cautious when choosing between these pathways.

##### 21. Have you ever heard of synthetic biology, an emerging field of biological science?

- No.

##### 22. Beyond accessing scientific knowledge, do you think visually impaired individuals face equal or even greater difficulties when participating in scientific practice, such as conducting research or experiments?

- I think the difficulties are enormous. At present, even blind students who study abroad or pursue postgraduate education through mainstream entrance examinations generally concentrate on the humanities and social sciences. I previously knew someone who studied mathematics at the Central University of Finance and Economics. By his third year, the university advised him to change his major, and he eventually transferred to social work. That major was easier to complete because it relied less heavily on vision, did not require laboratory experiments, and mainly involved written work. This is quite common. Many blind students choose social work or other humanities and social science subjects, or arts-related fields such as music.

##### 23. Do you have any suggestions or design ideas that could help blind individuals gain better access to scientific research, experiments, or related careers?

- I think that before we can even consider how blind individuals might pursue careers in these fields, we first need to address how they can access the necessary knowledge more easily. Discussing employment opportunities might still be a little premature.

### Feedback 1

After Ms. Guan received our samples and tried them out, she outlined three suggested enhancements: adding guiding audio with instructions on how to explore the graphics by touch, which could be provided through QR codes and accessed easily through the blind mode on smartphones; adding signs to the graphics so that blind learners can identify the correct orientation of the graphics; and adding Braille labels corresponding to different features on the graphics, if possible.

In our next round of refinement, we prioritised addressing Ms. Guan’s feedback. However, as our current knowledge and practical experience with Braille were limited, we decided to add Braille labels in future iterations beyond the iGEM competition timeline, while carefully adding them where particularly necessary, such as for the ATCG bases. Alongside these improvements, we also addressed the two issues identified through our own reflection and further research by standardising the sizes of the graphics and enhancing the tactile distinction between different structures.

### Intervene 2

We completed modeling the graphics for all 20 concepts selected. On top of that, we wrote detailed **audio guidance** that includes both navigation instructions and mini lectures on the corresponding features as blind learners explore the graphics by touch. We then converted these scripts into audio files that can be accessed by scanning **QR codes**. To make the code easier to locate, we reserved a raised square in the lower-right corner of each graphic for the QR code sticker. In the lower-left corner, we placed a **direction arrow** to indicate the upward orientation of the graphic.

From the perspective of an educational accessibility expert, Mr. Zhang recommended clarifying one primary learning objective for each graphic, or separating equally important objectives into individual models for a larger display. This is because tactile perception is slower and more sequential than visual perception, and densely packed details within a small area can cause sensory interference, whereas vision can process multiple pieces of information simultaneously.

Mr. Zhang also advised us to use softer printing materials, such as _TPU_, so that learners can access fine internal structures more easily by gently folding or stretching the model. This was also one of the suggestions raised by Ms. Guan and her partner during our follow-up testing, in which we provided models printed with hard _PLA_ material.

Ms. Guan and her partner agreed that our current work was at least 70% more effective than learning from a textbook alone. Nevertheless, they also offered other suggestions for improvement, including the order in which features are introduced in our descriptions. For example, in the paramecium graphic, they recommended introducing the surrounding collecting canals before the centrally located contractile vacuole. Additionally, they felt that the models were a bit small, with many details packed too closely together to be easily distinguished by touch, and suggested increasing the overall model size to make individual features more perceptible.

### Beyond

Building on the remaining areas for improvement identified through feedback and our ongoing reflections, we plan to further develop our tactile graphics, with a focus on the following aspects:

- **Built-in Audio**: Integrate built-in speakers so learners can access guiding audio with a single press instead of scanning a QR code.

- **Larger Graphics**: Enlarge the graphics to provide more space for tactile exploration and Braille labels.

- **Focused Learning Objectives**: Focus each graphic on one primary learning objective, or separate equally important objectives into individual graphics for a more magnified display and clearer tactile perception.

- **Consistent Design Language**: Establish a unified design language system across the series, such as standardizing how structures like mitochondria are represented in plant and animal cell models.

- **Three-Dimensional Forms**: Move beyond raised, planar representations toward fully three-dimensional forms, such as transforming a raised circle into a sphere, to provide learners with a more intuitive understanding of spatial structures.

## Deaf Community

### Learn

One of our teammates, Nina, was always happy to share the new signs she learned with the rest of the team. Inspired by expressive sign language song videos, she started learning Chinese Sign Language (CSL) in July. Once, during a discussion about plasmid design, one of us casually asked her, “How do you sign ‘promoter’ in CSL?” She paused and replied, “I don’t know—I’m not sure if there’s a sign for it.”

Nina and several teammates began looking into it. We found that "promoter" is not included as a single standard sign in either CSL or ASL (American Sign Language). People can only fingerspell "P-R-O-M-O-T-E-R". During our further research, we found that many emerging scientific terms lack established signs in both CSL and ASL, with the gaps more pronounced in CSL. And multiple studies have highlighted that **the absence of signs for scientific terms can hinder Deaf people's access to and participation in science**. :fn-ref{#14} :fn-ref{#15} :fn-ref{#16}

### Intervene 2

### Beyond

We plan to continue sharing educational sign language videos on social media. Alongside videos introducing science to Deaf audiences, we also hope to create content that helps researchers, educators, and science communicators understand Deaf people’s perspectives and take an active role in making science more accessible.

Building on our collaboration with Mr. Gou, we hope to combine his sign language expertise with our knowledge of biology and synthetic biology to develop CSL expressions for scientific terms. We will invite more Deaf sign language users to review the proposed signs, and improve them based on their suggestions.

Following the bloggers’ suggestions, we will also seek partnerships with schools and educational institutions to share these expressions with Deaf students in science lessons. Learning through a familiar form of communication could make scientific concepts easier to understand and explore. We hope this will help more Deaf students enjoy learning science and, over time, encourage them to pursue their interests through further study or research.

With comprehensive and clear instructions that are easy to follow, our kit materials were published on our iGEM Wiki and other social media platforms. This not only expands access to science education for children on the autism spectrum but also plays a role in raising public awareness and guiding more people about how to support them respectfully.

### Beyond

Looking ahead, we hope to establish a long-term collaboration with Superb School, working with its staff to distribute our teaching kits to more families and educators. We also plan to organize more offline activities that actively involve parents, such as family seminars, to gather continuous feedback from parents and make refinements of our teaching kits accordingly. In addition, we aim to submit our teaching kits to more authoritative institutions for professional review, ensuring their scientific accuracy, educational suitability and effectiveness for children on the autism spectrum. Meanwhile, we will seek opportunities to broaden the kits' reach and make it more visible and accessible to a wider audience.

Our idea began with a social media post featuring a volunteer teacher conducting a red cabbage indicator experiment with children from low socioeconomic backgrounds in a classroom at a school without even a laboratory. We were struck by the children’s curiosity and enthusiasm for scientific exploration despite the limited resources available to them, and began to wonder: How true is this for children growing up in low-income households across China?

During our research, we found that in 2025, 281 million people in China had an annual per capita disposable income of approximately US$1,420 or less. :fn-ref{#21} For children growing up in these low-income households, limited family resources can coincide with limited educational resources at school. Together, these findings helped us identify a key barrier: although China’s nine-year compulsory education system provides all students with access to science theory lessons, **children from low socioeconomic backgrounds may have limited opportunities to engage in hands-on scientific exploration**.

### Intervene 2

To make the workflow more intuitive for beginners, we developed [_FoundryUI_](/software), a **node-based graphical interface** for computational protein design.

FoundryUI brings the main stages of protein design into a single workspace. Each node performs a specific task, and the links between nodes show how data moves through the pipeline. Users can inspect the workflow, adjust individual steps, and reuse it for another design task.

The platform integrates _RFdiffusion3_, _RoseTTAFold3_, _ProteinMPNN_, and LigandMPNN from Rosetta Foundry. It includes prepared workflows for folding prediction, ligand-binder design, protein-binder design, enzyme design, and sequence redesign. Typed connections prevent incompatible data from being linked, while the 3D selector allows users to choose residues and atoms directly from a structure.

Beginners can start with a prepared workflow, replace the inputs, and adjust the relevant parameters. Users retain control over each step, with workflow validation, run status, logs, issues, and outputs available for inspection. This helps beginners understand how the pipeline works rather than treating it as a “black box.”

To address the computing resource barrier, we also made FoundryUI available through a _Google Colab_ notebook, giving beginners a guided setup process in their browser and access to free computing resources for initial trials. We provided installation instructions and a preconfigured _AutoDL_ environment with the required software and dependencies already installed, further reducing the setup effort.

## Older Adults With Limited Formal Education

### Learn

When choosing a topic for our [debate](/education#ethical-debate) as part of our Education activities, we considered discussing the benefits and risks of genetically modified (GM) foods. The discussion led us to our own grandparents’ views on GM foods, and we surprisingly realized that almost all of them had been influenced by biased television advertisements, misleading online information, and other forms of publicity, leading them to assume that GM foods were inherently harmful.

That same day, we each went home and spoke with our grandparents about how they accessed and evaluated scientific information, both online and through promotional materials. From their responses, we learned that many people of their generation in China had fewer opportunities for formal education. Many also struggled to use digital devices effectively, limiting their access to scientific information online. Even when they could access such information, they often found it difficult to understand scientific terminology and complex explanations in online science articles. Combined with the mixed quality of information available online, these challenges made it difficult for them to distinguish reliable information from misleading claims. We therefore identified the barrier as **a lack of scientific information presented in ways that are familiar and accessible to people with limited formal education**.

## Accessible Wiki

Our efforts to make science more inclusive also shaped how we shared our own work. We therefore made accessibility a priority in our Wiki’s design, so that more people could use the educational materials, software, and research documentation available there.

We combined accessible UI components, keyboard and screen-reader support, automated testing, and manual validation to make our documentation usable by as many visitors as possible.

### Accessibility By Design

Our interface is built with _**Reka UI**_, an accessibility-focused component library that provides accessible interaction patterns, keyboard controls, focus management, and appropriate ARIA behaviour by default.

This foundation is used throughout several interactive components of our website:

:content-components-inclusivity-accessible-wiki-accessibility-playground

As a result, the majority of interactive parts of the website can be operated without a mouse. This particularly supports users who rely on keyboards, screen readers, or other assistive technologies to interact with web interfaces, such as users with motor impairments, blind users, and temporarily injured users.

### Accessibility Tools

The **accessibility tools menu** in the bottom-right corner provides some useful features.

**High-Contrast Mode:** This changes the color scheme to high contrast mode. This can be particularly helpful for users with low vision, reduced contrast sensitivity, or certain forms of color-vision impairment, who may have difficulty distinguishing elements when colors have similar brightness or contrast.

**_OpenDyslexic_ Font:** This changes the font of the website to OpenDyslexic, a font with letters having heavy weighted bottoms to indicate direction. This option is intended for users with dyslexia or other reading difficulties who personally find this style of typeface easier to follow.

**Back to Top:** This scrolls the page to the top. In mobile view, it can be combined with the "on this page" on the top to easily navigate within the page.

### Continuous Accessibility Testing

This allows us to identify and correct issues as pages and components are developed, rather than checking after the website is finished.

We additionally use **Google Lighthouse accessibility audits** and **axe-core full-site scanning** as an independent automated check, and our website passes both:

### WCAG 2.2 Compliance

We designed and reviewed our documentation pages against the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (_WCAG_) 2.2**, targeting **Level AA compliance**.

We supplemented the automatic checks with manual testing, including the following aspects as reported by Google Lighthouse:

- Interactive controls are keyboard focusable

- Interactive elements indicate their purpose and state

- The page has a logical tab order

- Visual order on the page follows DOM order

- User focus is not accidentally trapped in a region

- The user's focus is directed to new content added to the page

- HTML5 landmark elements are used to improve navigation

- Offscreen content is hidden from assistive technology

- Custom controls have associated labels

- Custom controls have ARIA roles

### Accessible Reading Experience

We present long-form content in an accessible way.

We make the website responsive, which means it can fit in different screens and the text size can be adjusted.

We investigated recommendations for readable text layouts and limited the width of line of text to fewer than approximately 80 characters per line. This reduces the distance the eye must travel when moving from the end of one line to the beginning of the next and makes longer passages easier to follow.

This design also aligns with the WCAG recommendation for text width at Level AAA.

## Inclusive Packaging

Guided by our commitment to inclusivity, we incorporated inclusive design principles into Expelliodor’s packaging to accommodate users with visual impairments or limited grip strength and dexterity, who may face difficulties locating, opening, or handling conventional deodorant packaging.

After analysing Degree Inclusive, we identified several accessible design features that could be adapted for Expelliodor, particularly its enhanced grip placement and Braille instructions. :fn-ref{#24} Building on this inspiration, we developed our own packaging design with additional features to address the diverse needs of our target users.

### Key Features

- **Enhanced Grip Placement**: Inspired by Degree Inclusive, the contoured sides of the bottle provide a more secure grip and facilitate single-handed handling.

- **Tactile Marker**: The word “Deodorant” is displayed in Braille to help users with visual impairments identify the product.

- **Rigid, Rounded Band**: Positioned near the bottle opening, this band functions as either a supporting handle or a hook, allowing the bottle to be hung while supporting its full weight for easier storage and location.

- **Press-to-Open Lid**: Designed to open with a single press, reducing the grip strength and hand dexterity needed to access the product and facilitating single-handed use.

- **Hinged Lid**: Permanently attached to the bottle body, the lid allows users to close the bottle with one hand without repositioning a separate cap, while reducing the risk of loss.

- **Tactile Opening Point:** A clearly defined opening point can be identified by touch, helping users with visual impairments locate where to open the bottle.

- **Raised Grip Texture**: Provides additional friction for a more secure grip, particularly for users with limited hand strength or dexterity.

- **Large Lettering**: Makes the product name easier to read for older adults and users with low vision.
